Economics / Analyse: build chains of reasoning from the data (6 marks)

Pearson Edexcel · International GCSE Economics · 4EC1

Analyse: build chains of reasoning from the data (6 marks)

Pearson's meaning: write an extended answer that expands and explores one economic idea or issue, set in the context of the question. In 4EC1 it always starts 'With reference to the data above and your knowledge of economics, analyse...'. There is no judgement to make: you explain how and why something happens, step by step, using the case.

Command word: AnalyseUsually 6 marksAbout 7 minutes

What Pearson asks

Pearson's meaning: write an extended answer that expands and explores one economic idea or issue, set in the context of the question. In 4EC1 it always starts 'With reference to the data above and your knowledge of economics, analyse...'. There is no judgement to make: you explain how and why something happens, step by step, using the case.

How the marks are earned

Six marks split equally: 3 for AO2 (using economic terms and theory correctly) and 3 for AO3 (picking out and using the data). The answer is marked as a whole on three levels: 1-2, 3-4 and 5-6. The top level needs clear, developed links between cause and effect, accurate terms, and the case data used to support the chain. Points that are listed but not developed stay in the lower levels.

Method

  1. Underline the cause and the result named in the question (for example: better quality, then the current account).

    Why: Your chain must join exactly these two things.

  2. Define the key term in one short sentence.

    Why: Correct use of terms is the AO2 half of the marks.

  3. Pick two pieces of evidence from the data (a number, a fact about the market or country).

    Why: Using the data is the AO3 half; without it you cannot reach the top level.

  4. Write chain 1: cause, then 'this means', then 'so', then the result. Use at least three linked steps.

    Why: Examiners reward developed links, not single statements.

  5. Write chain 2: a different route to the same result, again tied to the data.

    Why: Two developed chains give enough depth for Level 3.

  6. Finish each chain by saying clearly how it affects what the question asked about.

    Why: It shows the reasoning answers the question. Do not add a judgement: Analyse does not ask for one.

Answer frame

Key term means ... . In the data, ... (quote a fact or number). This means ..., so ..., which leads to ... (result). A second reason is ... . Because the data shows ..., ... happens, so ..., which also ... (result).

Common errors

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Listing several points with no 'this means / so' links, which keeps the answer in Level 1 or 2.
  • Writing a general textbook answer that never uses the case or its numbers (loses AO3).
  • Explaining the cause but never reaching the result the question asks about.
  • Adding 'however' points and a conclusion, which wastes time because Analyse does not reward evaluation.
  • Using economic terms loosely, for example mixing up the current account and the government budget.

Worked illustration

November 2025 · Paper 2 · Q4(b)

The complete original question is shown; the illustration answers part (b).

Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The current account records money coming into and going out of a country, mainly from trade in goods and services. Exports bring money into the UK and imports send money out. The data says France has a high-income population and a well-known luxury sector, so French buyers can afford good products and expect high standards. If UK firms raise the quality of their goods, those goods become more attractive than rival products in this very competitive market. This means more French consumers will choose UK goods, so the volume of UK exports to France rises. Since UK exports to France were already worth £45bn in 2023, even a small percentage rise brings in a large extra sum. A second route is price. Better quality lets UK firms charge higher prices, and rich consumers who value quality are less likely to stop buying when the price goes up. So each export sale earns more money. Both routes raise UK export revenue. If export earnings grow faster than spending on imports, the UK's balance of trade in goods improves, so any current account deficit gets smaller or a surplus gets bigger. That is the positive impact on the current account.

Why it earns the marks

Level 3 (5-6). AO2: the current account and exports are defined and used correctly, and two developed chains (more export volume; higher export prices) reach the result. AO3: the answer uses the high-income French market, its luxury sector, the competitive market and the £45bn figure.
