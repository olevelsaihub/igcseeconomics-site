Formulas and key facts

Short, must-learn points for Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1). No formula sheet is printed in Economics papers, so learn every formula here. Items are marked Paper 1, Paper 2 or Both papers.

FormulasElasticity valuesDiagram rulesDefinitionsCommand words

Formulas

Markets and elasticity

Excess demand

Paper 1

excess demand = quantity demanded − quantity supplied

Excess supply = quantity supplied − quantity demanded. Answer in units of the good, not money.

Price elasticity of demand (PED)

Paper 1

PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

Usually negative. Rearranged: % change in quantity demanded = PED × % change in price.

Price elasticity of supply (PES)

Paper 1

PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price

Positive: price and quantity supplied move the same way.

Income elasticity of demand (YED)

Paper 1

YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income

Above 1 luxury; 0 to 1 necessity; below 0 inferior.

Opportunity cost on a PPC

Paper 1

opportunity cost = amount of the other good given up

Old amount − new amount of the other good, in units of that good.

Firms: revenue, costs and profit

Total revenue

Paper 1

total revenue = price × quantity sold

Rearranged: quantity = total revenue ÷ price.

Total cost

Paper 1

total cost = total fixed cost + total variable cost

Total variable cost = variable cost per unit × number of units.

Average cost

Paper 1

average cost = total cost ÷ quantity produced

Money per unit, usually to 2 decimal places.

Profit

Paper 1

profit = total revenue − total cost

A negative answer is a loss. Per unit: profit = price − cost per unit.

Labour productivity

Paper 1

labour productivity = total output ÷ number of workers

Units of output per worker, not money.

Wage income

Paper 1

gross pay = hourly wage × hours worked

Give the currency.

Percentages and data

Percentage change

Both papers

percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100

Always divide by the old value. A fall gives a negative answer.

Percentage share

Both papers

percentage share = part ÷ whole × 100

Pick the right whole before dividing.

Applying a percentage

Both papers

amount = rate ÷ 100 × total

Used for interest, tax and tariffs.

Mean (average)

Both papers

mean = sum of the values ÷ number of values

Keep the units of the data.

The economy and government

Inflation from a price index

Paper 2

inflation rate = (new index − old index) ÷ old index × 100

From a base of 100: inflation since the base year = index − 100.

Scaling a price by an index

Paper 2

new price = old price × new index ÷ old index

Unemployment rate

Paper 2

unemployment rate = number unemployed ÷ labour force × 100

Divide by the labour force, not the population. Unemployed = labour force − employed.

GDP per capita

Paper 2

GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population

Put both in the same units first (1 billion = 1,000 million).

Fiscal (budget) balance

Paper 2

fiscal balance = government revenue − government spending

Negative = budget deficit; positive = budget surplus.

Tax revenue by group

Paper 2

total = sum of the taxes in that group only

Direct taxes: income and profits. Indirect taxes: spending.

Trade and exchange rates

Trade in goods (visible) balance

Paper 2

visible balance = exports of goods − imports of goods

Invisible balance = exports of services − imports of services.

Current account balance (trade figures)

Paper 2

balance = visible balance + invisible balance

Negative = deficit; positive = surplus.

Converting currencies

Paper 2

if £1 = €1.18: pounds × 1.18 = euros; euros ÷ 1.18 = pounds

Check the answer: the currency worth less per unit gives the bigger number.

Price with a tariff

Paper 2

price with tariff = import price + tariff

A percentage tariff: tariff = rate ÷ 100 × import price.

Calculation habits that earn the marks

  • Show every step: the method mark is usually given for the correct working even if the final answer is wrong.
  • Round only at the end, to the decimal places the question asks for.
  • Give the unit: %, $, €, £ or units of the good. A missing unit or % sign can cost a mark.
  • Keep the minus sign for a fall, a loss, a deficit and a normal PED, unless the question asks for the size of the change.
  • Put figures in the same units before dividing (millions with millions, $bn with $bn).

Diagram rules

  • Which curve moves? A change that affects buyers (income, tastes, population, price of a substitute or complement, advertising) moves demand. A change that affects producers (costs, technology, weather, taxes, subsidies, number of firms) moves supply.
  • Which way? More demanded or supplied at every price: shift right. Less: shift left. Draw the new curve parallel to the old one.
  • Labels Label the axes Price and Quantity, the curves D and S, the new curve D1 or S1, the old equilibrium P and Q and the new one P1 and Q1, with dashed lines to both axes.
  • Price change only A change in the good's own price is a movement along the curve, not a shift.
  • Indirect tax and subsidy A tax shifts supply left (price up, quantity down). A subsidy shifts supply right (price down, quantity up).
  • Tariff A tariff raises the price of imports: the domestic price rises, imports fall and domestic producers supply more.
  • Maximum and minimum prices A maximum price is set below equilibrium and causes excess demand. A minimum price (such as a minimum wage) is set above equilibrium and causes excess supply.
  • Production possibility curve A point inside the curve shows unused resources; moving along it shows opportunity cost; an outward shift shows economic growth.

Definitions to learn

The market system Paper 1

  • Economic problem: unlimited wants but scarce resources, so choices must be made.
  • Factors of production: land, labour, capital and enterprise. Their rewards are rent, wages, interest and profit.
  • Opportunity cost: the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
  • Demand: the quantity of a good buyers are willing and able to buy at each price.
  • Supply: the quantity of a good sellers are willing and able to sell at each price.
  • Equilibrium: the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears.
  • Substitutes and complements: substitutes are used instead of each other; complements are used together.
  • Public sector, private sector: public: owned and run by the government. Private: owned by individuals and firms.
  • Market failure: when the free market gives a poor use of resources, such as too much pollution or too few merit goods.
  • Externalities: costs or benefits to a third party who is not part of the deal. Social cost = private cost + external cost.
  • Public good: non-excludable and non-rival, so the free market will not provide it (street lighting).
  • Merit and demerit goods: merit goods are under-consumed (education); demerit goods are over-consumed (cigarettes).

Business economics Paper 1

  • Division of labour: splitting production into separate tasks, each done by a specialist worker.
  • Fixed and variable costs: fixed costs do not change with output; variable costs rise as output rises.
  • Economies of scale: average cost falls as a firm grows. Internal: from the firm itself. External: from the whole industry growing.
  • Diseconomies of scale: average cost rises when a firm grows too large (poor communication, low motivation).
  • Competitive market: many firms, similar products, easy entry: low prices and choice for consumers.
  • Monopoly and oligopoly: monopoly: one firm dominates the market. Oligopoly: a few large firms dominate.
  • Labour market: wages are set by the demand for and supply of labour.
  • Production and productivity: production is making goods and services; productivity is output per worker.

Government and the economy Paper 2

  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP: more goods and services produced.
  • GDP: the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year.
  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level, measured by the CPI.
  • Deflation: a fall in the general price level.
  • Demand-pull and cost-push inflation: demand-pull: total demand grows faster than output. Cost-push: firms' costs rise and are passed on.
  • Unemployment: people of working age who are willing and able to work but have no job.
  • Types of unemployment: frictional (between jobs), structural (industry decline), cyclical (recession), seasonal (time of year).
  • Fiscal policy: changing government spending and taxation to affect the economy.
  • Monetary policy: changing interest rates (and the money supply) to affect the economy; set by the central bank.
  • Supply-side policies: policies that raise the economy's capacity to produce: education, training, infrastructure, deregulation.
  • Direct and indirect tax: direct: on income and profits. Indirect: on spending, such as VAT.
  • Progressive, regressive, proportional tax: progressive takes a larger share of higher incomes; regressive a larger share of lower incomes; proportional the same share.
  • Absolute and relative poverty: absolute: not enough for basic needs. Relative: well below the average income in that country.
  • Recession: negative economic growth for two quarters in a row.

The global economy Paper 2

  • Globalisation: the growing links between countries' economies through trade, investment and people.
  • Multinational company (MNC): a firm with its headquarters in one country and production in other countries.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a firm from one country investing in businesses or assets in another country.
  • Free trade: trade between countries with no barriers such as tariffs or quotas.
  • Protectionism: shielding domestic firms from foreign competition with tariffs, quotas or subsidies.
  • Tariff and quota: a tariff is a tax on imports; a quota is a limit on the quantity imported.
  • Trading bloc: a group of countries that remove trade barriers between themselves (free trade area, customs union, common market).
  • Current account: records trade in goods and services, plus income and transfer flows, with other countries.
  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another.
  • Appreciation and depreciation: a rise (appreciation) or fall (depreciation) in a floating currency's value. "SPICED": a Strong Pound makes Imports Cheaper and Exports Dearer.
  • Fixed and floating exchange rates: floating: set by demand and supply. Fixed: held at a set level by the government or central bank.

Command words and marks

Command word (usual marks)What earns the marks
Define / What is meant by (2)A precise meaning in one or two sentences. Use an example if it helps.
