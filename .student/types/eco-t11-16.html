Knowledge card

Mixed economy and the public and private sectors

Written
  • The public sector is the part of the economy owned and controlled by the government. It provides goods and services such as roads, schools and hospitals, aims to serve the public interest, and is funded mainly by taxes.
  • The private sector is made up of firms owned by individuals or groups. They are controlled by their owners and usually aim to make a profit.
  • A mixed economy is one where the public and private sectors both provide goods and services.
  • In a mixed economy, what, how and for whom to produce is decided partly by market forces (prices, profit) and partly by the government.
  • Public provision can give everyone access, create external benefits and jobs, and reduce social costs, but it has an opportunity cost and may be less efficient without a profit motive.
  • The size of each sector differs between countries; in some, the state employs most workers.

Before you begin

  • Scarcity, opportunity cost and the basic economic problem (ECO-T11-02).
  • Profit as a business aim (ECO-T11-06).
  • External benefits (ECO-T11-20).
1

The method

  1. Define the sector or sectors involved.

    Why: Definitions earn the first marks and frame the answer.

  2. Contrast ownership (government v private), control and aims (public interest v profit) where relevant.

    Why: These three differences are named in the specification.

  3. Explain what the public sector provides in the case and why: access for all, external benefits, jobs, lower future costs.

    Why: This builds the benefit chains using the data.

  4. Weigh the costs: opportunity cost of tax money, funding limits, efficiency, crowding.

    Why: Counter-arguments earn AO4 marks.

  5. For 9- and 12-mark items, follow the Assess or Evaluate card and finish with a judgement that says what the outcome depends on.

    Why: The top level needs a supported conclusion.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining a mixed economy as 'a mix of goods' rather than both sectors providing goods and services.
  • ✗
    Saying the public sector aims to make a profit.
  • ✗
    Giving only benefits in Assess and Evaluate answers, with no opportunity cost.
  • ✗
    Not using the figures or details given about the country or project.
  • ✗
    Confusing the public sector with public limited companies, which are private.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Define the public sector and its aim.
  2. Benefit chain: free access, exercise, health, lower healthcare spending, more output.
  3. Explain why private firms would under-provide parks.
  4. Counter-arguments: funding limits, opportunity cost, crowding.
  5. Judge, with a 'depends on' condition.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The public sector is the part of the economy owned and run by the government, which aims to serve the public interest rather than make a profit. Providing outdoor areas and parks is a good example of what it can do. A public park is free to enter, so everyone can use it, including people on low incomes who could not pay to join a gym. The data says access to such areas encourages physical exercise and benefits mental wellbeing. If more people exercise, fewer become obese or suffer from stress, so fewer need medical treatment. This lowers the government's healthcare spending and keeps more people fit enough to work, which raises output for the whole economy. These gains also reach third parties, so the benefit to society is larger than the benefit to each visitor. A private firm would find it hard to charge for an open park and still make a profit, so without the public sector too few parks might exist.

However, there are costs. Parks must be paid for from tax revenue, and the data notes that funding is not always available. Money spent on creating and maintaining parks cannot also be spent on schools or hospitals, so there is an opportunity cost, and land used for a park could have been used for housing. Popular parks may also become too crowded, which means fewer people can benefit and the health gains shrink.

Overall, the public sector providing parks is likely to bring real benefits, because the health gains spread across the community and reduce future government spending on healthcare. How large the benefit is depends on whether the government can fund enough parks, and maintain them, so that they do not become overcrowded; where money is short, working with private sponsors could help.

How the marks are earned

AO2/AO3 (up to 6): public sector defined, health and cost-saving chains developed from the data, and the reason private firms would under-provide. AO4 (up to 3): funding limits, opportunity cost and crowding weighed, then a conditional judgement.

Assess · November 2020 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define the public sector and public sector investment.
  2. Data: more than half of Cambodia's 6,000 km of roads are of poor quality; overloaded trucks cause safety issues and damage; business is growing.
  3. Chain: repaired roads -> cheaper, faster transport -> lower firm costs -> more output and growth -> more tax revenue.
  4. Chain: safer roads -> fewer accidents -> more people able to work; new roads need less maintenance -> future savings.
  5. Against: opportunity cost (health, education); many roads so slow benefits; overloading may keep damaging roads; growth may outpace building.
  6. Judgement: large benefit because poor roads limit growth; depends on enforcing truck load limits and funding without cutting key services.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The public sector is the part of the economy owned and run by the government in the public interest. Public sector investment is government spending on capital, such as roads.

More than half of Cambodia's 6,000 km of roads are 'of poor quality'. Government investment to repair them would bring several benefits. First, firms could move goods more quickly and cheaply. Lower transport costs reduce firms' costs of production, so they can cut prices, sell more and expand, which raises output and economic growth. Second, better roads would mean fewer accidents caused by overloaded trucks, so fewer workers are injured and more people are able to work. Third, building new roads that need less maintenance saves the government money on repairs in the future.

These benefits are positive externalities: firms and households gain from good roads without paying for them directly, so the private sector would not provide enough roads on its own. As businesses grow, they earn more profit and employ more people, so the government collects more tax revenue, which helps pay back the investment. The construction work also creates jobs and incomes in the short run.

However, the benefits may be limited. High public investment has an opportunity cost: money spent on roads cannot be spent on health care or education, which might give larger long-term benefits. With so many poor roads, the work will take many years, so the gains may come slowly. If drivers keep overloading their trucks, even new roads may be damaged quickly and the repair costs return. If business keeps growing, the government may not be able to build roads fast enough to keep up.

Overall, high public investment in roads is likely to benefit Cambodia to a large extent, because poor roads are holding back business growth that is already happening. The size of the benefit depends on whether the government also enforces limits on truck loads, and whether the spending can be funded without cutting key services such as health and education.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines the public sector and applies the 6,000 km of poor roads and overloading to developed chains on costs, safety, externalities and tax revenue; balances with opportunity cost, time lag, renewed damage and growth outpacing roads; reaches a judgement that depends on enforcing load limits and funding.

Evaluate · November 2025 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define the public sector and public sector investment in infrastructure.
  2. Data: AU$107m to expand the Swan River ferry network; five new electric ferries built by local firms; over 130 passengers per trip; 72 km river; new stops near a bus depot and the university.
  3. Short-run chain: local firms build ferries -> jobs and incomes -> multiplier -> more tax revenue.
  4. Long-run chain: more capacity and links to buses and the university -> better labour mobility and access to education -> fewer car journeys and less congestion.
  5. Against: opportunity cost of AU$107m; time lag; sailing clubs' safety concerns; parking problems; low usage risk.
  6. Judgement: fairly large benefit; depends on passenger demand and how well safety and parking are managed.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The public sector is the part of the economy owned and run by the government. Public sector investment is government spending on capital goods, such as transport, which the private sector may not provide enough of because many of the benefits go to people who do not pay for them.

The Western Australian Government plans to invest AU$107m to expand the Swan River ferry network. In the short run, the five new electric ferries will be built by local firms. These firms will need more workers and materials, so incomes rise; workers spend their incomes in Perth, which creates further jobs through the multiplier effect. Some of the spending returns to the government as higher tax revenue.

In the long run, the ferries raise transport capacity. Each ferry can carry more than 130 passengers per trip, on a 72 km waterway that connects many areas of Perth. People can travel to work in more places, which improves the geographical mobility of labour, so firms find the workers they need and unemployment can fall. The new stop near the bus depot links ferries with buses, so fewer people need private cars, which reduces congestion and pollution, negative externalities that impose costs on others. The stop near the University of Western Australia makes it easier for students to reach education, which raises the skills of the future workforce.

However, there are costs. The AU$107m has an opportunity cost: it could have been spent on health care, schools or other projects that might give higher returns. Building ferries and new stops takes time, so benefits may not appear for several years while the costs are paid now. Local sailing clubs are worried about safety for smaller boats; if accidents happen there are external costs and the government may need extra spending on safety. Politicians warn that extra parking for ferry users could cause problems such as congestion near the stops, which would cancel some of the benefit of fewer cars. If passenger numbers are low, the ferries will run with empty seats and the investment will be poor value.

Overall, the investment is likely to benefit Western Australia to a fairly large extent, through work for local firms now and better transport links later. The extent depends on how many people actually use the ferries, helped by the links to buses and the university, and on whether the government manages the safety and parking problems well. If demand is strong and these problems are controlled, the benefits should outweigh the cost.

Why it reaches the top level: Accurate public-sector and externality knowledge applied to AU$107m, five local ferries, 130 passengers and 72 km in short-run multiplier and long-run mobility chains; evaluates opportunity cost, time lag, safety and parking concerns and low usage; judgement depends on passenger demand and managing safety and parking.

Assess · November 2020 · Paper 1 · Q2(h)

  1. Define the economic problem (scarce resources, unlimited wants: what, how and for whom to produce), the public sector and the private sector.
  2. Data: Cuba relies mostly on public sector ownership; Japan relies more on the private sector but provides health, education and street lighting publicly and produces many electronic goods.
  3. Chain for private sector: profit motive -> firms keep costs low and follow consumer demand through prices -> fewer wasted resources, e.g. Japan's electronic goods.
  4. Chain for public sector: aims at public interest -> provides goods the market under-provides, such as street lighting, health and education.
  5. Against each: private sector ignores those who cannot pay and public goods; public sector may be inefficient without a profit incentive.
  6. Judgement: a mixed approach like Japan's is most effective; depends on the type of good - private for consumer goods, public for merit and public goods.

Assess · June 2023 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q2(h)

  1. Define the public sector and its aim of raising social benefits and cutting social costs.
  2. Data: parks help reduce health issues such as obesity, encourage exercise and benefit mental wellbeing; but funding is not always available and parks can become too crowded.
  3. Chain: free public parks open to everyone -> more exercise -> better physical and mental health -> less demand for healthcare -> lower public spending and a healthier workforce.
  4. Chain: positive externalities mean the private sector would under-provide parks if it had to charge.
  5. Against: cost of building and maintaining parks; opportunity cost of land (housing) and of government money; crowding reduces the benefit.
  6. Judgement: benefits are significant if parks are well funded and not overcrowded; depends on available funding and size of parks relative to users.
