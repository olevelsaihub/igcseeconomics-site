Knowledge card

Causes and effects of current account deficits and surpluses

Written
  • A current account deficit means imports are worth more than exports; a surplus means exports are worth more than imports.
  • Quality: better domestic goods raise demand for exports and reduce demand for imports, so the current account improves (a smaller deficit or bigger surplus). Better foreign goods do the opposite.
  • Price: cheaper domestic goods or dearer imports improve the current account; dearer domestic goods or cheaper imports (for example after a tariff cut) worsen it.
  • Exchange rate: a depreciation (the currency falling in value) makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, which tends to improve the current account.
  • Impacts of a deficit: a leakage (money spent abroad instead of at home), so lower demand for domestic firms, less output and more unemployment; it must be paid for using foreign currency reserves or borrowing.
  • Possible upsides of a deficit: consumers get a wider range of cheaper or better imports, and cheap imports can keep inflation down.
  • Benefits of a surplus: strong export sales create jobs and raise GDP and living standards, and the country builds foreign currency it can invest abroad.

The method

  1. Work out whether the question is about a cause (why it happened) or an impact (what it does).

    Why: Causes start from quality, price or exchange rate; impacts start from the deficit or surplus itself.

  2. For a cause, name the factor and say whether it changes demand for exports, imports, or both.

    Why: The development mark is the link to export or import demand.

  3. Finish the cause chain with the direction of the balance: more money in (improves) or more money out (worsens).

    Why: Credit needs the final link to the current account, not just to exports or imports.

  4. For an impact, chain from the balance to the domestic economy: demand for local goods, output, jobs, reserves, living standards.

    Why: Analyse and Assess marks reward developed consequences.

  5. Apply each chain to the named country and its figures.

    Why: Context earns a mark in Explain and AO3 marks in longer answers.

  6. For a 9-mark Assess, follow the Assess card: weigh upsides (cheaper imports, lower inflation, a later fall in the exchange rate) and judge on size and how long it lasts.

    Why: AO4 needs balance and a supported conclusion.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Stopping at 'exports rise' without saying what this does to the current account.
  • ✗
    Getting the direction wrong for foreign quality: better foreign goods raise imports and worsen the domestic current account.
  • ✗
    Explaining an impact with no chain to jobs, output or reserves.
  • ✗
    In Assess answers, treating every deficit as a disaster without weighing cheaper imports or the size and duration of the deficit.
  • ✗
    Leaving out the country's figures (for example the deficit as a share of GDP).

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the data: Indonesia's deficit was expected to reach 3% of GDP by end-2018; the minister said this was manageable as 2014 was worse; an economist warned it was growing and serious.
  2. Define a current account deficit.
  3. Cost 1: leakage: spending on imports replaces spending on Indonesian goods, so output falls and unemployment rises.
  4. Cost 2: the deficit must be financed with foreign reserves or borrowing, which cannot go on for ever.
  5. Upside: cheaper or better imports raise living standards and keep inflation down; a later fall in the exchange rate can make exports more competitive.
  6. Judge: impact depends on size and duration; 3% is moderate, but a growing deficit becomes more serious.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A country has a current account deficit when it spends more on imports than it earns from exports. Indonesia's deficit was expected to reach 3% of GDP by the end of 2018. One impact is a leakage of spending out of the economy. When Indonesian households and firms buy imported goods instead of goods made at home, Indonesian firms receive less demand. They may cut output and employ fewer workers, so unemployment can rise and GDP can grow more slowly. A deficit can also suggest that Indonesian goods are not selling well abroad, so export industries may lose jobs too.

A second impact is the problem of paying for the deficit. If Indonesia spends more on imports than it earns from exports, it must make up the difference by using its reserves of foreign currency or by borrowing from abroad. Reserves can run out and debts must be repaid with interest, so a deficit that keeps growing, as the economist Faisal Basri warned, could become a serious problem.

However, a deficit is not always harmful. It may mean that Indonesian consumers are able to buy a wider range of cheaper or better-quality imported goods, which raises their living standards. Cheap imports can also keep prices down and reduce inflation. In addition, over time a deficit may cause Indonesia's exchange rate to fall, which makes its exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer, helping to correct the deficit naturally.

Overall, the impact depends on the size of the deficit and how long it lasts. At 3% of GDP, and lower than in 2014, the minister may be right that it is not yet a crisis, and the benefits of cheaper imports partly offset the costs. But if the deficit keeps growing year after year, the loss of output and jobs and the difficulty of financing it would make the impact on Indonesia increasingly negative.

How the marks are earned

Levels marked out of 9. AO2 for the definition and the leakage, jobs and financing chains; AO3 for using the 3% of GDP figure, the comparison with 2014 and the two opposing views; AO4 for cheaper imports, lower inflation and exchange-rate adjustment, ending with a size-and-duration judgement.

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Assess · November 2020 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define current account deficit (imports and outflows greater than exports and inflows); data: expected 3% of GDP by end of 2018.
  2. Negative: imports are a leakage -> less demand for Indonesian firms -> lower output, jobs and growth.
  3. Negative: deficit must be financed by borrowing or reserves -> debt, loss of confidence, currency fall, cost-push inflation (Faisal Basri's warning).
  4. Positive: cheaper, wider choice of imports; capital goods imports raise future capacity; lower exchange rate can correct it; the 2014 deficit was even higher.
  5. Judgement: manageable at 3% of GDP; depends on its size, duration, type of imports and financing.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The current account records a country's trade in goods and services with the rest of the world, plus income flows and transfers. A current account deficit means more money flows out, mainly for imports, than flows in, mainly from exports. Indonesia's deficit was expected to reach 3% of GDP by the end of 2018.

A deficit can harm the economy. Spending on imports is a leakage from the circular flow: money spent on foreign goods does not create income for Indonesian firms. If Indonesians buy imports instead of domestic goods, or if Indonesian exports are weak, domestic firms sell less, cut output and may cut jobs, so GDP grows more slowly and unemployment rises. The deficit also has to be financed, by borrowing from abroad, selling assets to foreigners or using foreign currency reserves. If the deficit keeps growing, as the economist Faisal Basri warns, foreign debt builds up and investors may lose confidence. This could cause the currency to fall sharply, making imports such as fuel and raw materials more expensive and causing cost-push inflation.

However, the impact may not be serious. A deficit of 3% of GDP is moderate, and the minister points out that the deficit in 2014 was even higher, so Indonesia has managed similar deficits before. Imports give consumers a wider range of goods at lower prices, which raises living standards and reduces inflationary pressure. If the imports are capital goods, such as machinery for factories and infrastructure, they increase Indonesia's productive capacity and can raise future exports. A deficit can also correct itself: higher demand for foreign currency may lower the exchange rate, making Indonesian exports cheaper and imports dearer, which reduces the deficit.

Overall, a deficit of 3% of GDP is unlikely to be a major problem in the short term, which supports the minister's view. However, Faisal Basri is right that it should be watched. The impact depends on how long the deficit lasts, whether it keeps growing, what is being imported and how easily it can be financed.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines the current account and a deficit, applies the 3% of GDP figure and the two opposing views in the data, develops chains (leakage, jobs, financing, currency, inflation) and counter-chains (cheaper imports, capital goods, exchange-rate correction), and judges with clear conditions.
