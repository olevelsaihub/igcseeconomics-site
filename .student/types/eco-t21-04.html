Worked explanation

  1. Read the data: Germany's GDP fell 0.4% in a quarter; inflation was a record 10.4%; consumer spending and business investment fell; there was an energy crisis with high gas and oil prices.
  2. Define recession: GDP falls for two quarters in a row.
  3. Chain 1 (demand side): spending falls, firms sell less, they cut prices or stop raising them, so inflation slows.
  4. Chain 2 (cost side): weak demand for labour and materials lowers wages and input prices, so firms' costs and prices rise more slowly.
  5. Limit 1: Germany's inflation is partly cost-push from energy; a recession does little to cut world gas and oil prices.
  6. Limit 2: if the central bank keeps interest rates low to fight the recession, spending is supported and inflation may stay high.
  7. Judge: a recession is likely to reduce demand-pull inflation, but how far inflation falls depends on how much of it comes from energy costs.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A recession is when a country's GDP falls for two quarters in a row. Germany's GDP fell by 0.4% in one quarter, so it may be entering a recession. In a recession households worry about their jobs and spend less, and firms delay investment; the data shows German consumer spending and investment in buildings and machinery both falling. With lower total demand, shops and manufacturers have unsold stock, so they are less able to raise prices and may offer discounts to attract customers. This reduces demand-pull inflation, so Germany's record rate of 10.4% could start to fall.

A recession can also lower firms' costs. As firms cut output, they need fewer workers and buy fewer materials. With higher unemployment, workers are in a weaker position to win large pay rises, and suppliers of raw materials face weaker orders, so input prices rise more slowly. Because firms' costs grow more slowly, they do not need to raise their own prices as much, which again helps to bring inflation down.

However, the effect depends on what is causing Germany's inflation. Much of it comes from the energy crisis, with high gas and oil prices. This is cost-push inflation: firms pass higher fuel and power costs on to customers. A fall in German spending does little to reduce world energy prices, so prices could keep rising even while output falls. In addition, during a recession the central bank may keep interest rates low to encourage borrowing and spending. This supports demand and may stop inflation from falling quickly, even though growth is weak.

Overall, a recession is likely to reduce inflation in Germany to some extent, because weaker demand and slower wage growth limit firms' ability to raise prices. But because a large part of Germany's 10.4% inflation is caused by energy costs rather than high demand, the recession on its own may not bring inflation down much. The final effect depends mainly on whether gas and oil prices fall and on how the central bank sets interest rates.

How the marks are earned

Levels marked out of 9. AO2 for the recession definition and the demand and cost chains; AO3 for using Germany's 0.4% fall, 10.4% inflation, and falling spending and investment; AO4 for the cost-push energy limit and interest-rate point, ending in a judgement on what the effect depends on, reaching Level 3.

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Assess · June 2024 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define recovery phase; data: growth only 3% in 2022; exports up 14.8% in 2023; services and construction at a 12-year high by March 2023; 5% target.
  2. For: higher demand -> firms need more workers (derived demand) -> unemployment falls, especially in services and construction.
  3. For: more incomes -> more spending -> multiplier and confidence -> more jobs and new firms.
  4. Against: hiring lags output; export recovery expected to be temporary due to interest rate rises abroad; automation; skills mismatch between sectors.
  5. Judgement: unemployment likely falls; depends on how long the recovery lasts and whether firms hire rather than automate.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The economic cycle is the pattern of rises and falls in real GDP over time. A recovery is the phase after a recession or slowdown, when output starts to grow again. China grew by only 3% in 2022, but in 2023 exports rose by 14.8% and by March 2023 the services and construction sectors had reached a 12-year high.

During a recovery, demand for goods and services rises. Labour is a derived demand, so when firms need to produce more they hire more workers, and unemployment falls. This is especially likely in services and construction, which use a lot of labour and are growing fastest. The 14.8% rise in exports means foreign demand is also creating jobs in factories and transport. As more people earn wages, their spending rises, which creates further demand and more jobs through the multiplier effect. Consumer and business confidence improves, so firms invest and new businesses start up, creating even more jobs. If China reaches its growth target of 5%, cyclical unemployment should fall noticeably.

However, the fall in unemployment may be limited. Firms often wait to be sure a recovery will last before hiring, so employment usually lags behind output; at first they may ask existing staff to work longer hours. The data warns that the export recovery is expected to be temporary, because interest rate rises in many economies may weaken global demand. If exports fall back, export firms will not need extra workers and may cut jobs. Some firms may invest in machines and automation instead of workers, so output rises without many new jobs. The recovery may also be uneven: unemployed workers from sectors that are not recovering may lack the skills for construction or service jobs, leaving structural unemployment.

Overall, a recovery is likely to reduce unemployment in China, mainly through more jobs in services and construction. However, the size of the fall depends on how long the recovery lasts, especially whether global demand for Chinese exports stays strong, and on whether firms hire workers rather than invest in automation.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines the cycle and recovery, uses the 3% growth, 14.8% export rise, 12-year high and 5% target in chains (derived demand, multiplier, confidence), gives a balanced counter-case (lagged hiring, temporary exports, automation, structural unemployment) and a conditional judgement.

Assess · June 2024 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

  1. Define recession (two consecutive quarters of negative growth); data: GDP fell 0.4% in Q4 2022; inflation at a record 10.4%.
  2. For lower inflation: falling consumer spending and business investment -> lower aggregate demand -> less demand-pull pressure -> slower price rises.
  3. For: weaker demand for labour and materials -> lower wage growth and input prices -> lower costs and prices.
  4. Against: inflation is largely cost-push from high gas and oil prices, which a recession does not remove; low interest rates to fight the recession could add to inflation.
  5. Judgement: recession likely slows inflation somewhat; depends on whether inflation is demand-pull or cost-push and on energy prices and policy.
