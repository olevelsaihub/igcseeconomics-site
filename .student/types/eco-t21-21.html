Reducing poverty: progressive tax, benefits, education, healthcare

Written

Poverty can be cut by a progressive tax (a higher % from higher incomes), benefits that raise low incomes, and education and healthcare that help people earn more.

Written-answer check

After your attempt, see the full worked explanation

Worked explanation

  1. Read the data: India offers cash transfers, food rations and training courses to very low-income people.
  2. Define benefits briefly.
  3. Chain 1: cash transfers raise income so essentials can be bought, cutting absolute poverty.
  4. Chain 2: food rations protect health and free income for other needs, helping people work.
  5. Chain 3: training leads to jobs and lasting income, lifting households out of poverty.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Benefits are payments or other support that the government gives to people on low incomes, funded by tax revenue. They reduce poverty mainly by raising the real income of the poorest households directly.

First, the cash transfers in India add to the income of very low-income families. With this extra money they can buy essentials such as food, shelter and medicine that they previously went without, so fewer households fall below the level needed to meet basic needs. This reduces absolute poverty.

Second, food rations target the most basic need of all. Families who receive rations spend less of their small income on food, which frees money for other necessities, and better nutrition improves their health. Healthier people can work more days and earn more, so the benefit of the ration lasts beyond the food itself.

Third, the training courses for unemployed workers tackle a cause of poverty rather than only its symptoms. Workers who gain new skills are more likely to find a job, so they earn a regular wage instead of depending on support. This raises their income permanently and can lift the whole household out of poverty, which also narrows the gap between rich and poor in India.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked, 3 AO2 + 3 AO3. A clear meaning of benefits and developed chains to lower poverty give AO2; using all three Indian measures (cash transfers, rations, training) and finishing each chain in India's context gives the excellent interpretation needed for Level 3 (5-6).

Assess · November 2020 · Paper 2 · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define benefit payments and redistribution of income.
  2. Data: CCB is tax-free, raised in 2018 to match the cost of living, falls as income rises; over CAD$23.3bn to about 3.7 million families; over 300,000 children lifted out of poverty.
  3. Chain: means-tested payments target poorest families -> higher disposable income -> less relative poverty and inequality -> healthier, better-educated children earn more later.
  4. Against: poverty trap and disincentive to work as benefit is withdrawn; opportunity cost and tax burden; resentment from those not entitled.
  5. Judgement: strong advantages because it is targeted and proven; depends on how steeply it is withdrawn; best combined with progressive tax and education.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Benefit payments are money transfers from the government to people so that they can reach a minimum standard of living. Redistribution of income means moving income from richer to poorer households, usually by taxing higher earners and paying benefits to lower earners.

The Canada Child Benefit has clear advantages. It is means-tested: as a family's income rises the payment falls, and some higher income households get nothing. This targets the money at the poorest families, so each dollar does the most to reduce inequality and relative poverty. The scheme has distributed more than CAD$23.3bn to around 3.7 million families and helped lift more than 300,000 children out of poverty, so it clearly works on a large scale. Because the benefit is tax-free and was raised in 2018 to match the cost of living, its real value is protected from inflation, so poor families can still afford food, clothing and housing. Children who grow up out of poverty are likely to be healthier and do better at school, so they earn more as adults; this reduces inequality in the future, not just today. Extra income may also help parents pay for childcare and return to work.

However, there are disadvantages. Because the benefit falls as income rises, some parents may face a poverty trap: earning more means losing benefit, so the gain from working extra hours is small, which can discourage work. The CAD$23.3bn has an opportunity cost, as it could have funded schools or hospitals, and it must be paid for through taxes. Families without children get no help, and higher earners who pay tax but receive nothing may resent the scheme.

Overall, benefit payments such as the CCB have strong advantages for redistributing income in Canada, because they are targeted and have reduced child poverty quickly. Their success depends on how sharply the benefit is withdrawn as income rises, since a gentle withdrawal limits the poverty trap. They work best alongside progressive taxes and investment in education.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines benefits and redistribution and applies the means test, CAD$23.3bn, 3.7 million families, 300,000 children and the 2018 cost-of-living rise in developed chains; balances with poverty trap, opportunity cost and resentment; judges strong advantages depending on the withdrawal rate, alongside other policies.

Evaluate · June 2019 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define inequality, absolute poverty and progressive taxation.
  2. Data: 210 super-rich people in Vietnam earn enough in one year to lift 3.2 million out of poverty; Oxfam calls for higher taxes to fund healthcare, education and jobs, plus a living wage and fair tax by businesses.
  3. Chain: higher tax rates on top earners -> narrower after-tax gap and more revenue -> spending on healthcare and education -> poor families more productive and better paid.
  4. Against: disincentive to work and invest; capital flight and tax avoidance; unpopular; tax alone does not help the poor unless spent well.
  5. Alternatives: living wage, benefit payments, job creation may act faster and more directly.
  6. Judgement: important but not most effective alone; depends on tax collection and how revenue is spent; a combination is best.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Inequality is the uneven distribution of income and wealth; absolute poverty is when people cannot afford basic needs such as food and shelter. A progressive tax takes a larger percentage of income as income rises, so high earners pay a bigger share.

Oxfam reports that Vietnam has 210 super-rich people whose combined earnings for one year could take 3.2 million people out of poverty and end absolute poverty. This shows income is extremely unequal, and that a higher progressive tax on top incomes could raise a lot of revenue. Higher tax rates on the rich directly narrow the gap between rich and poor after tax, so inequality falls. The extra revenue could then fund healthcare, education and job creation, as Oxfam suggests. Free healthcare means poor families do not lose income through illness; education raises the skills of poor children, so they can get better-paid jobs later. This tackles poverty at its root. Keeping tax rates low for low earners at the same time raises their take-home pay and encourages people to join the labour force.

However, progressive taxes may not be the most effective way. Higher tax rates can reduce the incentive for high earners and entrepreneurs to work and invest, and some may move their money or businesses abroad, so revenue may be less than expected. Oxfam asks business leaders to pay their fair share of tax, which suggests some do not, so raising rates may not raise revenue if collection is weak. Raising taxes can also be politically unpopular. Most importantly, a tax by itself does not help the poor; it only works if the revenue is spent well. Other policies act more directly: a living wage raises the pay of low-income workers straight away, benefit payments give money directly to the poorest, and job creation reduces unemployment, a major cause of poverty.

Overall, an increase in progressive taxes is an important part of reducing inequality in Vietnam, because the income of 210 people is so large compared with the needs of 3.2 million poor people. However, it is probably not the most effective way on its own. Its success depends on whether the government can actually collect the tax without driving away investment, and on whether the revenue is spent on healthcare, education and jobs. A combination of progressive tax, a living wage and spending on public services is likely to work best.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines inequality, absolute poverty and progressive tax, applies 210 super-rich and 3.2 million poor in chains from tax to revenue to services; evaluates disincentives, avoidance, unpopularity and more direct alternatives (living wage, benefits, jobs); judgement depends on collection and how revenue is spent.

Assess · November 2021 · Paper 2 · Q2(g)

  1. Define healthcare as a merit good with external benefits; link health to ability to work and earn.
  2. Data: healthcare spending in England was £129bn in 2018-2019, expected to be nearly £134bn by 2019-2020; access is free; Figure 2 shows spending trend since 2010/11.
  3. Chain: free treatment -> poorer people treated quickly -> fewer sick days and longer working lives -> higher incomes and productivity -> less poverty and inequality.
  4. Chain: healthcare also creates jobs for nurses, doctors and builders.
  5. Against: huge cost to taxpayers and opportunity cost; free access creates excess demand and waiting lists; other policies (education, progressive tax) may target inequality more directly.
  6. Judgement: useful as part of a mix; depends on whether extra spending reaches the poorest and reduces waiting times.

Assess · June 2022 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

  1. Define benefit payments and redistribution; Universal Credit helps people on low income or out of work.
  2. Data: Figure 3 monthly standard allowance from £342.72 (single, under 25) to £594.04 (couple, over 25).
  3. Chain: tax revenue paid as benefits to low-income households -> more disposable income -> can buy necessities -> lower relative poverty and inequality.
  4. Against: may discourage work and create a poverty trap; opportunity cost and tax burden; allowance may be too low to cover living costs.
  5. Judgement: clear advantages for the poorest; depends on the size of payments versus living costs and on work incentives; best combined with progressive tax and education.
