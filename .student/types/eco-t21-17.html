Taxes, subsidies and parks to protect the environment

Knowledge card

Taxes, subsidies and parks to protect the environment

Written
  • An externality is a side effect of producing or consuming something that falls on a third party (someone not involved in the deal). A negative externality is a harm, such as pollution; a positive externality is a benefit.
  • A tax on a polluting good or activity raises its price, so people buy or produce less of it and the harm falls. The government also collects revenue.
  • A subsidy is money the government gives to a producer (to lower costs) or to a consumer (to lower the price). Subsidising a clean option, such as solar power or buses, makes people switch to it.
  • Government provision of parks and national parks protects land and wildlife from development, gives space for exercise and leisure, and can attract tourists.
  • Drawbacks: subsidies and parks cost the government money and have an opportunity cost (the next best use of that money or land); taxes may barely work when demand is price inelastic and can hurt low-income households.
  • Other ways to protect the environment include regulation, fines and pollution permits; these are useful alternatives in an evaluation.

Before you begin

  • What a negative and a positive externality are
  • How a change in price affects the quantity demanded
  • The idea of opportunity cost
  • Price elasticity of demand in simple terms (whether buyers react a lot or a little to price)
1

The method

  1. Name the measure and say what it is (a tax, a subsidy, or providing a park).

    Why: The first mark in short answers goes to identifying the method.

  2. Explain how it changes price or behaviour: a tax makes the harmful choice dearer; a subsidy makes the clean choice cheaper; a park keeps land protected.

    Why: Marks reward the mechanism, not just the name.

  3. Finish the chain: less of the harmful activity or more of the clean one, so pollution or the external cost falls (or the external benefit rises).

    Why: The environmental result completes the explanation.

  4. Use the figures and names in the data (tax rates, subsidy amounts, visitor numbers).

    Why: Application to context earns the context mark and the AO3 marks.

  5. For a 9-mark Assess or 12-mark Evaluate, follow the Assess or Evaluate card: weigh cost and opportunity cost, inelastic demand, effects on low incomes or firms, and alternative policies, then judge.

    Why: AO4 marks need a balanced argument and a supported conclusion.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Naming a tax or subsidy but never saying how it changes what people buy or produce.
  • ✗
    Mixing up the two tools, for example saying a subsidy is put on the polluting good.
  • ✗
    Ignoring the figures in the extract, so the answer stays general and loses application marks.
  • ✗
    Giving only advantages in a 9 or 12-mark answer, with no counter-argument such as cost or inelastic demand.
  • ✗
    Ending without a judgement, or with a judgement that does not say what the outcome depends on.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

What a complete response includes

Marks go to explaining how the measure alters prices or behaviour and the resulting cut in environmental harm, applied to the extract. Longer answers also need costs, limits and alternatives weighed, then a supported judgement.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the extract: Germany may raise the tax on meat from a reduced 7% to the standard 19%; animal methane is 14.5% of greenhouse gas emissions.
  2. Define the tool briefly and explain the chain: higher tax, higher price, less meat bought, fewer animals, lower emissions.
  3. Add a second benefit: extra tax revenue that can fund other green schemes, and a push towards meat alternatives.
  4. Weigh the limits: inelastic demand, the burden on low-income families and farmers, and alternative policies.
  5. Judge: say whether taxation is worthwhile and what its success depends on.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A tax is a charge the government adds to a good, so it can be used to make a product that harms the environment more expensive. In Germany, meat at present carries a reduced tax rate of 7%; raising it to the standard 19% would push up the price shoppers pay for meat. At a higher price, households are likely to buy less meat, so farmers rear fewer animals. Since methane from animals makes up 14.5% of greenhouse gas emissions, which is more than the direct emissions from transport, lower meat output should cut a major source of pollution. In this way the tax makes buyers pay something closer to the true cost of meat, including the harm that climate change does to third parties. A second advantage is that the German Government would collect extra revenue, which it could spend on other environmental projects or on animal welfare, so the environment gains twice. The higher price also gives consumers a reason to switch to plant-based foods that do less damage.

However, the tax may not reduce meat eating by much. Meat is a regular part of many diets, so demand may be price inelastic: people keep buying it even after a 12 percentage point rise in the tax, which means emissions fall only slightly while shoppers simply pay more. The tax also falls hardest on low-income families, who spend a bigger share of their income on food, and German farmers could lose income if sales drop. Other methods, such as regulating farms, pollution permits or subsidies for meat alternatives, might target emissions more directly.

Overall, a meat tax is a sensible tool because it works through prices and raises revenue, but how much it protects the environment depends mainly on how price elastic the demand for meat is. If demand is inelastic, Germany should not rely on taxation alone and should pair it with other measures, for example using the revenue to subsidise greener alternatives.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked, 3 AO2 + 3 AO3 + 3 AO4. Defining the tax and explaining the price-to-emissions chain earns AO2; using 7%, 19% and 14.5% plus the revenue point earns AO3; inelastic demand, effects on poorer families and farmers, alternatives and a conditional judgement give the balanced evaluation for Level 3 (7-9).

Assess · June 2021 · Paper 2 · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define indirect tax and negative externality; data: animal methane is 14.5% of greenhouse gas emissions, more than direct transport emissions.
  2. For: tax from 7% to 19% -> higher meat price -> lower quantity demanded -> fewer animals -> less methane; price closer to full social cost.
  3. For: tax revenue can fund animal welfare or cleaner farming; incentive to switch to meat alternatives.
  4. Against: demand for meat may be price inelastic, so emissions fall only a little; tax is regressive for low-income families; farmers lose income.
  5. Judgement: useful because polluters pay, but depends on price elasticity of demand and use of revenue; best combined with subsidies or regulation.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A tax on meat is an indirect tax: a charge added to the price of a good and paid to the government. Germany wants to use it because meat production creates a negative externality, which is a cost that falls on third parties. Methane from animals causes 14.5% of greenhouse gas emissions, more than the direct emissions from transport. Farmers and shoppers do not pay for this damage, so the market price of meat is too low and too much meat is produced and eaten.

Raising the tax on meat from the reduced rate of 7% to the standard rate of 19% would push up the price of meat. As the price rises, the quantity of meat demanded falls, so farms need fewer animals. Fewer animals means less methane, so the external cost falls and the price moves closer to the full social cost. The tax also earns revenue for the government. This money could pay for cleaner farming methods or better animal welfare, which is the second aim in the data. Higher meat prices also give firms an incentive to develop and sell meat alternatives.

However, the tax may not cut pollution by much. Meat is a regular part of many diets, so demand may be price inelastic. If demand is inelastic, a rise from 7% to 19% causes only a small fall in the quantity bought, so methane falls only a little and the tax mostly raises revenue. The tax is also regressive: low-income families spend a larger share of their income on food, so they lose the most. German farmers could also see lower sales and lower incomes.

On balance, taxation is a sensible tool for Germany because it makes polluters pay and raises money for the environment. How effective it is depends on the price elasticity of demand for meat and on what the revenue is spent on. It is likely to work best when combined with other methods, such as subsidies for meat alternatives or regulation of large farms.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines indirect tax and negative externality, applies the 7% to 19% rise and the 14.5% methane figure in full chains (price, demand, emissions, revenue), gives a balanced counter-case (inelastic demand, regressive, farm incomes) and ends with a judgement that depends on elasticity and use of revenue.

Evaluate · June 2022 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define negative externality; data: up to 60,000 deaths in 2018, pollution costs about 5% of GDP a year; 3.6 million cars and 58 million motorbikes, many old.
  2. For: 33% higher fuel tax -> higher fuel price -> less driving or switch to buses -> fewer emissions; polluters pay nearer the full social cost.
  3. For: revenue of $2.4 bn a year ($650.2 m extra) can fund public transport or replacing old vehicles.
  4. Against: fuel demand likely price inelastic (work trips, deliveries) so pollution falls little; cost-push inflation; regressive for poorer motorbike users; public transport may not cope.
  5. Alternatives: emission standards, subsidies for electric vehicles, scrapping schemes.
  6. Judgement: partly effective; depends on price elasticity and whether revenue improves alternatives; best as part of a mix.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Air pollution is a negative externality: a cost that falls on third parties who did not cause it. In Vietnam, up to 60,000 deaths in 2018 were linked to air pollution, and poor air quality costs the country about 5% of GDP per year. A main cause is transport. Vietnam has 58 million motorbikes and 3.6 million automobiles, and many are old with limited emission control. Drivers do not pay these external costs, so too much fuel is used.

An environmental tax on petrol and diesel is an indirect tax that raises the price of fuel. A 33% rise in the tax makes each journey more expensive, so some people drive less, share vehicles or switch to buses. Less fuel burned means fewer emissions, so the external cost falls. The tax makes polluters pay closer to the full social cost of their choices. It also raises revenue: the proposal would bring in $2.4 bn each year, an increase of $650.2 m. The government could spend this on cleaner public transport or on helping owners replace old vehicles, which attacks the cause of the pollution directly. Over time, higher fuel prices also give people a reason to buy newer, cleaner vehicles.

However, the tax may be less effective than it looks. Many trips are needed to get to work or to deliver goods, so demand for fuel is likely to be price inelastic. If demand is inelastic, the quantity of fuel bought falls only a little and pollution falls only slightly; the main effect is higher revenue. Higher fuel prices also raise firms' transport costs, which can push up prices across the economy and cause cost-push inflation. Low-income households that depend on their vehicles to get to work lose a larger share of their income, so the tax is regressive. If public transport cannot cope with extra passengers, people have no real alternative and will keep driving.

Other policies could work alongside the tax. Regulation, such as emission standards, would target the old vehicles directly, and subsidies for electric vehicles would reward the cleaner choice.

In conclusion, the tax is likely to be only partly effective on its own. It sends a clear price signal and raises $2.4 bn a year, but it will only cut pollution by a large amount if drivers have real alternatives. The judgement depends on how price elastic the demand for fuel is and on whether the revenue is used to improve public transport. A tax combined with regulation and spending on cleaner transport would protect the environment most.

Why it reaches the top level: Accurate terms (externality, indirect tax, inelastic demand, regressive) applied to the 60,000 deaths, 5% of GDP, vehicle numbers, 33% rise and revenue figures; coherent chains on both sides; alternatives considered; a supported conclusion that depends on elasticity and use of revenue.

Assess · June 2019 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q2(g)

  1. Define: parks give positive externalities (benefits to people who do not pay, e.g. better health); free to use, so the market would provide too few and government must provide them.
  2. For: data says walkers in Hyde Park did better than on Oxford Street, where vehicle fumes removed the benefit of exercise; chain: healthier people -> less government health spending -> fewer sick days -> higher productivity.
  3. For: parks also protect wildlife and give children a place to play, and big parks can attract visitors who spend in local shops.
  4. Against: opportunity cost: the land and money could be used for housing or business; the government pays to create and maintain the park from taxes.
  5. Against: parks do not remove the cause (vehicle fumes); fuel taxes or vehicle regulation may cut pollution more directly.
  6. Judgement: provide more parks where many people will use them and alongside policies that cut traffic pollution; depends on land cost and how many people use them.

Evaluate · November 2020 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

  1. Define subsidy (government payment that lowers costs or prices); data: transport subsidy up by EUR 9 m to EUR 101m, bus subsidy from EUR 34.8 m to EUR 43.3 m.
  2. For: lower fares -> bus passengers grew 33% in one month -> fewer cars on the road -> less pollution, a negative externality reduced.
  3. For: easier movement for less well-off residents, so the subsidy also helps equity and access to jobs.
  4. Against: opportunity cost of EUR 101m paid from taxes; the air service subsidy more than doubled to EUR 5.53 m, and flights increase pollution, so the policy is not fully consistent.
  5. Against: passengers may be people who already walked or cycled, not car drivers; regulation or fines could cut pollution at lower cost and raise revenue.
  6. Judgement: advantages are real if the extra passengers come from cars; depends on how much transport adds to pollution and whether the cost can be sustained.

Evaluate · June 2023 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

  1. Define subsidy; data: EUR 6.9bn for renewable energy in 2022 to 2023; renewables almost 47% of energy, up from 30% in 2011.
  2. For: subsidy lowers producers' costs -> more wind and solar supply -> less fossil fuel burned -> fewer emissions; Spain has more wind and sun than most of Europe.

A-Grade Plan · Open its teaching block

Learn these types first: External costs and external benefits · Price elasticity of demand: meaning, values and uses

Answer technique: Analyse: build chains of reasoning from the data (6 marks) · Assess: weigh up factors using the data and judge (9 marks) · Describe: name one consequence and develop it (2 marks) · Evaluate: balanced argument and a supported conclusion (12 marks) · Explain: one point, two linked expansions, in context (3 marks) · Multiple choice: choose the one correct answer (1 mark) · State or identify: give one correct point (1 mark)
