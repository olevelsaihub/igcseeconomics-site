Knowledge card

Effects of unemployment on the economy, government and confidence

Written
  • Unemployed people produce nothing, so output (GDP) is lower than it could be: the economy wastes some of its resources.
  • The government pays more in unemployment benefits (welfare payments to people without work) when unemployment rises.
  • Tax revenue falls when unemployment rises: fewer people pay direct taxes such as income tax, and lower spending means less indirect tax such as VAT (a tax on spending).
  • When unemployment falls, the opposite happens: more income tax and VAT, and less benefit spending, giving the government more to spend on services.
  • Rising unemployment lowers consumer confidence (people fear for their jobs and spend less) and business confidence (firms expect fewer sales and delay investment).
  • Unemployment can increase poverty and social problems such as stress and poor health, especially if it lasts a long time.
  • Possible upside for firms: a larger pool of workers makes recruiting easier and weakens pressure for pay rises.

Before you begin

  • What unemployment means
  • The difference between direct taxes (on income) and indirect taxes (on spending)
  • What consumer and business confidence mean

The method

  1. Check the direction: rising or high unemployment, or falling or low unemployment.

    Why: Every chain reverses with the direction, and getting it wrong loses the marks.

  2. Name the effect asked for: output, benefit spending, tax revenue, confidence, poverty.

    Why: Identifying the effect is the first mark in Explain.

  3. Chain it through incomes and spending: jobs lost, incomes fall, spending falls, then the named effect.

    Why: Development marks reward the link, not just the outcome.

  4. Use the rate or figures given (for example 27.7% or a fall from 4.2% to 3.4%).

    Why: Context and AO3 marks need the data used.

  5. For a 9-mark Assess, follow the Assess card: add the other side (easier recruitment, lower wage pressure, short-term or frictional unemployment) and judge what the impact depends on.

    Why: AO4 needs balance and a supported conclusion.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying tax revenue rises when unemployment rises; both income tax and VAT tend to fall.
  • ✗
    Mentioning only income tax and missing the indirect tax effect through lower spending.
  • ✗
    Writing 'confidence falls' without saying what people or firms then do (spend less, invest less).
  • ✗
    In Assess answers, giving only costs with no counter-point or judgement.
  • ✗
    Not using the unemployment rate or region given in the data.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the data: unemployment reached 27.7% in 2017, a 13-year high; 39% of unemployed South Africans have never worked, rising to 60.3% for young people.
  2. Define unemployment briefly.
  3. Effect 1: lost output: over a quarter of the workforce produces nothing, so GDP is lower.
  4. Effect 2: the government pays more benefits but collects less income tax and VAT, so less is left for other services; it may also need training schemes for young people.
  5. Effect 3: poverty and social problems rise.
  6. Other side: firms recruit more easily and face less pressure to raise wages.
  7. Judge: the impact depends on the type and length of unemployment; with long-term youth unemployment this high, the costs clearly outweigh the gains.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Unemployment is when people who want to work, and are able to, cannot get a job. In 2017 South Africa's unemployment rate reached 27.7%, its highest level in 13 years. With more than a quarter of the workforce out of work, the economy is producing far less than it could. These workers' skills and time are wasted, so South Africa's GDP is lower and the country grows more slowly than it could.

High unemployment also puts pressure on the government's budget. More people need unemployment benefits, so welfare spending rises. At the same time, people without jobs pay no income tax and, with lower incomes, spend less, so the government also collects less VAT. With higher spending and lower revenue, there is less money for schools, hospitals and infrastructure, or the government must borrow more. The government may also need to pay for education and training schemes, especially as 60.3% of unemployed young people have never had a job and so have no work experience. For households, losing a job means losing most of their income, so poverty rises, and long periods without work can lead to stress, poor health and crime, which the data suggests is a concern.

However, there can be some benefits for firms. With 27.7% unemployment there is a large pool of workers, so firms in South Africa may find it easier and cheaper to recruit. Workers also have less power to demand pay rises, so labour costs may stay low, helping firms compete with foreign producers and possibly attract investment.

Overall, the impact of unemployment depends on its type and how long it lasts. Short-term frictional unemployment does little harm, but South Africa's problem is very high and long-lasting, with many young people never having worked. This means the costs of lost output, a heavier burden on government finances and rising poverty are likely to be much greater than the gains to firms from cheaper labour.

How the marks are earned

Levels marked out of 9. AO2 for defining unemployment and developing output, benefit, tax and poverty chains; AO3 for using 27.7%, the 13-year high and 60.3% of young people never having worked; AO4 for the recruitment and wage-cost upside and a judgement on type and duration.

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Assess · June 2019 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define unemployment; data: 27.7% in 2017, highest in 13 years; 39% of unemployed never worked, 60.3% for young people.
  2. Economy: unused labour -> output below potential, lower spending -> downward multiplier, slower growth.
  3. Government: less income and spending tax, more benefit spending -> opportunity cost for public services; need for training schemes.
  4. Social: young people never gain skills -> long-term unemployment, poverty, crime.
  5. Other side: large labour pool and lower wage pressure may help firms compete and attract investment.
  6. Judgement: very damaging; depends on duration, type of unemployment and training policy.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Unemployment means people of working age who want a job and are available for work do not have one. In 2017, South Africa's unemployment rate reached 27.7%, its highest level in 13 years, so more than a quarter of the labour force had no job.

The first impact is lost output. Unemployed workers are an unused factor of production, so the economy produces below its potential and GDP is lower than it could be. Unemployed people have little income, so they cut their spending. Firms then sell less and may cut more jobs, a downward multiplier effect that slows economic growth.

The second impact is on the government. With 27.7% unemployed, fewer people pay income tax and lower spending reduces indirect tax revenue, while spending on unemployment benefits and support rises. This worsens the government's budget and creates an opportunity cost, as less money is left for schools, hospitals and infrastructure.

The third impact is social and long term. The data shows that 39% of unemployed South Africans have never worked, and for young people the figure is 60.3%. People who never work do not gain skills or experience, so they become less employable over time and unemployment becomes harder to reduce. This can increase poverty and inequality and lead to social problems (for example, crime may rise), which can discourage investment. The government may need to spend more on education and training schemes.

However, the impact is not all negative. A large pool of available workers means firms can recruit easily, and weaker wage demands keep labour costs down. Lower costs may make South African firms more competitive and could attract foreign investment, which in time creates jobs. The impact also depends on the type of unemployment: people who are only between jobs may find work quickly.

Overall, unemployment at 27.7% is likely to be very damaging, especially because so many young unemployed people have never worked. How severe it is depends on how long people stay unemployed, the type of unemployment and whether the government invests in training to help young people into work.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines unemployment, applies 27.7%, the 13-year high, 39% and 60.3% in developed chains (lost output and multiplier, fiscal pressure, long-term skills loss and poverty), gives a counter-view (cheaper, available labour) and reaches a judgement that depends on duration and type of unemployment.

Assess · June 2023 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

  1. Define unemployment; data: North East rate rose to 5.3% in the three months to October 2021; output fell and business confidence was severely harmed.
  2. Impact: less income and spending -> lower demand for local firms -> lower output and growth, possible further job losses.
  3. Government: higher benefit spending and lower tax revenue; may need training schemes; relative poverty may rise.
  4. Other side: benefit claimants fell by around 3,500 to 87,780, so some unemployment may be voluntary or short term; firms can recruit more easily with lower wage pressure.
  5. Judgement: harmful for the region; depends on type and duration of unemployment and how quickly confidence recovers.

Assess · November 2023 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

  1. Define unemployment rate; data: 3.4% in November 2022, down from 4.2% in January; employment up 64,000 to a record 13.77 million; near 50-year low.
  2. For: more people in work -> more income tax collected; more spending -> more indirect tax (GST/VAT); less spent on benefits.
  3. Use: extra revenue can fund public services or allow lower taxes elsewhere.
  4. Against: new jobs may be low paid, so little extra tax; informal work or evasion; labour shortage from lack of migration -> firms bid up wages -> cost-push inflation that may reduce real spending.
  5. Judgement: tax revenue likely rises; depends on the type and pay of new jobs and how long unemployment stays low.
