Written-answer check

After your attempt, see the full worked explanation

Worked explanation

  1. Define competition and pick the case facts: many restaurants, specialised food, discounts for regulars, live entertainment.
  2. Benefit chain 1: many rivals keep prices low, so customers spend less.
  3. Benefit chain 2: rivals compete on quality, variety and extras, so customers get more choice and better service.
  4. Drawback chain: low prices squeeze profits, some cut quality or close, so choice can shrink.
  5. Judge: depends on visitor numbers; with a steady flow of tourists, most effects are likely positive.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Competition is rivalry between firms trying to win customers from each other. Dahab has a large number of restaurants competing for the same visitors, so customers are likely to gain in several ways. First, if one restaurant charges much more than its neighbours, customers can simply walk next door. This forces owners to keep prices low, so visitors can eat out cheaply and have more money left for activities such as water sports. Second, restaurants also compete in ways other than price. Some specialise in certain types of food, some reward regular visitors with discounts and others offer live entertainment. Customers therefore enjoy a wide choice of food and atmosphere, and owners have a strong reason to give good service so that visitors return on later trips. However, competition can also harm customers. When prices are pushed down, profit per meal falls. Some owners may respond by buying cheaper ingredients or employing fewer staff, which lowers quality. Restaurants that cannot cover their costs may close, reducing choice, and a customer's favourite place might disappear. Discounts that only go to regular visitors also mean first-time tourists may pay more. On balance, the impact on customers is likely to be mainly positive. Dahab is popular with visitors from around the world and from within Egypt, and many return, so restaurants have a steady flow of customers. This makes it more likely that they can stay profitable while keeping prices low and quality high. The drawbacks would matter more if visitor numbers fell, because then the weakest restaurants would be forced to cut quality or close.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked out of 12 (4 knowledge and application, 4 analysis, 4 evaluation). Definition and case use earn application; developed chains on price, choice and service earn analysis; drawbacks (cost cutting, closures) plus a conclusion depending on visitor numbers give the balanced, supported evaluation needed for Level 3.

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Evaluate · January 2023 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define competition; Dahab has a large number of restaurants competing for local, Egyptian and international visitors, many of whom return.
  2. For: rivals keep prices low; discounts for regular visits -> customers spend less.
  3. For: non-price competition (specialist food, live entertainment) -> more choice and better service to keep repeat customers.
  4. Against: thin profit margins -> cheaper ingredients, fewer staff, less investment; some restaurants close, reducing choice; one-off tourists may get less attention to quality.
  5. Judgement: customers gain overall; depends on restaurants earning enough profit to keep quality and on the share of repeat visitors.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Competition is when firms try to win customers from rival firms, for example through lower prices, better quality or different products. Dahab has a large number of restaurants competing for customers, who include visitors from around the world, visitors from other parts of Egypt and people who return many times.

The first likely impact is lower prices. If one restaurant charges too much, customers can easily go to another nearby. So restaurants must keep prices close to those of their rivals, and some offer discounts for regular visits. Lower prices let customers eat out more often for the same budget.

Second, competition raises quality and choice. Restaurants also compete on non-price factors: some specialise in certain types of food, while others provide live entertainment. Customers therefore enjoy a wide variety of meals and experiences and can choose the one that best suits their tastes. To keep customers returning, restaurants must offer good service and good food, because unhappy customers will try a rival next time. This matters in Dahab, where many visitors return many times; a restaurant with a poor reputation would lose this repeat custom. Competition also encourages efficiency, because high-cost restaurants must cut waste to survive.

However, competition can also harm customers. Strong price competition squeezes profit margins. To cut costs, some restaurants may use cheaper ingredients or employ fewer staff, so quality and service fall. Restaurants with low profits may not be able to invest in better kitchens or premises. Some may make losses and close, which reduces choice; customers might lose a favourite restaurant or a type of food that only one place served. In addition, many customers are tourists who may visit only once, so some restaurants may chase one-off visitors with low prices instead of building a reputation for quality. Discounts for regular visits also mean that first-time visitors may not gain from lower prices.

In conclusion, competition is likely to benefit restaurant customers in Dahab overall, mainly through lower prices, more choice and pressure to keep service good. However, this depends on whether restaurants can still earn enough profit to maintain quality, and on how many customers are repeat visitors who reward good restaurants. If competition becomes so intense that restaurants cut quality or close, some of the benefits for customers would be lost.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines competition and applies the many restaurants, specialised food, discounts, live entertainment and repeat visitors in developed chains on price, quality, choice and efficiency; balances with cost-cutting, closures and one-off tourists; gives a supported conclusion with 'depends on' conditions.
