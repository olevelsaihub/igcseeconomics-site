Knowledge card

Oligopoly: features, collusion and effects

Written
  • An oligopoly is a market in which a few large firms control most of the sales (e.g. five firms with most of the market share).
  • Features: a few large firms dominate, products are differentiated (branded or made different), there are barriers to entry, and firms are interdependent (each watches and reacts to its rivals).
  • Non-price competition means winning customers by things other than price: branding, logos, customer service, loyalty cards, quality, environmental image, modern facilities.
  • Price competition can lead to price wars, where rivals keep cutting prices, which benefits consumers in the short run.
  • Collusion is when rival firms secretly cooperate, e.g. agreeing to fix prices or limit supply; a group that does this is a cartel. It acts like a monopoly.
  • Effects: collusion means higher prices, less choice and harder entry for new firms; rivalry can bring lower prices, more choice, better quality and innovation funded by profits.

Before you begin

  • What market share means.
  • The meaning of monopoly and competition.
  • What barriers to entry are.
1

The method

  1. Define oligopoly: a market in which a few large firms control most sales.

    Why: Knowledge marks start with an accurate definition.

  2. To show a market is an oligopoly, match each feature to evidence: number of firms or shares, branding and differences, barriers such as high set-up costs or permits.

    Why: Application marks need the data used as evidence for each feature.

  3. For effects of collusion, chain: firms agree prices or limit supply, competition falls, consumers pay more and have less choice.

    Why: Analysis credit comes from a developed mechanism, not a bare statement.

  4. For benefits of rivalry, chain: firms fight for market share through price wars or non-price competition, so consumers get lower prices, better service or more choice.

    Why: Shows how firm behaviour affects consumers.

  5. For 9-mark Assess or 12-mark Evaluate, follow those cards: weigh rivalry benefits against collusion and barrier costs, then judge.

    Why: Balance and a supported judgement are needed for top levels.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying an oligopoly has one dominant firm (that is a monopoly).
  • ✗
    Listing features without pointing to evidence in the data (firm names, market shares, branding).
  • ✗
    Describing collusion as 'firms working together' without the result: higher prices or less choice.
  • ✗
    Assuming oligopoly is always bad for consumers and ignoring price wars and non-price benefits.
  • ✗
    Calling a product with differences between brands a 'unique product'.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Define oligopoly.
  2. Feature 1, few dominant firms: five main companies supply Uganda's petrol.
  3. Feature 2, differentiation and non-price competition: logos, colours, service, environment, facilities, loyalty cards, motivated staff.
  4. Feature 3, barriers to entry: very high set-up costs.
  5. Link each feature to why it shows an oligopoly.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

An oligopoly is a market in which a small number of large firms control most of the sales. Uganda's petrol market fits this, as it is led by just five main companies, so a small number of sellers supply most of the petrol. A second feature of oligopoly is that firms make their products different from each other and compete mainly on things other than price. Each Ugandan company has its own logo and colours so drivers recognise its brand. They also stand out in different ways: Vivo Energy focuses on excellent customer service, Total Energies on its environmental impact, Nile Energy on modern facilities, Hass Petroleum on a loyalty card and Stabex International on motivated staff. Because petrol itself is very similar, this non-price competition is how each firm tries to keep customers returning to its stations instead of a rival's. A third feature is barriers to entry. The case states that set-up costs are very high, as a new firm would need storage, transport and a network of filling stations. Few new firms can afford this, so the same five companies keep their dominant positions. With few dominant firms, branded differences, non-price competition and high entry barriers, the petrol market in Uganda shows the main features of an oligopoly.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked out of 6 (3 application, 3 analysis). The definition shows knowledge; five firms, named brands and their differences, and high set-up costs apply the data; explaining why each feature points to oligopoly gives the developed analysis for Level 3.

Assess · June 2022 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q2(h)

Answer plan

  1. Define oligopoly; Figure 4: Vodacom 33%, Tigo 27%, Airtel 26%, Halotel 10%, Zantel 3%, others 1% (June 2019).
  2. Chain: large firms -> economies of scale -> lower average costs -> lower prices; close rivalry -> deals, bundles and better coverage.
  3. Chain: high profits -> research and development -> better network quality.
  4. Against: Zantel-Tigo merger and Smart Telecoms' exit reduce choice; barriers to entry; costly non-price competition; risk of collusion.
  5. Judgement: benefits consumers while rivalry among the top three stays strong; depends on regulation of mergers and collusion.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

an oligopoly is a market where a few large firms have most of the sales. In Tanzania's telecoms market in June 2019, Vodacom had 33%, Tigo 27% and Airtel 26%, so three firms held most of the market, while Halotel had 10% and Zantel 3%.

The oligopoly may benefit consumers. The largest firms serve a large share of all subscribers, so they gain economies of scale: the cost of masts and networks is spread over many users, lowering average costs, which can be passed on as lower prices. Because the top three firms have similar shares, rivalry between them is strong. Each firm knows that if it does not offer good deals it will lose subscribers to a rival, so they compete through price cuts, bundles of data and calls, and better coverage. High profits also allow firms like Vodacom to invest in research and development, such as faster networks, which improves quality for consumers. The merger of Tigo's 27% with Zantel's 3% could even create a stronger rival to Vodacom, keeping competition at the top intense.

However, the oligopoly may also harm consumers. The market is becoming more concentrated: after the merger there are fewer independent firms, and Smart Telecoms stopped trading because it was unable to keep up with bigger rivals, so consumers lost a choice. High barriers to entry, such as the cost of building a network, make it hard for new firms to enter. The remaining firms are interdependent and may avoid price wars, choosing non-price competition such as advertising, which raises costs rather than lowering prices. In the worst case they may collude to fix prices, which is illegal but hard to detect, leading to higher prices and less choice.

Overall, the oligopoly in Tanzania can benefit consumers while the few large firms still compete closely, as their similar market shares suggest. However, as mergers and exits reduce the number of firms, the risk of higher prices grows. Whether consumers benefit depends on whether competition between the leading firms stays strong and on whether the government controls mergers and prevents collusion.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines oligopoly and applies the 33%, 27%, 26%, 10% and 3% shares, the Tigo-Zantel merger and Smart Telecoms' exit in chains on scale economies, rivalry and R&D; balances with concentration, entry barriers, non-price competition and collusion; judgement depends on continued rivalry and regulation.

Evaluate · June 2019 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define oligopoly and interdependence; use Figure 7 to show a few named supermarkets (Albert Heijn, Super Unie, Jumbo, Lidl, Aldi) hold most of the Dutch market.
  2. Chain: interdependence -> fear that price cuts are matched -> avoid price wars -> non-price competition (advertising, loyalty cards, delivery, opening hours).
  3. Chain: large size -> bulk-buying economies of scale -> lower average costs and high profits; brands and store networks create barriers to entry.
  4. Chain: small 'Others' firms specialise in niche markets to survive.
  5. Against: discounters still compete on price; non-price competition is costly; collusion is illegal; small firms may be taken over; online shopping may change the market.
  6. Judgement: firms mostly pushed to non-price competition and growth; depends on the strength of leading firms against discounters and on changing consumer habits.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

An oligopoly is a market where a few large firms have most of the sales. Figure 7 shows that Dutch supermarkets form an oligopoly: in 2017 Albert Heijn had about 35% of the market, Super Unie about 29.5% and Jumbo about 18.5%, so the three largest chains held over 80% between them. Lidl (about 7%) and Aldi (about 6%) are much smaller, and all other shops together have only about 3%.

Competing in an oligopoly influences firms in several ways. First, firms are interdependent: each supermarket's sales depend on what its rivals do. If Albert Heijn cuts prices, Jumbo will lose customers unless it responds. Because a price cut is likely to be matched, a price war could reduce profits for all of them. So firms are pushed to avoid price competition and instead use non-price competition, such as advertising, loyalty cards, own-brand ranges, online delivery, longer opening hours and better customer service.

Second, the large firms can benefit from economies of scale. Buying food in huge quantities earns discounts from suppliers, so their average costs are lower. This lets them earn high profits, which they can reinvest in new stores and technology, strengthening their position. Strong brand names and the cost of building a network of stores create barriers to entry, so new firms find it hard to break in.

Third, smaller firms in the 'Others' group are pushed to specialise: they survive by serving niche markets, such as organic or local products, which the big chains do not focus on.

However, the influence is not the same for every firm. Smaller chains such as Lidl and Aldi, with only about 7% and 6% of the market, may need to compete harder on price to win customers from the leaders, so price competition can still happen in part of the market. Heavy spending on advertising and loyalty schemes raises costs, which can cut profits. Firms may be tempted to collude to keep prices high, but this is illegal and risks heavy penalties. Smaller firms risk being taken over or forced out by the dominant chains. The market is also changing, and firms that fail to keep up with trends such as online grocery shopping may lose market share, however large they are now.

Overall, competing in an oligopoly mainly pushes Dutch supermarkets towards non-price competition and towards growing larger to gain economies of scale, while forcing small firms to specialise. How strongly firms are influenced depends on how dominant the leading chains stay (over 80% for the top three) compared with smaller rivals like Lidl and Aldi, and on how quickly consumer habits, especially online shopping, change. Firms that innovate are likely to keep their share; those that do not may lose it.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines oligopoly and applies Figure 7's named supermarkets in developed chains on interdependence, non-price competition, scale economies, barriers to entry and niche specialisation; evaluates discounters' price competition, cost of non-price rivalry, collusion risk, takeovers and online change; judgement depends on dominance and changing habits.
