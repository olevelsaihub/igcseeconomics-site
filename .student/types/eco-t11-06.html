Knowledge card

Profit maximisation and other business aims

Written
  • Profit = total revenue minus total costs. Economists assume that firms, especially in the private sector, aim to maximise profit.
  • Some firms choose other aims: maximising sales or revenue, caring for customers, charitable work, survival, or growth.
  • Managers may have different aims from owners. A manager paid by sales may prefer more sales to the highest profit.
  • Giving up some profit now can bring gains later: loyal customers, a good reputation, positive publicity or motivated staff.
  • A new firm may charge low prices to attract customers and survive, even though this means less profit at first.

Before you begin

  • Revenue, costs and profit.
  • The difference between the private and public sectors.
  • How a price change affects quantity demanded.
1

The method

  1. State the assumption that firms aim to maximise profit.

    Why: It sets up why the case is unusual.

  2. Identify the firm's actual aim from the case (customer care, charity, sales, survival, growth).

    Why: Naming the right aim is the first creditworthy step.

  3. Explain how this aim reduces profit in the short run (lower prices, extra costs).

    Why: Shows you understand the trade-off.

  4. Explain the possible gain: more customers, loyalty, reputation, publicity or motivated staff, using case figures.

    Why: This develops the reason into a full chain.

  5. For a 9-mark Assess, follow the Assess card: benefits, then counter-arguments (for example, lost customers or higher costs), then a judgement that says what it depends on.

    Why: AO4 marks need both sides weighed and a supported conclusion.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying the firm aims to maximise profit when the case clearly shows a different aim.
  • ✗
    Explaining the benefit but not saying that profit is lower in the short run.
  • ✗
    In Assess answers, giving only one side or no final judgement.
  • ✗
    Confusing consumer behaviour (habits, copying) with producer aims.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Identify Simona's current aim: customer care, with low prices and no penalty for lateness.
  2. Explain the problem it causes: wasted slots, long waits, lost customers, lower revenue, loan difficulty.
  3. Benefits of the change: more revenue per appointment, fewer wasted slots, loan repaid.
  4. Counter-arguments: customers may leave if demand is price elastic; reputation may suffer.
  5. Judge: likely beneficial, depending on the size of the price rise.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Economists assume that firms aim to maximise profit, but Simona has chosen a different aim: caring for her customers by keeping prices low and not charging anyone who arrives late or cancels at the last minute. This aim has caused problems. Because lateness and late cancellations cost customers nothing, appointment slots are wasted and her diary fills up, so some customers have had to wait weeks. Those customers have gone elsewhere, so her revenue has fallen and she is struggling to repay her bank loan.

Raising prices and charging a fee for lateness or late cancellation could bring clear benefits. Each appointment would earn more, and the fee pays her for time that would otherwise be lost, so revenue should rise and help her meet the loan repayments, cutting the risk that the business closes. The fee also gives customers a reason to arrive on time or cancel early, so fewer slots are wasted. Simona could then serve more customers each week, shorten waiting times and perhaps win back customers she has lost, raising revenue again.

However, some customers may come to Simona precisely because she is cheap and flexible. If there are other hairdressers nearby, demand for her service may be price elastic, so a price rise could cause a larger percentage fall in customers and total revenue could actually fall, making her money problems worse. A new fee could also annoy loyal customers and damage the friendly reputation she has built.

Overall, the changes are likely to benefit Simona, because her main problem is wasted appointments and the fee tackles this directly while also raising revenue for the loan. How much she gains depends on the size of the price rise: a small rise plus a fee should keep most customers, while a large rise risks driving them to rivals. She can still care for customers through good service while charging a fair price.

How the marks are earned

AO2/AO3 (up to 6): business aims explained and Simona's data applied to show falling demand, revenue and loan risk. AO4 (up to 3): counter-arguments on lost price-sensitive customers and reputation, then a 'depends on' judgement. Top level overall.

Grade 9 long answers

Assess · November 2020 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define business aims (profit maximisation, social or charitable aims, customer care); Lanterns aims to help young local people in a town with few jobs.
  2. For change: slow service -> complaints -> fewer customers -> lower revenue -> losses or closure; experienced staff raise productivity and profit.
  3. Against: customers may visit because of the social aim; experienced staff cost more in wages; the town loses job opportunities for under-25s.
  4. Middle option: train current staff or hire one experienced supervisor.
  5. Judgement: keep the aim but improve training; depends on how fast waiting times fall and how serious any losses become.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Firms can have different aims. Profit maximisation means producing where the gap between total revenue and total cost is as large as possible. Other aims include social or charitable aims, caring for customers or maximising sales. Lanterns was set up by Jo and Fiona to help young people in a town with few job opportunities, so it employs under-25s who were previously inexperienced and unemployed. Fiona now wants to hire experienced staff and aim to maximise profit.

There are good reasons to change. Customers are complaining about the time it takes for orders to arrive. If service stays slow, customers will go elsewhere, so demand and revenue fall. If revenue falls below costs, the café makes a loss and may close, which would end the jobs it gives young people anyway. Experienced waiters and waitresses work faster and make fewer mistakes, so productivity rises: more customers are served each hour, waiting times fall and customers return. Higher revenue and profit would make the café financially secure and could allow it to expand.

However, there are strong reasons not to change. Some customers may choose Lanterns because they support its aim of helping local young people. If that aim is dropped, these customers could leave, so revenue may not rise. Experienced staff usually expect higher wages, so costs rise and profit may not improve much. The change would also remove job opportunities in a town where they are scarce, which goes against the reason the sisters started the café. The real problem may be a lack of training rather than the aim itself. The sisters could train their young staff, or employ one experienced supervisor to guide them, which could speed up service while keeping the social aim.

On balance, Lanterns should not drop its social aim completely, but it must make enough profit to survive. The best option is likely to keep the aim while improving training. This depends on how quickly training can cut waiting times and on how many customers visit because of the social aim; if the café starts making serious losses, a stronger focus on profit may become necessary.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines business aims and profit maximisation, applies the café's social aim, young inexperienced staff and slow-service complaints in chains to revenue, costs and survival, gives a balanced counter-case (loyal customers, higher wages, training alternative) and reaches a conditional judgement.

Assess · November 2023 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

  1. Define business aims; Simona puts customer care ahead of profit: low prices, no charge for late arrivals or late cancellations.
  2. For change: missed and late appointments waste slots -> weeks-long waits -> lost customers -> lower revenue -> trouble repaying the bank loan.
  3. For change: higher prices and fees raise revenue per customer and discourage late cancellations, so more slots are used.
  4. Against: customers may come for the low prices and flexibility; demand may be price elastic, so higher prices could lose more customers and revenue.
  5. Alternative: manage bookings more carefully (reminders, waiting list).
  6. Judgement: changes help if demand is price inelastic and loyalty is strong; depends on customer response.

Assess · November 2025 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

  1. Define business aims; Fitnation's aim includes charitable work: discounted boxing for city residents one day a week.
  2. Benefits: boxing becomes affordable to more people; good reputation attracts new customers who may later join, raising sales and possibly profit.
  3. Against: facilities are very busy that day, so members paying $25 to $50 a month get less use.
  4. Chain: unhappy members cancel -> lower full-price revenue -> harder to make a profit.
  5. Judgement: beneficial if it brings in new members and members accept the trade-off; depends on how many members leave versus how many new customers join.
