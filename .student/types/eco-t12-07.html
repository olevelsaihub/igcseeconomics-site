Knowledge card

Economies and diseconomies of scale

Written
  • Economies of scale: the average cost (cost per unit) of production falls as a firm grows and produces more.
  • Internal economies happen inside one growing firm: purchasing (bulk buying discounts), marketing (advert cost spread over more sales), technical (better use of big machines), financial (cheaper loans), managerial (specialist managers), risk-bearing (many products or markets).
  • External economies happen when the whole industry grows in an area, so every firm's costs fall: a pool of skilled labour, better infrastructure (roads, rail, ports) and nearby suppliers.
  • Diseconomies of scale: average cost rises because a firm has grown too big. Causes: bureaucracy (many layers, rules and paperwork), poor communication, weaker control and low worker motivation.
  • Long-run average cost (LRAC) curve: U-shaped with output on the x-axis and average cost on the y-axis. Falling part = economies of scale; lowest point = most efficient scale; rising part = diseconomies.
  • Lower unit costs can mean lower prices for customers, higher profits and stronger competitiveness; mergers and takeovers are often justified by expected economies of scale.

Before you begin

  • Average cost = total cost / output.
  • What a merger and a takeover are.
  • The difference between a single firm and a whole industry.
1

The method

  1. Define the term in two parts: average cost falls (or rises) as the firm or industry expands.

    Why: Definitions earn one mark for the cost change and one for the link to growth.

  2. Decide which type fits the case: internal (one firm grows), external (the industry or area grows) or a diseconomy (the firm is too big).

    Why: Using the wrong type, e.g. bulk buying for an external economy, loses the application marks.

  3. Name the specific economy or diseconomy and tie it to a fact in the case (number of shops, users, rail line, staff numbers).

    Why: Application marks need the data, not a generic list.

  4. Chain it to the unit cost: what is spread or cheapened, so average cost falls; then what follows (lower prices, higher profit, competitiveness).

    Why: Analysis credit comes from developed chains ending in an effect.

  5. For 9-mark Assess or 12-mark Evaluate, follow those cards: weigh likely economies against diseconomies, set-up costs and uncertainty, then judge.

    Why: Top levels need both sides and a supported judgement.

  6. On an LRAC diagram, label the falling section 'economies of scale', the lowest point 'most efficient', and the rising section 'diseconomies of scale'.

    Why: Each correctly placed label earns one mark.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining economies of scale as 'lower costs' without saying average (unit) costs, or without linking to growth.
  • ✗
    Calling skilled labour or infrastructure an internal economy; these are external.
  • ✗
    Listing types of economies without showing how each lowers cost per unit in the case.
  • ✗
    On the LRAC diagram, putting 'most efficient' anywhere other than the lowest point.
  • ✗
    In Assess and Evaluate, ignoring diseconomies of scale, so the answer is one-sided.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Define external economies: falling average costs for firms because the whole industry or area develops.
  2. Pick the case facts: better rail infrastructure, new Hyderabad to Karnataka route, more freight carrying cement, iron ore and steel.
  3. Chain 1: easier, cheaper transport of raw materials, lower input costs, lower average costs for construction firms.
  4. Chain 2: more reliable supplies, fewer delays, more building projects completed, costs spread over more output.
  5. Note that firms anywhere on the network can gain, not just one firm.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

External economies of scale are reductions in a firm's average costs that come from growth of the industry or the area around it, rather than from the firm itself getting bigger. The upgrade of India's railway, including the new line between Hyderabad and Karnataka, is an improvement in infrastructure, a classic source of external economies. Construction firms rely on heavy raw materials such as cement, iron ore and steel. With more freight trains and new routes, these materials can be moved in larger loads and more cheaply than by road, so the cost of getting materials to building sites falls. Because transport is a big part of a construction firm's costs, its average cost per building project falls. Faster and more reliable deliveries also mean fewer days when workers and machines stand idle waiting for materials, so firms can complete more projects with the same staff and equipment, spreading their fixed costs over more output. These benefits are not limited to one firm: any construction firm near the network gains, which is why they are external. Lower average costs could let these firms charge lower prices to win contracts or earn higher profits to invest.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked out of 6 (3 application, 3 analysis). A correct definition of external economies and the infrastructure link show knowledge; using the rail line and named raw materials gives application; chains to cheaper transport, idle-time savings and lower average cost with a consequence give Level 3 analysis.

Assess · June 2023 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define economies and diseconomies of scale using long-run average cost.
  2. Data: 59 million subscribers, more if new programmes go ahead; studios, many channels, DC Comics and CNN; value of $150bn.
  3. Chain: fixed programme and platform costs spread over 59 million -> lower cost per subscriber; shared studios -> technical economies.
  4. Chain: joint advertising -> marketing economies; $150bn size -> cheaper borrowing (financial economies).
  5. Against: communication and control problems, unhappy staff, slow technology integration, may already be at lowest average cost.
  6. Judgement: economies likely; depends on how well managers combine staff and systems.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Economies of scale are falls in long-run average cost (cost per unit) as a firm grows. Diseconomies of scale are rises in average cost when a firm becomes too large.

The merged Warner Bros. Discovery is expected to have 59 million streaming subscribers. Many costs of a streaming service, such as making a programme or running the servers, are mostly fixed: a new series costs about the same however many people watch it. Spreading these costs over 59 million subscribers lowers the cost per subscriber. If the planned new programmes attract more subscribers, average cost falls further.

There are other internal economies too. Technical economies: the two firms can combine their film, animation and television studios and share one streaming platform, so expensive equipment is used more fully and duplicate systems can be closed. Marketing economies: one advertising campaign can promote many popular channels, CNN and DC Comics characters together, so advertising cost per subscriber falls. Financial economies: with an estimated value of $150bn, the firm looks low-risk to banks, so it can borrow at lower interest rates to pay for new programmes. Managerial economies: specialist managers can run each function across all the channels, instead of each firm paying for its own.

However, the merger may also cause diseconomies of scale. Joining two large firms with many channels and studios makes communication slower and control harder, so decisions take longer and mistakes cost more. Staff may worry about job losses and become less motivated, which lowers productivity and raises cost per unit. Combining technology systems takes time, so savings may not appear for several years. If the firm is already close to its lowest average cost, growing further could push costs up.

Overall, the merger is likely to bring economies of scale, especially from spreading programme costs over 59 million subscribers and from cheaper finance. However, this depends on how well managers combine the two firms' staff and systems; if the merger is managed badly, diseconomies could cancel out the savings.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines economies and diseconomies of scale and applies 59 million subscribers and $150bn to developed chains for several internal economies; balances with communication, motivation and time-lag diseconomies; concludes economies are likely but depend on how well the merger is managed.

Evaluate · November 2024 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define economies and diseconomies of scale using long-run average cost.
  2. Data: bought for $44bn; users rose to 530 million by 2023; free publicity from the new name; costly research and development; advertising revenue down 65%; thousands of job losses including managers.
  3. Chain: fixed costs spread over 530 million users; free publicity -> marketing economy; everything app on one platform -> technical economies; size -> financial economies.
  4. Against: R&D cost raises average cost if users do not grow; fewer managers and demotivated staff -> control and communication problems; users may leave over changes.
  5. Counter: job cuts lower wage costs and remaining staff may work harder.
  6. Judgement: some economies likely but diseconomies risk is high; depends on the everything app working and staff morale.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Economies of scale are reductions in long-run average cost as a firm increases its scale of output. Diseconomies of scale are increases in average cost when a firm grows too large.

There are good reasons to expect X to gain economies of scale. Users rose to a new high of 530 million by 2023. Running a social media platform involves large fixed costs, such as servers and software engineers, so spreading them over 530 million users lowers the cost per user. The publicity from the change of name also meant X did not need to pay high advertising costs, so its marketing cost per user fell, which is a form of marketing economy.

The plan for an 'everything app' could bring technical economies. If messaging, payments, ridesharing and food delivery all run on one platform, the same servers, software and user accounts serve every function, so fewer resources are needed than for separate apps. Financial economies are also likely. A firm bought for $44bn is very large, so lenders see it as safer and may offer cheaper loans to fund the advanced technology.

However, there are strong signs of diseconomies. The research and development for the new technology is a significant cost, and if user numbers do not keep growing it will raise average cost rather than lower it. Advertising revenue has fallen by 65%, so X may struggle to pay for the platform; this limits its ability to grow to the scale where savings appear. Thousands of workers, including managers, have lost their jobs. Remaining staff may fear for their own jobs and become demotivated, lowering productivity. Fewer managers coordinating many new functions can cause communication problems and loss of control, which push average cost up. Finally, if users dislike the major changes, they may move to a competitor, shrinking the user base over which costs are spread.

On the other hand, cutting thousands of jobs also cuts wage costs, and the workers who remain might work harder to keep their jobs, which could lower average cost.

Overall, X is likely to gain some economies of scale from its 530 million users and its size, but the risk of diseconomies is high. Whether average costs fall depends mainly on whether the 'everything app' technology works and keeps users, and whether the smaller workforce stays motivated and well managed. In the short run diseconomies may dominate; economies of scale are more likely in the long run if the new platform succeeds.

Why it reaches the top level: Accurate definitions applied to 530 million users, $44bn and the everything app in developed chains for marketing, technical and financial economies; balanced by R&D cost, 65% revenue fall, job losses, control problems and user loss, plus a counterpoint; judgement depends on technology success and workforce motivation.

Assess · November 2020 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

  1. Define internal economies of scale: lower average cost from a firm's own growth (bulk buying, technical, marketing, risk-bearing).
  2. Data: capacity of around 200 million passengers a year, double Atlanta; $11.7bn cost; four terminals, six runways, 29.5 square miles.
  3. Chain: very large purchases of seating and equipment -> bulk-buying discounts; high fixed cost spread over 200 million passengers -> low cost per passenger.
  4. Chain: six runways -> risk-bearing (aircraft moved if a runway fails); shops and cafes share marketing as the world's largest airport.
  5. Against: diseconomies from size (communication, control); passenger numbers are not guaranteed; built in stages so bulk buying may be limited.
  6. Judgement: likely to gain internal economies if it reaches its passenger capacity; depends on demand and how well it is managed.

  1. Define economies of scale (falling average cost as a firm grows) and diseconomies of scale.
  2. Data: Indosat is growing, valued at $2.2bn with more than 60 million customers; Indonesia is Southeast Asia's most populated country.
