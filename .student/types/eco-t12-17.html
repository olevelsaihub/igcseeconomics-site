Knowledge card

Demand for labour: what makes firms hire

Written
  • Demand for labour is the number of workers firms are willing and able to employ at each wage rate.
  • Labour is a derived demand: firms want workers only because consumers want the product the workers make. More demand for the product means more demand for workers.
  • Substitutes for labour, especially machines and technology, can reduce the demand for workers if they do the job more cheaply or efficiently.
  • Productivity matters: if each worker produces more, a worker is more valuable to the firm, which can raise demand for skilled workers, though fewer workers may be needed for the same output.
  • The wage rate: at a higher wage, firms usually want fewer workers (a movement along the demand curve).

Before you begin

  • The law of demand and what a demand curve shows.
  • That labour is a factor of production.
  • What productivity means.
1

The method

  1. Define demand for labour and name the factor in the question (derived demand, substitutes, productivity).

    Why: Knowledge marks need the right factor and its meaning.

  2. For derived demand, chain: demand for the product rises, firms must produce more, so they need more of the workers who make it.

    Why: This is the core mechanism examiners reward.

  3. For machines, chain: machines become cheaper or better, firms switch from workers to machines, so demand for labour falls.

    Why: Shows the substitution effect on hiring.

  4. Apply to the job and figures in the case (woodcutters, call-centre staff, shop workers, a 5% rise).

    Why: Application marks need the case details.

  5. For Assess and Evaluate, follow those cards: weigh the main factor against others (machines still need staff to build and maintain them; productivity; wage costs) and judge.

    Why: Top levels need balance and a supported judgement.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining derived demand as consumers' demand for a product, rather than firms' demand for workers that comes from it.
  • ✗
    Saying machines always cut all jobs, ignoring workers needed to build, program and repair them.
  • ✗
    Mixing up demand for labour (firms) with supply of labour (workers).
  • ✗
    Not using the specific job in the case, e.g. writing about 'workers' instead of woodcutters.
  • ✗
    Treating a wage rise as shifting demand rather than moving along it.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Define derived demand for labour.
  2. Link the product to the worker: wooden furniture needs wood, which woodcutters cut.
  3. Use the data: demand for wooden furniture expected to rise by about 5% over five years.
  4. Chain: furniture makers produce more, need more wood, timber firms hire more woodcutters.
  5. Note the size of the effect: a modest rise spread over five years suggests a gradual increase in hiring.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Demand for labour is the number of workers that firms want to employ. It is a derived demand, which means firms only want workers because people want the goods or services those workers help to produce. Woodcutters cut down the trees that provide the wood used to make wooden furniture, so their jobs depend on how much wooden furniture consumers buy. Because tastes have changed, the demand for wooden furniture is expected to rise by about 5% over the next five years. To meet this, furniture makers will want to produce more, so they will need to buy more wood. Timber firms will then need to cut down more trees, which means they will want to employ more woodcutters, or offer existing woodcutters more hours. So the rise in demand for furniture is likely to increase the demand for woodcutters. As the increase is only about 5% and spread over five years, the rise in demand for woodcutters is likely to be gradual rather than sudden, and it could be smaller if timber firms use more cutting machines instead of extra workers.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked out of 6 (3 application, 3 analysis). Defining derived demand shows knowledge; linking woodcutters to wooden furniture and using the 5% figure applies it; the developed chain from furniture demand to wood to hiring, with a note on size, gives Level 3 analysis.

Assess · June 2022 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define demand for labour and its three influences: derived demand, productivity, substitutes.
  2. For: online sales reached more than 20% of retail in 2019, so fewer shop workers are needed.
  3. For: at least 10% of shops empty for five years; machines now send online orders, replacing workers.
  4. Against: people are needed to build, program and repair machines, and to pack and deliver online orders.
  5. Against: machines raise productivity and can lower prices, which may increase total sales and jobs.
  6. Judge: reduces demand for some jobs, not all; depends on machine costs versus wages and new jobs created.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The demand for labour is the number of workers that firms are willing and able to employ at a given wage rate. It depends on the demand for the product the workers make (derived demand), on workers' productivity, and on the availability and cost of substitutes such as machines.

In UK retail, machines are clearly reducing the demand for some labour. Technology has made it easier for consumers to buy online, and online sales reached more than 20% of total retail sales in 2019. As shoppers move online, fewer people visit town-centre shops, so shops sell less and need fewer shop assistants. At least 10% of all shops have been empty for the last five years, which shows these jobs disappearing. In warehouses, machines now send out online orders, doing work that employees used to do. If a machine can do a task more cheaply or quickly than a worker, a profit-maximising firm will substitute capital for labour, so demand for these workers falls.

However, machines do not reduce the demand for all labour. Someone has to design, build, program and repair the machines, so demand for engineers and technicians rises. The growth of online shopping also creates new jobs: orders still have to be packed, checked and delivered, so demand for warehouse and delivery workers may increase. Machines can also make workers more productive, which lowers costs and prices; if this raises sales, firms may need more workers overall. Some tasks, such as dealing with customer problems, may still need people.

Overall, the availability of substitutes such as machines is likely to reduce the demand for certain types of labour, especially shop workers in town centres, but it will not always reduce the total demand for labour. The final effect depends on how cheap machines are compared with wages, how far tasks can be automated, and how many new jobs are created in technology, warehouses and delivery as online sales keep growing.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines demand for labour and its determinants (AO2); chains online growth over 20% and 10% empty shops to fewer shop jobs and machine substitution (AO3); counters with jobs building and maintaining machines, delivery work and productivity gains, ending in a judgement that depends on relative costs and new job creation (AO4).

Evaluate · June 2023 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define demand for labour and derived demand: workers are wanted to produce goods people buy.
  2. For: festivals and football matches raise demand for events, so more security staff are hired.
  3. For: holidays and celebrations raise grocery sales, so supermarkets need more staff; demand falls back afterwards.
  4. Against: robots replacing waiters, automated customer services and machinery mean higher demand may be met by machines.
  5. Against: higher productivity lets the same workers meet more demand; varies by industry (few substitutes for security).
  6. Judge: derived demand matters a great deal; depends on how easily tasks can be automated and productivity.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The demand for labour is the number of workers that firms are willing and able to employ at a given wage rate. It is a derived demand, because firms do not want workers for their own sake; they want them to produce goods and services that consumers buy. So when demand for the product rises, demand for the workers who make or sell it also rises. Other factors also affect labour demand, such as how productive the workers are and whether machines could do the work instead.

The data gives clear examples of derived demand. During music festivals and sporting events, such as football matches, more fans attend, so stadiums need more security staff to keep people safe. More security staff are employed at these times than at others, because demand for the event has risen. In the same way, ahead of special holidays and national celebrations people buy more groceries, so supermarkets need more employees to sell them. When the events end or the holiday passes, demand for the product falls and so does the demand for these workers. This shows derived demand has a strong and direct effect: the number of workers needed rises and falls with sales.

However, derived demand is not the only factor. Technological advances mean machines are increasingly used where they raise productivity. Waiters are being replaced by robots in restaurants, online customer issues are handled by automated services, and manufacturing uses machinery. In these industries, even if demand for the product rises, firms may meet it with machines rather than extra workers, so labour demand may not rise at all. Higher productivity has a similar effect: if each worker can serve more customers, the firm can meet higher demand without hiring more staff, and may even need fewer workers to keep costs down.

The importance of derived demand also differs between jobs. For security at festivals and football matches, there are few substitutes for people, so the number of staff follows demand for the event closely. In restaurants, customer services and manufacturing, machines are easier to use, so substitution matters more. Also, machines still need people to design, build and maintain them, so some new labour demand is created.

In conclusion, derived demand affects the demand for labour to a large extent, because firms only hire workers to meet demand for their products, as the security and supermarket examples show. However, the size of the effect depends on how easily machines can replace workers and on productivity. Where tasks can be automated, technology increasingly decides how many workers are needed; where they cannot, derived demand remains the main factor.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines labour demand and derived demand (AO2); develops chains from events and holidays to more security and supermarket staff and back (AO3); weighs robots, automated services and productivity, compares industries, and reaches a conclusion that depends on how easily tasks can be automated (AO4).
