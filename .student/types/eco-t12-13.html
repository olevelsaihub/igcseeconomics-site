Knowledge card

Monopoly: features, barriers to entry, pros and cons

Written
  • A monopoly is a market with one dominant seller (one firm supplies all or nearly all of the product).
  • Features: one dominant firm, a unique product (no close substitute), high barriers to entry, and the firm is a price-maker (it can set the market price).
  • Barriers to entry are obstacles that make it hard for new firms to enter a market: high start-up costs, patents and legal rights, expensive technology, strong branding and marketing, and the low costs that come from economies of scale.
  • A patent is a legal right to be the only producer of a newly designed product for a period, so rivals cannot copy it.
  • Disadvantages for consumers: higher prices, little or no choice, and possibly poorer quality or slower service because the firm has no rivals pushing it.
  • Possible advantages: economies of scale can lower average costs (and possibly prices); big profits can pay for research and innovation; no wasteful duplication (e.g. one rail network).

Before you begin

  • What a market and competition are.
  • What economies of scale are.
  • The idea of substitutes.
1

The method

  1. Identify or define the idea asked for: monopoly, price-maker, unique product, patent, or barrier to entry.

    Why: Definition and feature items reward the precise meaning.

  2. For a barrier, explain how it keeps rivals out: what a new firm would need and why it cannot get it.

    Why: Describe and Explain marks need the mechanism, not just the name.

  3. For effects, trace no competition, so the firm can raise price or reduce quality, so consumers pay more or get less choice.

    Why: Credit comes from linking monopoly power to an outcome for the named group.

  4. Balance with possible benefits: economies of scale, profit funding innovation, no duplicated infrastructure.

    Why: Questions often ask if a monopoly is 'always bad', so both sides are needed.

  5. For 9-mark Assess and 12-mark Evaluate, follow those cards: use case data (market share, prices, service limits) and finish with a supported judgement.

    Why: The top level needs application, balance and a justified conclusion.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining monopoly as 'one firm' only, without the idea of dominating the market supply.
  • ✗
    Naming a barrier to entry without explaining why it stops new firms.
  • ✗
    Assuming a monopoly is always bad and missing economies of scale or innovation benefits.
  • ✗
    Confusing price-maker (sets price) with price-taker.
  • ✗
    Mixing up monopoly (one dominant firm) with oligopoly (a few large firms).

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

What a complete response includes

Short items reward precise definitions and features. Describe and Explain reward a feature or barrier developed into how it blocks rivals or affects buyers. Long answers reward case-based costs and benefits of monopoly power and a reasoned judgement.

Worked explanation

  1. Name the barrier: high start-up costs.
  2. Develop: explain why it stops new firms, they must raise a very large sum before they can sell anything, and most cannot.
  3. Finish with the outcome: the existing firm stays dominant.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Setting up in the industry may require huge spending on factories, equipment or networks before any sales are made. Most new firms cannot borrow or save that much money, and lenders may see the investment as too risky, so they are unable to enter and the existing firm keeps its dominant position.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for identifying that entry needs a very large amount of money, and 1 mark for developing why this keeps rivals out (they cannot raise or borrow the funds, so the dominant firm stays alone).

Assess · June 2023 · Paper 1 · Q2(h)

Answer plan

  1. Define monopoly; PKP runs trains and the rail network in Poland for passengers and freight.
  2. Data: over 18,510 km of track; its stated aims are the state's transport needs and consumers' interests.
  3. Chain: huge fixed track costs spread by one operator -> economies of scale, no duplication -> lower fares and investment in service.
  4. Against: price maker with little competition -> higher fares, less choice of routes, less incentive to be efficient or innovate; freight costs passed on.
  5. Judgement: benefits consumers to some extent as a natural monopoly; depends on PKP following its consumer aims and on regulation.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A monopoly is a market with one dominant seller. PKP is the main firm running both the trains and the rail network in Poland, so passengers and freight firms have little choice.

A monopoly like PKP can benefit consumers. Running over 18,510 km of track involves huge fixed costs, such as building and maintaining the track and signals. With one firm, these costs are spread over all passenger and freight journeys, so it gains economies of scale and lower average costs. If two firms each built their own track, resources would be wasted on duplication; this is why rail is often a natural monopoly. Lower costs could be passed on as lower fares. PKP says it aims to serve the state's transport needs and its consumers' interests, not only profit, so it may keep fares low and run services on less popular routes that a profit-driven firm would cut. Its profits or savings could also be invested in faster, more reliable trains.

However, a monopoly may harm consumers. With few competitors, PKP is a price maker and could charge high fares, so consumers pay more than in a competitive market. Without rivals, it has less incentive to keep costs down or improve quality, so trains may be old, slow or unreliable. Consumers also have less choice of routes and times. Freight firms that rely on the network may face higher costs, which they pass on to consumers as higher prices for goods.

Overall, PKP is likely to benefit consumers to some extent, because rail track is costly and should not be duplicated, so one large operator can be the cheapest way to provide the service. Whether consumers benefit depends mainly on PKP's aims and how it is controlled: if it truly follows its aim of serving consumers and the state, or is regulated to limit fares, the benefits outweigh the costs; if it acts mainly to maximise profit, consumers are likely to lose.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines monopoly and applies PKP's control of 18,510 km of track and its stated aims in chains on economies of scale, avoided duplication and service; balances with price-maker power, inefficiency, lack of choice and freight costs; judgement depends on PKP's aims and regulation.

Evaluate · June 2022 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define monopoly; KCOM is the only telecoms provider in Kingston upon Hull and surrounding areas.
  2. Data against consumers: packages more expensive than the rest of the country, low download limits, fibre not available everywhere.
  3. Chain: no competition -> price maker -> higher prices and lower quality limits -> less consumer choice; possible inefficiency.
  4. Data for consumers: speeds up to 250 Mb, discounted bundles of phone and mobile, high-quality TV packages coming; no duplication of networks and economies of scale.
  5. Judgement: not always bad but mostly bad on price here; depends on owners' objectives and regulation or new entry.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A monopoly is a market with one dominant seller. KCOM is the sole telecoms provider for Kingston upon Hull and its surrounding towns and villages, so households have few broadband options.

The data show several ways in which this monopoly harms consumers. Because consumers cannot switch, KCOM is a price maker: its packages are more expensive than those in the rest of the country. Consumers pay more and also receive less, because the packages have low download limits. Some households may therefore struggle to afford good internet access. KCOM also faces little pressure to invest everywhere: its fastest fibre optic packages are not available across the whole region, so some consumers get a slower service at a high price. Without competitors, a monopoly can also become inefficient, letting its costs rise because it knows customers cannot leave, and these costs are passed on through prices.

However, a monopoly is not always bad for consumers. KCOM's fibre optic broadband can reach up to 250 Mb, which is a high-quality product. As the only network, it avoids wasteful duplication of cables, and serving the whole area may give it economies of scale, so its average costs can be lower than if several firms competed. It offers home phone and mobile deals at a discounted price when bought together, which saves consumers money, and it will soon offer high-quality television packages, showing some innovation. A monopoly's secure profits can fund this kind of investment, which small competing firms might not afford.

Weighing these points, KCOM seems mostly bad for consumers on price and choice, because its packages cost more than elsewhere and have low download limits. Yet it is not always bad, since it offers fast speeds in some areas, bundle discounts and new services. Whether a monopoly is good or bad depends on its owners' objectives and on regulation. If KCOM uses its cost savings and profits to bring fast broadband to everyone and to keep prices fair, consumers gain; if it uses its power to keep prices high and limits low, they lose. Government regulation of prices, or helping new firms to enter the market, could reduce the harm. So a monopoly such as KCOM is not always bad, but without competition or regulation it is likely to be bad for consumers in this case.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines monopoly and applies KCOM's sole-provider status, higher prices, low download limits, patchy fibre and 250 Mb speeds in developed chains on both sides, including economies of scale, bundles and innovation; judgement that it is not always bad depends on owners' objectives and regulation.

Evaluate · June 2019 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

  1. Define monopoly; Google has 94.33% of the South American search market, with Bing 1.30% and others 4.37%; consumers use it free while firms pay to advertise.
  2. Chain: huge market share -> adverts reach far more potential customers -> higher sales -> firms recover advertising costs.
  3. Chain: monopoly profits fund research and better search tools; economies of scale may lower advertising prices; need to stay ahead keeps quality up.
  4. Against: lack of competition lets Google charge advertisers high prices; little alternative choice for firms; complacency may reduce service quality.
  5. Judgement: firms benefit to a large extent through reach; depends on Google's pricing and whether rivals such as Bing grow to give advertisers choice.
