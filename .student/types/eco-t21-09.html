Effects of inflation and deflation, including menu and shoe leather costs

Knowledge card

Effects of inflation and deflation, including menu and shoe leather costs

Written
  • Purchasing power is how much a sum of money can buy. Inflation reduces it: the same money buys fewer goods and services.
  • Real wages compare wages with prices. If prices rise faster than wages, real wages fall and living standards drop; if wages rise faster than prices, people can buy more.
  • Menu costs are the costs to firms of changing their listed prices, such as reprinting brochures, menus and price labels or updating websites. Higher inflation means more frequent changes, so bigger menu costs.
  • Shoe leather costs are the time and effort spent searching for the lowest prices (or making extra trips to banks) when prices are rising fast.
  • High or unstable inflation creates uncertainty: consumers and firms find it hard to plan, confidence falls, spending, borrowing and investment may fall, and people may save more as a precaution.
  • Deflation (falling prices) can also hurt confidence: people delay buying because they expect lower prices later, so demand falls.
  • Low and stable inflation (often a target near 2-3%) helps planning and keeps a country's exports price-competitive against other countries.

  • The meaning of inflation and deflation
  • What wages and living standards are

The method

  1. Start with what happens to prices and purchasing power, using the inflation rate given.

    Why: Almost every chain on this idea begins with money buying less (or more).

  2. If a named cost is asked, define it in your own words: menu costs for firms repricing, shoe leather costs for buyers searching.

    Why: A clear meaning is the first mark and avoids mixing the two up.

  3. Develop the knock-on effect: real wages and living standards, confidence and uncertainty, saving and borrowing, investment, or how often prices must change.

    Why: Development marks need the consequence, not just the cause.

  4. Apply each link to the data: the inflation rate, the goods named, the type of firm or consumer.

    Why: AO3 marks need the figures and context used, not just mentioned.

  5. For a 6-mark Analyse, follow the Analyse card: two or three linked chains with data. For a 9-mark Assess, follow the Assess card and add limits such as wages rising with prices.

    Why: Higher levels need developed, applied chains and, for Assess, balance and a judgement.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Swapping the two named costs: menu costs fall on firms changing prices; shoe leather costs fall on buyers searching for cheaper deals.
  • ✗
    Saying inflation always lowers living standards without comparing price rises with wage rises.
  • ✗
    Writing about inflation in general without using the given rate (for example 10.4%) in the chain.
  • ✗
    Stopping at 'confidence falls' without saying what consumers or firms then do (borrow less, save more, delay investment).
  • ✗
    Treating deflation as good news for everyone and missing that delayed spending can reduce demand and confidence.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the data: Germany's annual inflation reached a record 10.4% in October 2022 and is expected to stay high for two more years.
  2. Define consumer confidence: how positive consumers feel about the economy and their own finances.
  3. Chain 1: 10.4% inflation cuts purchasing power; unless wages keep up, people can buy less and living standards fall, so confidence drops.
  4. Chain 2: falling confidence makes consumers cautious: they borrow less and save more to protect themselves.
  5. Chain 3: high inflation expected for two years creates uncertainty, so planning big purchases is hard and confidence is weakened further.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Consumer confidence is how optimistic consumers feel about the economy and their own finances, which affects how willing they are to spend and borrow. With inflation in Germany at a record 10.4%, prices in Germany are rising quickly, at a record 10.4% a year are rising quickly. This reduces purchasing power: unless wages rise by a similar amount, each euro buys fewer goods and services than before. Many German households will find they can afford less, so their living standards fall and they feel less positive about the future. Consumer confidence therefore falls.

Lower confidence changes how people behave. Consumers who are worried about rising bills become more cautious. They are less willing to take out loans for items like cars or furniture, because they are unsure they will be able to repay them, and they may save more to build a safety net against further price rises. This means less spending in the German economy.

Inflation is also expected to stay high for another two years. This makes it hard for households to plan ahead, because they do not know what prices will be next year or whether their pay will keep up. This uncertainty adds to the fall in confidence, especially for big purchases planned over several months. Overall, high inflation of 10.4% is likely to reduce consumer confidence in Germany, leading to lower borrowing and spending and higher precautionary saving.

How the marks are earned

Levels marked out of 6 (3 AO2, 3 AO3). AO2 for defining confidence and developing purchasing power, caution, saving and uncertainty chains; AO3 for using the 10.4% rate and the two-year forecast to drive each chain. Developed, applied chains reach Level 3 (5-6).

Grade 9 long answers

Assess · June 2019 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define inflation and purchasing power.
  2. Data: annual inflation rose to 11.9% in October 2017; transport prices up 16.79% and food up 12.74%; Figure 3 shows the rate changing over time.
  3. Chain: necessities rising faster than average -> real incomes fall if wages lag -> poorer households and savers hit hardest; shoe leather and menu costs rise.
  4. Chain: higher transport costs -> cost-push inflation; uncertainty -> lower investment; exports less competitive.
  5. Against: wages may keep up; demand-pull inflation may lower unemployment; borrowers gain; the rate may fall again.
  6. Judgement: significant negative impact; depends on wage growth and how long inflation stays high.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general level of prices, which reduces the purchasing power of money. In October 2017, Turkey's annual inflation rate rose to 11.9%, which is high.

High inflation harms consumers. Transport prices rose by 16.79% and food by 12.74%, both faster than the 11.9% average. Food and transport are necessities, so households cannot easily cut back, and poorer households, who spend a larger share of their income on them, are hit hardest. If wages rise more slowly than prices, real incomes fall, so people can buy fewer goods and living standards fall. People on fixed incomes, such as pensioners, and savers, whose savings lose value, also lose out. Shoe leather costs rise as consumers spend time shopping around for the best deals, and firms face menu costs from changing prices often.

High inflation also harms firms and the wider economy. Rising transport costs push up firms' costs of production, which may lead to further price rises (cost-push inflation). Uncertainty about future prices makes planning hard, so business confidence and investment may fall, slowing economic growth. If Turkey's inflation is higher than in other countries, its exports become less price competitive, so exports may fall and the current account may worsen.

However, the impact may be less severe in some cases. If wages rise as fast as prices, workers' real incomes are protected. If the inflation is demand-pull, caused by strong spending, firms may hire more workers, so unemployment may fall. Borrowers gain because the real value of their debts falls. Figure 3 shows that the inflation rate has changed over time, so if it falls again soon, the damage may be short-lived.

Overall, inflation of 11.9% is likely to have a significant negative impact on Turkey, especially on poorer households, because the biggest price rises are in food and transport. The size of the impact depends on whether wages keep up with prices and on how long the high inflation lasts; if it is brought down quickly, the long-term damage will be limited.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines inflation and applies 11.9%, 16.79% and 12.74% in developed chains on real incomes, necessities, shoe leather and menu costs, investment and competitiveness; balances with wage growth, demand-pull employment, borrowers and Figure 3's changing rate; judgement depends on wages and duration.
