Knowledge card

Rising and falling currencies: effects on trade and the current account

Written
  • In a floating system (the market sets the rate, no government intervention): a rise in the exchange rate is an appreciation and a fall is a depreciation.
  • In a fixed system (the government sets the rate): an official increase is a revaluation and an official decrease is a devaluation.
  • Reading rates: if £1 = $1.40 changes to £1 = $1.30, each pound buys fewer dollars, so the pound has fallen (depreciated).
  • A fall in the currency (depreciation or devaluation): exports become cheaper for foreign buyers and imports become dearer at home, so exports tend to rise and imports to fall. Governments devalue mainly to boost demand for exports.
  • A rise in the currency (appreciation or revaluation): exports become dearer abroad and imports cheaper, so exports tend to fall and imports to rise.
  • Current account: the part of the balance of payments that records trade in goods and services (exports minus imports) and some income flows. A weaker currency tends to improve it; a stronger one tends to worsen it.
  • Knock-on effects: a weaker currency can raise growth and jobs but also inflation (dearer imports); a stronger one can cut inflation but hurt exporters. The size of the effect depends on price elasticity of demand (how much buyers respond to a price change), how big and long-lasting the change is, and non-price factors such as quality.

The method

  1. Name the change: floating rise is appreciation, floating fall is depreciation, government rise is revaluation, government fall is devaluation.

    Why: Many marks, especially in multiple choice and definitions, rest on the correct term.

  2. If rates are given, check whether one unit of the home currency now buys more or less foreign currency.

    Why: Buying less means it has fallen, so exports from that country are cheaper.

  3. State the price effect: a weaker currency makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home (reverse for a stronger one).

    Why: This is the core of every chain.

  4. Follow through to quantities: demand for exports and imports changes, so net exports and the current account move.

    Why: The development mark needs the link from prices to trade volumes.

  5. Add the consequence and context: current account surplus or deficit, growth or jobs in the named country.

    Why: Context earns the third Explain mark and AO3 in longer answers.

  6. For a 9-mark Assess, follow the Assess card: argue the expected effect, then judge it depends on price elasticity, the size and length of the change, and quality.

    Why: AO4 needs conditional judgement.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Mixing up depreciation (market-driven) with devaluation (government-set), or appreciation with revaluation.
  • ✗
    Misreading rate pairs: thinking £1 = $1.30 after £1 = $1.40 means the pound has risen.
  • ✗
    Saying a weaker currency makes exports dearer or imports cheaper.
  • ✗
    Jumping straight to 'the current account improves' without the price and volume steps.
  • ✗
    In the 9-mark answer, ignoring what the effect depends on, such as price elasticity or quality.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Data: Germany had a current account surplus of €268bn in 2018, and the euro had weakened.
  2. Reason: German exports become cheaper for foreign buyers.
  3. Develop: if foreign buyers respond to lower prices, they buy more German goods.
  4. Consequence: export revenue rises relative to imports, so net exports and the current account improve.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

When the euro falls in value, foreign buyers need less of their own currency to buy German goods, so German exports become cheaper abroad. If foreign consumers respond to these lower prices, demand for German exports rises. Exports therefore increase relative to imports, so Germany's net exports rise and its current account surplus, already €268bn in 2018, becomes even larger.

How the marks are earned

1 mark: the reason (German exports become cheaper abroad, raising demand for them). 1 mark: development (foreign buyers respond to the lower prices). 1 mark: context (higher net exports enlarge Germany's €268bn current account surplus).

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Assess · January 2023 · Paper 2 · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define depreciation in a floating exchange rate system.
  2. Data: in 2020, 2% of US exports went to Australia (machinery, vehicles, medical instruments, aircraft); the US dollar depreciated against the Australian dollar.
  3. Chain: weaker US dollar -> fewer Australian dollars needed to buy US goods -> US exports more price competitive -> quantity demanded rises -> more US output, jobs and a better trade balance with Australia.
  4. Against: 2% share means small overall effect; high-value specialised goods may have inelastic PED; long-term contracts; size and duration of the depreciation; quality and Australian incomes matter.
  5. Judgement: demand likely rises but only slightly; depends on PED and on how large and lasting the depreciation is.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A depreciation is a fall in the value of a currency against another currency in a floating exchange rate system. In 2020 the US dollar depreciated against the Australian dollar.

After the depreciation, each Australian dollar buys more US dollars. This means US goods cost fewer Australian dollars, so US exports become cheaper for Australian buyers. For example, an Australian airline buying a US aircraft priced in US dollars would need fewer Australian dollars to pay for it. Because US exports are now more price competitive than goods from other countries, the quantity demanded of US machinery, vehicles, medical instruments and aircraft by Australia should rise. Higher exports increase revenue for US firms, so they may raise output and hire more workers. If exports to Australia grow faster than imports from Australia, the US trade balance with Australia improves.

However, the impact may be limited. Only 2% of US exports went to Australia in 2020, so even a large rise in demand would have a small effect on total US exports. The effect also depends on the price elasticity of demand (PED) for US exports. Machinery, medical instruments and aircraft are specialised, high-value goods, often bought for quality, reliability and after-sales service rather than price, so demand may be price inelastic and change little. Some may be bought under long-term contracts agreed before the depreciation. The size and length of the depreciation also matter: a small or short-lived fall in the dollar may not change buying decisions. Other factors, such as Australian incomes, competition from other countries and the quality of US goods, also affect demand.

Overall, the depreciation is likely to increase demand for US exports to Australia, but only by a small amount. The extent depends mainly on how price elastic Australian demand for these high-value goods is and on how large and lasting the depreciation is; if demand is inelastic, quality and Australian incomes will matter more than the exchange rate.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines depreciation and applies the 2020 fall against the Australian dollar, the 2% export share and the named goods in a developed price-competitiveness chain to demand, output and trade balance; balances with PED, contracts, size and duration and non-price factors; judgement depends on PED and depreciation size.
