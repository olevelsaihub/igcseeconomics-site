Worked explanation

  1. Read the data: growth of 6.2% a year; oil and gas over 60% of GDP; agriculture 10% of GDP but half the workforce; GDP per capita rising over 2009 to 2018 to above $7,000.
  2. Benefit 1: more output needs more workers, especially in oil and gas, so unemployment falls.
  3. Benefit 2: rising GDP per capita means higher average incomes, more spending and better living standards; poverty can fall, which matters as many workers are in low-output farming.
  4. Cost 1: very fast growth can overheat the economy and cause inflation.
  5. Cost 2: dependence on oil and gas brings pollution and uses up finite resources; gains may go to a few, so inequality rises.
  6. Judge: the benefits are real but depend on whether the gains reach farm workers and whether growth is sustainable.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Economic growth is a rise in a country's output over time. Turkmenistan's GDP has been growing by 6.2% a year, which brings several benefits. First, to produce more, firms need more workers. Oil and natural gas extraction makes up more than 60% of GDP, so as this sector expands it creates jobs, both directly and in firms that supply it, and unemployment falls. Second, Figure 4 shows GDP per capita rising over the period to above $7,000, so the average person has more income. With more income, households can buy more goods and services, such as better food and housing, so living standards rise. Growth can also reduce poverty. Half the labour force works in agriculture, which produces only 10% of GDP, so many people have low incomes; new jobs and higher tax revenue for public services could help lift them out of poverty.

However, growth also has costs. If output grows faster than the economy can cope with, demand may run ahead of supply and the economy may overheat, pushing up prices and wages and causing inflation that cuts the real value of people's incomes. Growth based on oil and gas also causes pollution and uses up finite resources that cannot be replaced, so future generations may lose out and Turkmenistan may face problems when reserves run low. Finally, the gains may be shared unequally. Oil and gas employ relatively few people, so profits may go mainly to a small group, while the half of the workforce in agriculture gains little and inequality widens.

Overall, growth of 6.2% is likely to benefit Turkmenistan through more jobs and higher average incomes, as the rising GDP per capita suggests. But the size of the benefit depends on whether the gains spread to the many agricultural workers and whether the country can avoid inflation and reduce its reliance on finite oil and gas. If growth is managed and shared, the benefits are likely to outweigh the costs.

How the marks are earned

Levels marked out of 9. AO2 for defining growth and developing job and living-standard benefits; AO3 for using 6.2%, the 60% oil and gas share, the farm workforce and rising GDP per capita; AO4 for overheating, pollution, depletion and inequality, ending with a judgement on what the benefits depend on.

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Assess · June 2021 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define economic growth; data: annual growth of 6.2%; GDP per capita rises (lowest $4 129.5, highest $7 467.9).
  2. For: higher income per head -> more goods and services -> higher living standards.
  3. For: more output -> more jobs, especially linked to oil and gas (over 60% of GDP) -> multiplier; more tax revenue for public services.
  4. Against: agriculture employs 50% of workers but gives only 10% of GDP, so gains may be uneven; finite oil and gas; pollution; risk of inflation from fast growth.
  5. Judgement: real benefits; depends on spreading gains to farm workers and diversifying the economy.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Economic growth is an increase in the real output of an economy over time, usually measured by real GDP. Turkmenistan has had annual GDP growth rates of 6.2%, and Figure 4 shows GDP per capita rising over the period, from $4 129.5 at its lowest to $7 467.9 at its highest.

The first benefit is higher incomes and living standards. As output grows faster than the population, average income per person rises, so households can buy more goods and services, such as better food, housing and health care. Second, firms need more workers to produce more output, so unemployment falls. Oil and natural gas extraction accounts for more than 60% of GDP, so growth in this sector creates jobs directly, and spending by its workers and suppliers creates more jobs through the multiplier effect. Third, growth raises tax revenue without higher tax rates, because incomes, spending and profits all increase. The government can spend this on schools, hospitals and infrastructure, or on agriculture, which employs 50% of the labour force but produces only 10% of GDP. This shows that farm workers have low productivity and low incomes, so investment in farming could help reduce poverty.

However, the benefits may be limited. The growth comes mainly from oil and gas, which are likely to employ far fewer people than farming. Since half the labour force works in agriculture, many people may see little rise in income, so inequality may increase. Oil and gas are finite resources, so growth based on extracting them may not be sustainable, and the economy depends heavily on world energy prices. Extraction also causes pollution. Fast growth of 6.2% a year could also cause demand-pull inflation if spending rises faster than the economy's capacity to produce.

Overall, growth is likely to bring real benefits to Turkmenistan, as the rise in GDP per capita shows. However, how widely they are felt depends on whether the government uses oil and gas revenue to raise productivity in agriculture and to diversify the economy, so the gains reach the 50% of workers in farming and last after the resources run out.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines growth, applies the 6.2% rate, GDP per capita range, 60% oil and gas share and the 10% versus 50% agriculture contrast in chains (incomes, jobs, multiplier, tax), balances with inequality, finite resources, pollution and inflation, and gives a conditional judgement.
