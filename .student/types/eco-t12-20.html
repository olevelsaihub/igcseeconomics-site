Knowledge card

Trade unions: how they affect pay and jobs

Written
  • A trade union is an organisation of workers that acts to protect and improve its members' interests.
  • Unions aim to raise pay, improve working conditions (hours, health and safety), protect jobs and workers' rights, and give legal help.
  • Collective bargaining: the union negotiates with employers on behalf of all members, which gives workers more power than negotiating alone.
  • Unions can put pressure on employers with industrial action such as strikes; they may also lobby the government for laws that help workers.
  • Possible downsides: higher wages raise firms' costs, so firms may employ fewer workers, raise prices or replace workers with machines; strikes cause disruption.
  • Union power is greater when many workers are members, when the union is large and well established, and when the firm can pass costs on to customers.

Before you begin

  • What a wage rate is.
  • How firms' costs affect their prices and hiring.
  • The labour market diagram (demand and supply of labour).
1

The method

  1. Define a trade union: an organisation of workers that protects members' interests.

    Why: Definition marks need both 'organisation of workers' and 'protects interests'.

  2. Explain the mechanism: collective bargaining or strike threat gives workers power to win higher pay or better conditions.

    Why: Analysis marks reward how the union achieves its aims.

  3. Use the case: union name, membership numbers, the pay rise won, strikes held.

    Why: Application marks need the data.

  4. Give the other side: higher wages raise costs, which may lead to fewer jobs, higher prices or automation; or the union may be weak if few workers join.

    Why: The Assess card needs a counter-argument.

  5. Follow the Assess card to judge: what does the outcome depend on (membership, productivity gains, ability to pass on costs)?

    Why: A supported judgement is needed for Level 3.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining a trade union as a group that 'helps the economy' or 'helps firms' rather than one that protects workers.
  • ✗
    Assuming unions always win higher pay with no cost to jobs or prices.
  • ✗
    Not using the union's numbers in the case (members, pay rise, size).
  • ✗
    Writing only about strikes and forgetting negotiation and better conditions.
  • ✗
    Ending a 9-mark Assess without a judgement.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Define trade union and its aims.
  2. Use the data: Usdaw negotiated a rise from £9.20 to at least £10 per hour for 110,000 Morrisons workers; most are members.
  3. Benefit chain for employees: higher pay, better living standards, more motivation.
  4. Cost chain for Morrisons: wage bill rises, so it may cut staff or hours, or raise prices.
  5. Counter-balance: motivation and productivity may rise; a leading supermarket may pass costs on. Judge.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A trade union is an organisation of workers that acts to protect and improve its members' pay, conditions and rights. Most of Morrisons' workers belong to the union Usdaw, which gave the union strong bargaining power. By negotiating on behalf of all of them, Usdaw won a pay rise from £9.20 to at least £10 per hour for 110,000 workers, an increase of about 80p an hour. For employees this is a clear benefit: their income and living standards rise, and they may feel more valued and motivated. For Morrisons, however, the rise increases its wage bill considerably because it applies to such a large workforce. To protect its profits, Morrisons might employ fewer workers, cut hours, or replace some staff with self-service tills, so some employees could lose out. On the other hand, the impact on Morrisons may be smaller than it looks. Better-paid workers may work harder and stay longer, raising productivity and cutting the cost of recruiting and training new staff. Being a leading supermarket, Morrisons may also pass some of the cost on through slightly higher prices, especially for groceries that customers keep buying even when prices rise. Overall, union activity has clearly benefited Morrisons' employees through higher pay. The impact on Morrisons depends on whether productivity rises enough, and whether it can raise prices without losing customers to rivals; if not, the higher costs could lead to fewer jobs, which would reduce the benefit to employees.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked out of 9 (3 application, 3 analysis, 3 evaluation). Definition and the pay figures give application; chains on higher income and higher costs leading to job cuts give analysis; productivity and price pass-through as counter-points plus an 'it depends' judgement give Level 3.

Calculation check

  • 10-9.20 = 0.8
  • round((10-9.20) ÷ 9.20 × 100, 1) = 8.7

Grade 9 long answers

Assess · November 2024 · Paper 1 · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define trade union and collective bargaining power.
  2. For: strikes by Verdi disrupted railways and airports, pressuring employers to settle.
  3. For: 2.5 million transport workers won a 5.5% pay rise in April 2023.
  4. Against: Verdi wanted 10.5%, so it achieved only part of its claim.
  5. Against: employers resist if they cannot pass costs to passengers; higher wages may cut jobs; members may leave.
  6. Judge: fairly effective; depends on member support for strikes, disruption caused and employers' ability to pay.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A trade union is an organisation of workers that bargains with employers on their behalf about pay and working conditions. This is called collective bargaining. Because workers negotiate as one group, they have more bargaining power than each worker alone.

The data shows unions can raise wages. In April 2023 a deal gave 2.5 million German transport workers a 5.5% pay rise. Verdi, the second largest union in Germany, speaks for many workers, and employers cannot easily ignore it. Its main weapon was strike action, which caused major disruption on railways and at airports. A strike stops services, so transport firms lose revenue and customers. To end these losses, employers have an incentive to agree to higher pay. Without the union, an individual worker asking for a rise could simply be replaced, so collective action gave workers a pay rise they might not have won alone. A settled deal can also reduce future disruption and may improve motivation and productivity.

However, the union was only partly effective. Verdi argued for a 10.5% increase but workers received 5.5%, roughly half of what was demanded. Employers also have power: if transport firms cannot pass higher wage costs on to passengers through higher fares, they must accept lower profits, so they resist large rises. Higher wages can also lead firms to employ fewer workers in future, so some of the gain in pay may be lost through fewer jobs. Strikes also cost workers, because they may lose pay while on strike, and they can lose public support if passengers are badly affected. If members see that the union cannot win its full demand, some may decide the membership fee is not worth paying, which weakens the union next time.

Overall, trade unions are fairly effective at increasing wages, as the 5.5% rise for 2.5 million workers shows, but they rarely achieve everything they ask for. Their success depends on how many members support strike action, how much disruption a strike causes, and whether employers can afford higher wages or pass the cost on to customers.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines trade union and collective bargaining (AO2); chains strike action to disruption, lost revenue and pressure to settle, using 2.5 million workers and the 5.5% rise (AO3); counters with the 10.5% claim, employer costs, job losses and member support, ending in a judgement with clear conditions (AO4).

Assess · June 2019 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q3(f)

  1. Define a trade union and collective bargaining for higher wages and better conditions.
  2. For: With nearly 700,000 members, SAFTU is the second largest union group, so it may have strong bargaining power.
  3. Chain: higher union wages raise firms' labour costs, so firms may hire fewer workers (wage/employment diagram).
  4. Against: union influence has declined globally, and laws may limit union power.
  5. Against: if productivity rises with pay, or firms can raise prices, jobs may not be lost; we do not know SAFTU's share of the workforce.
  6. Judge: impact depends on membership share, legal limits and whether firms can absorb higher costs.

Assess · January 2023 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

  1. Define a trade union and its aims: higher pay, better conditions, protecting workers' rights.
  2. For employees: Usdaw negotiated a rise from £9.20 to at least £10 per hour for 110,000 workers.
  3. For Morrisons: higher pay may raise motivation and productivity and reduce staff turnover.
  4. Against: higher wage costs for Morrisons may lead it to cut staff or hours, risking jobs.
  5. Against/depends: Morrisons may pass costs on through higher prices, which works if demand is price inelastic.
  6. Judge: good for employees if jobs are kept; impact on Morrisons depends on productivity gains and price elasticity.

Assess · June 2025 · Paper 1 · Q2(g)

  1. Define a trade union and how it bargains for pay and conditions.
  2. For pay: GMWU negotiated a monthly minimum wage of $500 for mine workers.
  3. For conditions: its 2023 focus is health and safety; long history since 1944 and over 16,000 members give influence.
  4. Against: falling demand for mine products means jobs are at risk, and it is still in talks to protect them.
  5. Against: higher pay and safety costs may lead firms to cut workers if they cannot raise prices.
  6. Judge: effective on pay and safety so far; depends on demand for mine products and firms' ability to absorb costs.
