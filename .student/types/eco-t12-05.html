Knowledge card

Division of labour: meaning, benefits and drawbacks

Written
  • Division of labour means splitting the production process into small separate tasks, with each worker doing just one task.
  • It is a form of specialisation: each worker concentrates on one job and gets very good at it.
  • Advantages for the firm: workers become faster and more skilled, no time is lost moving between tasks or changing tools, less training is needed, output rises and average cost per unit falls.
  • Advantages for workers: they become expert at their task and may feel more confident.
  • Disadvantages for workers: repeating one task all day is boring (monotonous), they gain few skills, and they may lose their job if the task is automated.
  • Disadvantages for the firm: bored workers make mistakes and may leave (higher labour turnover); if one worker is absent the whole line can stop; products may become standardised.
  • Productivity = output per worker; division of labour is one way to raise it, but not the only way (training and technology are others).

Before you begin

  • What productivity means (output per worker).
  • What average cost is.
  • That labour is one of the factors of production.
1

The method

  1. Define it in two parts: splitting production into smaller tasks, and each worker doing only one task.

    Why: 2-mark definitions give one mark for each part.

  2. Choose the advantage or disadvantage asked for and name it clearly (e.g. workers become faster; work becomes boring).

    Why: The first mark in Explain is for identifying the point.

  3. Develop it with 'because' and 'so': why it happens and what it does to output or costs.

    Why: The second mark is for a linked development.

  4. Use the product and workplace in the case (tennis balls, noodles, iPhones, bottling).

    Why: Context earns a mark in Explain and is needed for top levels.

  5. For 9-mark Assess or 12-mark Evaluate, follow those cards: weigh the speed and cost gains against boredom, errors, absence hold-ups and other ways to raise productivity, then judge.

    Why: Questions ask whether a firm 'always' benefits or if it is the 'best way', which needs a balanced judgement.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining division of labour as 'dividing the workers into groups' without mentioning splitting production into tasks.
  • ✗
    Giving an advantage for workers when the question asks about the firm, or the other way round.
  • ✗
    Stating 'it is more efficient' with no explanation of why (speed, skill, no switching).
  • ✗
    Writing generic answers that ignore the product and workplace in the case.
  • ✗
    In evaluation answers, failing to consider whether another method (training, technology) might be better.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Pick one advantage for the firm: workers specialise and become quicker.
  2. Develop: they repeat the same stage of making tennis balls, so they build skill and waste no time switching.
  3. Effect: more tennis balls made per worker each hour, so average cost per ball falls.
  4. Keep it to one advantage, fully developed.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

One advantage is that each factory worker becomes highly skilled at their single task, one stage of making the tennis balls. Because they repeat the same stage all day and never stop to switch jobs, they work faster and make fewer mistakes. This means the firm can produce more tennis balls per worker in the same time, which lowers the average cost of making each tennis ball.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for identifying the advantage (workers specialise and become skilled), 1 for the development (faster work, no switching, fewer errors), and 1 for the context and outcome (more tennis balls per worker, lower average cost per ball).

Assess · January 2023 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define division of labour; Foxconn's 270,000 workers each do one task: screens, assembling, checking, packaging.
  2. For: specialisation -> faster, more accurate work, no time lost between tasks -> higher productivity -> lower cost per iPhone and higher profit.
  3. For: simple tasks mean quick training, useful when hiring many workers for a new model.
  4. Against: repetitive checking is boring -> mistakes, re-work, turnover; one failed stage (faulty screen) stops packaging; absent specialists are hard to replace.
  5. Judgement: benefits likely outweigh costs at this huge scale; depends on motivation and reliability of each stage.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Division of labour means splitting production into many small tasks, with each worker specialising in one task. At Foxconn's factory in Shenzhen, the 270,000 workers each do a specific job, such as handling the phone screen, assembling components, checking that the phone works or packaging it for shipping.

The first benefit is higher productivity, which is the output per worker in a period of time. A worker who fits screens all day repeats the same actions many times and quickly becomes skilled and fast at them. Workers also do not waste time moving between tasks or changing tools. As output per worker rises, the cost of making each iPhone falls, because the same wage bill is spread over more phones. Lower average costs mean higher profit for Foxconn and help it keep its contract with Apple against rival manufacturers.

A second benefit is that simple tasks need little training. New workers can be hired and trained quickly, which matters for a firm employing 270,000 people, especially when it needs more staff to meet demand for a new iPhone. Breaking production into stages also makes it easier to use machines for some tasks.

However, the benefits may be limited. Doing the same task, such as checking phones, thousands of times is boring. Bored workers may lose concentration, make mistakes and become demotivated, so phones need to be re-worked, which lowers productivity and raises costs. High staff turnover would also raise recruitment costs. There is also a risk from interdependence: production is a chain, so if one stage stops, for example because of faulty screens or absent workers, phones cannot reach packaging and the whole line slows. Specialised workers cannot easily cover for each other.

Overall, the benefits for Foxconn are likely to outweigh the drawbacks, because at such a large scale even small time savings on each phone add up to big cost savings. However, this depends on how well Foxconn keeps workers motivated, for example through job rotation, and on how reliable each stage of the production line is.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines division of labour and productivity, applies the 270,000 workers and named tasks in chains to lower unit costs and profit, gives a developed counter-argument (boredom, mistakes, turnover, interdependence) and reaches a judgement that depends on motivation and reliability of each stage.

Evaluate · January 2020 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define division of labour and productivity; now each worker blends, kneads with wooden rolling pins and cuts.
  2. For: each worker specialises in one stage -> more skill and speed, no time lost switching -> higher output per worker -> lower unit cost.
  3. For: helps supply the 70% of food stalls in Jenjarum reliably; stages make it easier to add machines.
  4. Against: repetitive kneading or cutting is boring -> mistakes, lower quality, staff turnover; one slow stage holds up the rest.
  5. Alternatives: machinery to replace hand methods used since 1950; training; job rotation.
  6. Judgement: helpful but probably not the best way alone; depends on Wong's funds for machinery and expected growth in demand.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Division of labour means splitting production into separate stages, with each worker specialising in one task. Productivity is the output produced per worker in a period of time. At present, each worker in Wong's factory does every job: blending ingredients to make dough, kneading it with long wooden rolling pins and cutting the noodles to size.

If Wong introduces division of labour, one group of workers could only blend, another only knead and another only cut. Each worker would repeat one task many times, so they would build skill and speed. They would no longer waste time switching between tasks or putting down one tool to pick up another. As output per worker rises, the average cost of each batch of noodles falls, because wages are spread over more output. This matters because the factory already supplies 70% of the noodle-based food stalls in Jenjarum; higher productivity would help it meet this demand reliably and perhaps win the remaining stalls. Splitting the work into stages would also make it easier to bring in a machine for one stage later.

However, division of labour has costs. Kneading or cutting noodles all day is repetitive and boring. Workers may lose motivation and concentration and make more mistakes, so quality falls and productivity may not rise as much as expected. Some staff might leave, raising recruitment and training costs. The stages also depend on each other: if the kneaders are slow or absent, the cutters have nothing to do and supply to the food stalls could be delayed, which could lose customers.

There are also other ways to raise productivity. The factory has used hand methods with wooden rolling pins and has operated since 1950, so investing in machinery, such as dough mixers or noodle-cutting machines, could raise output far more than reorganising workers. Training workers, or paying them according to output, could also help.

In conclusion, division of labour is a cheap and quick way to raise productivity, but it is probably not the best way on its own. The biggest gains are likely to come from investment in machinery, possibly combined with division of labour and job rotation to reduce boredom. The best choice depends on how much Wong can afford to invest and on how much demand from the food stalls is expected to grow.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines division of labour and productivity, applies the blending, kneading and cutting stages and the 70% market share in clear chains to unit costs, weighs boredom and interdependence, compares alternatives (machinery, training) and gives a supported conclusion with a 'depends on' condition.

Assess · June 2021 · Paper 1 · Q2(h)

  1. Define division of labour: tasks such as frying burgers, chopping lettuce and tomatoes, peeling and slicing potatoes for fries.
  2. For: specialisation -> more skill, less moving around the kitchen -> faster service and lower cost per meal -> more customers served and higher profit, which suits 'fast' food.
  3. Against: simple tasks like frying burgers are boring -> demotivation, higher staff turnover, extra recruitment costs.
  4. Against: a slow stage (e.g. chopping lettuce) holds up later stages, so customers still wait; absent workers are hard to cover.
  5. Judgement: firms do not 'always' benefit; depends on keeping staff motivated (job rotation) and keeping each stage balanced.

Evaluate · November 2023 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

  1. Define division of labour and productivity; Raphael's 23 employees mix, heat, cool, add eggs and shape dough into lengths of about 15 cm.
  2. For: each worker specialises in one stage -> skill and speed rise, no time switching -> higher output per worker and lower unit cost.
  3. For: helps meet demand from busy restaurants and street vendors across Madrid; stages make it easier to add machinery.
  4. Against: tasks like adding eggs all day are boring -> mistakes and delays; late pastry could lose busy restaurant customers; a small team of 23 makes covering absences hard.
  5. Alternatives: training, better baking equipment, job rotation.
  6. Judgement: useful but not necessarily best; depends on how much training the tasks need and whether Raphael can fund new equipment.
