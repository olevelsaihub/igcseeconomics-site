Worked explanation

  1. Define privatisation.
  2. Chain 1: private investment, so more aircraft and routes, so more sales.
  3. Chain 2: the profit motive, so efficiency and lower costs.
  4. Chain 3: less bureaucracy and political interference, plus experienced owners.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Privatisation means selling a firm owned by the government to private investors, so it moves out of the public sector and into the private sector. If TAP Air is privatised, it could gain in several ways. First, private investors who buy the airline will want a return on their money, so they are likely to put new funds into it. TAP Air could use this investment to buy newer aircraft or open new routes, so it can sell more flights to passengers and grow beyond its operations in Portugal, raising its revenue. Second, the new owners will aim to maximise profit. To do this they will look for ways to cut waste, for example by planning flight schedules more efficiently and removing unnecessary costs, so the cost of each flight falls. Lower costs allow TAP Air either to make more profit or to charge lower fares to compete with other airlines. Third, once the airline is no longer owned by the Portuguese Government, decisions will no longer be slowed by bureaucracy or changed for political reasons. Managers can react quickly to changes in the airline market, and if the new owners have experience of running airlines, they can bring better management skills. Together, extra investment, the profit motive and freedom from government interference could make TAP Air more efficient, more competitive and more profitable.

How the marks are earned

Level 3 (5-6): privatisation defined, then three developed advantages (investment and growth, profit-driven efficiency and lower costs, less political interference and expert owners) applied to TAP Air and Portugal.

Assess · June 2023 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define privatisation; Shetland College, the only Further Education college on the islands, privatised in August 2021; managers expect better training opportunities.
  2. For: freedom from government control -> faster decisions, courses matched to local employers; must cover costs -> efficiency.
  3. For: better training raises skills and productivity of local workers; government saves funding.
  4. Against: only college on the islands, so a local monopoly with little pressure to improve; critics fear fewer courses, worse staff contracts and higher managers' pay.
  5. Judgement: benefits possible but uncertain; depends on management and on government regulation of courses and fees.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Privatisation is the transfer of a business or activity from the public sector, owned by the government, to the private sector. Shetland College, the only Further Education college in the Shetland Islands, was privatised in August 2021. Its managers claim this will improve educational and training opportunities.

One possible benefit is more freedom and efficiency. As a private organisation, the college's managers can make decisions without waiting for government approval, for example opening new courses that match the needs of local employers. Because it can no longer rely on public money, it has an incentive to control costs and use its resources efficiently, otherwise it would make losses. Higher efficiency could mean better facilities and courses for the same money.

A second benefit is that the college must attract students to earn revenue, so it has a reason to improve quality and offer courses people want. Better training raises the skills of local workers, which increases their productivity and chances of finding work, and can help the island economy grow. The government also no longer has to fund the college, so it can spend that money on other public services.

However, these benefits may not happen. Shetland College is the only Further Education college on the islands, so it is effectively a local monopoly. Without competition, there is little pressure to improve quality or keep fees low. Critics fear it may reduce the number of courses, cutting those that do not make money, which would reduce opportunities for students. Managers might award themselves much higher pay, while staff could face worse contracts and working conditions, which may lower motivation and the quality of teaching.

Overall, privatisation could bring benefits through flexibility and a focus on efficiency, but because the college has no local rivals the benefits are uncertain. Whether students gain depends on how the college is managed and on whether the government regulates the courses and fees it offers.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines privatisation, applies the college's position as the only local provider, managers' claims and critics' concerns; develops chains on efficiency, quality and skills; balances them against monopoly power, course cuts and staff conditions; ends with a judgement dependent on management and regulation.

Evaluate · November 2021 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define privatisation; data: Air India is loss-making, struggling to pay oil companies for fuel; government cut almost half the debt before the sale.
  2. For: profit motive -> cost cutting and efficiency -> loss turned to profit.
  3. For: new investment and management skills -> reopen routes to Asia and Africa -> more revenue, more competitive against other airlines.
  4. Against: no state safety net; remaining debt; oil companies may refuse credit; strong competition; possible job cuts and route closures.
  5. Judgement: likely to benefit in the long run; depends on new owners' investment and skill; short term may be difficult.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Privatisation means selling a state-owned business to private owners. The Indian Government plans to sell all its shares in Air India, an airline that has been making a loss and struggling to pay oil companies for its fuel.

Air India may benefit in several ways. First, private owners have a profit motive. Under government control there was less pressure to cut costs, because losses could be covered by the state. Private owners will want a return on their investment, so they have an incentive to cut waste, improve efficiency and turn the loss into a profit. This could mean better use of aircraft, better pricing and better customer service.

Second, private owners may bring new investment and management skills. The airline has reduced the number of countries it flies to, especially in other parts of Asia and Africa. With new capital it could buy more fuel-efficient aircraft, which would cut its fuel bill, a cost it has struggled to pay, and reopen profitable routes. More routes would raise revenue and help Air India compete with other airlines on international flights, as well as on its many domestic flights from Mumbai and New Delhi.

Third, the government reduced almost half of the debt before announcing the sale. This makes the airline more attractive to buyers and gives the new owners a better starting position, so they can focus on investment rather than on repaying old debts.

However, the benefits are not certain. After privatisation Air India will have no safety net: if losses continue, the state will not step in to reduce its debts again. Some debt remains, and oil companies that struggled to get paid may be unwilling to supply fuel on credit, which raises costs. The airline market is highly competitive, so even with new owners Air India may struggle to win customers from established airlines. New owners may also cut jobs or close unprofitable routes, which helps profit but may harm workers and some passengers. Reversing losses and building new routes takes time, so little may change in the short run.

In conclusion, Air India is likely to benefit from privatisation in the long run, mainly from the profit incentive, new investment and a lower debt burden. However, this depends on how much the new owners are willing to invest, on their skill in running an airline and on how competitive the market is. In the short term, the airline may face difficulties before any benefits appear.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines privatisation and applies the losses, unpaid fuel bills, reduced routes and halved debt in coherent chains (profit motive, investment, routes, competitiveness); weighs loss of state support, supplier risk and competition; gives a supported short-run versus long-run conclusion with clear conditions.

Evaluate · June 2024 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

  1. Define privatisation; data: government wants to sell Lagos Airport, Nigeria's largest; subsidies paid some running costs and safety and facility improvements.
  2. Against consumers: private owners seek profit -> higher charges; without subsidies, spending on safety and facilities may be cut; trade union resistance suggests unhappy staff and poorer service.
  3. For consumers: growing population, rising demand for flights and a stronger industrial base give owners revenue to invest; profit motive -> efficiency and better service to attract more passengers.
  4. For: experienced private airport managers may run it better than the government.
  5. Judgement: consumers may benefit if owners invest and competition or regulation limits prices; depends on owners' objectives and level of investment.
