Knowledge card

Supply of labour: what changes the workforce

Written
  • Supply of labour means how many people (or hours of work) are offered to employers at each wage rate.
  • It increases with a bigger population, immigration (people moving into the country), a higher retirement age, a lower school-leaving age and more women joining the workforce (female participation).
  • It decreases with a smaller population, emigration, a lower retirement age and a higher school-leaving age.
  • The age distribution matters: a country with many older people may have fewer people of working age.
  • Skills and qualifications affect the supply of workers for particular jobs (e.g. teachers, engineers).
  • Mobility of labour: geographical mobility is the ability to move to another place for work; occupational mobility is the ability to change to a different type of job. More mobility raises supply where workers are needed.

Before you begin

  • What a supply curve shows.
  • The difference between demand for labour (firms) and supply of labour (workers).
  • Basic population ideas: birth rate, ageing, migration.
1

The method

  1. Name the factor and decide its direction: does it add people to the workforce or remove them?

    Why: The first mark in Explain is for the correct effect (increase or decrease).

  2. Explain why: e.g. a higher pension age means people cannot afford to stop working, so they stay in work longer.

    Why: The development mark needs the reason people join or leave.

  3. Apply to the case: the country, the ages, the numbers of migrants or graduates.

    Why: Context is a mark in Explain and needed for higher levels.

  4. Add the consequence if relevant: more labour can ease shortages and hold wages down; less labour can push wages up.

    Why: Analysis credit for following the effect through.

  5. For a 9-mark Assess, follow the Assess card: compare the factor with alternatives (migration, female participation, skills, school-leaving age) and judge which works best for the case.

    Why: Many questions ask if one method is 'most effective', which needs comparison.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Getting the direction wrong for school-leaving age: raising it reduces labour supply.
  • ✗
    Confusing factors that affect demand for labour (demand for the product) with supply factors.
  • ✗
    Saying 'more workers' without explaining why people enter or stay in work.
  • ✗
    In 'most effective method' questions, discussing only one method with no comparison.
  • ✗
    Ignoring that older workers may not suit every industry in the case.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Define supply of labour.
  2. Use the data: retirement age rising from 62 to 63 in 2022 and 65 by 2030; life expectancy over 84.
  3. Chain: people must work longer before getting a pension, so 62- to 64-year-olds stay in work, so supply rises.
  4. Counter: manufacturing (about a quarter of GDP) may need younger, physically able workers; finance needs specialist skills.
  5. Compare alternatives (migration, female participation, training) and judge.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The supply of labour is how many people are ready to offer their work at a particular wage. Singapore is raising its retirement age from 62 to 63 in 2022 and to 65 by 2030. This means people aged 62 to 64 will have to keep working before they can retire and receive a pension, so experienced workers stay in the workforce instead of leaving it. Because life expectancy in Singapore is over 84, many people in their early sixties are healthy enough to keep working, so the change should clearly increase the supply of labour, and it also keeps skilled and experienced people available to firms. However, it may not be the most effective method. The rise is gradual and only adds a few years of work for one age group, so the increase in supply is limited. Manufacturing makes up almost 25% of Singapore's GDP, and some factory jobs may suit younger workers better, so older workers may not fill the vacancies that matter most. The growing financial services sector needs specialist skills, and simply keeping older workers in their jobs does not create those skills. Other methods could work better for these sectors: allowing more skilled migrants could quickly add workers of any age with the right skills; encouraging more women to join the workforce adds people across all ages; and education and training would raise the supply of qualified financial workers. Overall, raising the retirement age is a useful and fairly certain way to increase the number of workers, but it is unlikely to be the most effective on its own. A combination with training and migration would better match the workforce to Singapore's growing industries.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked out of 9 (3 application, 3 analysis, 3 evaluation). Definition and use of the ages and life expectancy give application; the chain from pension timing to more workers gives analysis; comparing with migration, female participation and training for manufacturing and finance, with a reasoned conclusion, gives Level 3.

Assess · November 2021 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define supply of labour and list its main factors.
  2. For: retirement age rising from 62 to 63 in 2022 and 65 by 2030 keeps older people in work.
  3. For: life expectancy over 84 means many can work longer and bring experience.
  4. Against: the gain is small and gradual; manufacturing (almost 25% of GDP) may need younger, physically fit workers.
  5. Against: growing financial services need skills; migration, female participation or training may add more workers.
  6. Judge: helpful but not the most effective alone; depends on which sectors are short of workers.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The supply of labour is how many people want to work, and can work, at each wage rate over a period of time. It is affected by factors such as population size and age structure, the retirement age, migration, the number of women in work, and skills.

Raising the retirement age could increase the supply of labour in Singapore. At present people retire at 62, but this will rise to 63 in 2022 and to 65 by 2030. People who would have stopped working at 62 will stay in the workforce for longer, so the number of available workers rises without any new workers needing to arrive. Because life expectancy is now over 84 years, many people are healthy enough to keep working into their sixties, so the policy is realistic. Older workers also bring experience, so the extra supply may be of good quality. Keeping people in work longer also means more income is earned and more tax is paid.

However, it may not be the most effective method. The change is gradual and only adds a few years at the end of each career, so the increase in supply is fairly small and slow. It also depends on which jobs need workers. Manufacturing contributes almost 25% of GDP, and some factory jobs are physically demanding, so older workers may be less able or willing to do them. The growing financial services sector needs up-to-date skills, so older workers may need retraining. Other methods could add more workers more quickly: allowing more skilled migrants, helping more women into work, for example through childcare, or improving education and training so that more people have the skills firms need.

Overall, raising the retirement age will increase Singapore's labour supply, especially as people live longer, but it is unlikely to be the most effective method on its own. It is most useful where experience matters and the work is not physically demanding. Whether it is the best method depends on which sectors face shortages and how quickly extra workers are needed, so a mix with migration and training is likely to work better.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines supply of labour and its factors (AO2); chains the rise from 62 to 63 and 65 to more workers, supported by life expectancy over 84 (AO3); weighs small, slow gains, the 25% manufacturing share, financial services skills and alternatives, ending in a judgement depending on sector shortages and timing (AO4).

Assess · June 2022 · Paper 1 · Q2(h)

  1. Define supply of labour; more qualified people normally means more potential teachers.
  2. For: more science, maths and language graduates, and a £9 000 bonus attracts some into teaching.
  3. Against: many leave within one or two years due to long hours, bureaucracy and student behaviour.
  4. Against: science graduates easily find other jobs, so occupational mobility pulls them away.
  5. Against: the bonus applies only to some subjects, so other graduates have less incentive to teach.
  6. Judge: not always; depends on working conditions, pay compared with other jobs, and keeping teachers.

Assess · January 2023 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

  1. Define migration and how it moves the labour supply curve rightwards.
  2. Data: Dubai has more than 2.5 million people and over 80% are immigrant workers.
  3. For: migrants fill shortages in construction, cleaning and care, so the economy can produce and grow more.
  4. Against: migrants are concentrated in a few sectors and may lack skills for other shortages.
  5. Against: supply may be short-term if migrants return home; other methods may give a longer-term supply.
  6. Judge: large benefits for labour-heavy sectors; depends on skills mix and how long migrants stay.
