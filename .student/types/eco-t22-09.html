Knowledge card

Trading blocs: what they are and their effects

Written
  • Trading bloc: a group of countries that agree to reduce or remove trade barriers (tariffs, quotas) between themselves.
  • Members do not need the same currency, tax rates, language or shared borders, and they can still trade with countries outside the bloc.
  • Examples from past papers: AfCFTA (Africa), RCEP and CPTPP (Asia-Pacific), ASEAN, APTA, Mercosur.
  • Benefits for members: a larger tariff-free market to sell into, economies of scale and specialisation, cheaper imports and inputs, lower prices and more choice for consumers, more foreign direct investment (FDI), and more bargaining power in trade talks.
  • Drawbacks: domestic firms face stronger competition from member countries with lower costs, which can mean lower profits, closures and job losses; production may move to cheaper members; a country may become too reliant on trade within the bloc.
  • Overall effects depend on how competitive a country's firms are and how much it trades with the rest of the world.

Before you begin

  • What tariffs and quotas are.
  • Free trade and its gains and losses.
  • Economies of scale and specialisation.
  • What foreign direct investment is.
1

The method

  1. Define a trading bloc as countries joining together to cut trade barriers among themselves.

    Why: Both parts (group of countries, reduced barriers between them) are credited.

  2. Check whose view (the country, its firms or consumers) and which side (benefits or disadvantages) the question wants.

    Why: Off-focus points earn little.

  3. Develop each effect as a chain: no tariffs means cheaper exports means more sales means growth and jobs; or stronger competition means lower profits means closures and job losses.

    Why: Developed chains earn the AO2 marks.

  4. Use the bloc data: number of members, named partner countries, market size or GDP.

    Why: Application is the AO3 half of the marks.

  5. For a 9-mark Assess or 12-mark Evaluate, follow the Assess or Evaluate card: set benefits against competition, job losses and dependence, then judge what the outcome depends on.

    Why: AO4 requires counter-arguments and a supported judgement.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying bloc members must share a currency, borders or tax rates, or can only trade with each other.
  • ✗
    Giving general free-trade points without linking them to membership of the named bloc.
  • ✗
    Listing benefits for consumers when the question asks about firms, or the reverse.
  • ✗
    Not using the bloc details such as the number of members or the named partner countries.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Country: India; side: benefits of being in a trading bloc.
  2. Data: India is in the Asia-Pacific Trade Agreement (APTA), five members including Bangladesh, China, South Korea and Sri Lanka; Figure 4 shows FDI into India rising from $45.14bn in 2016 to $64.37bn in 2019.
  3. Chain 1: lower barriers give Indian firms a wider market, so exports and revenue rise.
  4. Chain 2: larger output brings economies of scale and specialisation.
  5. Chain 3: cheaper imports from members mean lower prices and more choice for Indian consumers.
  6. Chain 4: easier trade with neighbours makes India more attractive for FDI.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Countries in a trading bloc agree to cut trade barriers among themselves. As a member of APTA, India can sell to the other members, such as Bangladesh, China, South Korea and Sri Lanka, with lower tariffs. Indian goods are therefore cheaper in these markets, so Indian firms can increase their exports and sales revenue, which raises output and creates jobs in India.

Selling to a larger market lets Indian firms produce on a bigger scale. They gain economies of scale, so their average costs fall, and India can specialise in the goods and services it produces most efficiently, such as software, making its firms more competitive.

Indian consumers also benefit. Imports from other members face lower tariffs, so goods from countries like China and South Korea are cheaper and there is a wider choice in Indian shops, raising living standards.

Finally, because firms based in India can export easily to the other members, India becomes a more attractive place for multinationals to build factories. This may help explain why FDI into India rose from $45.14bn in 2016 to $64.37bn in 2019, bringing more capital, jobs and skills.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked, 3 AO2 and 3 AO3. Level 3 needs an accurate bloc definition and several developed benefits (wider market and exports, economies of scale and specialisation, lower prices and choice, FDI), applied to APTA's members and the FDI figures.

Assess · June 2019 · Paper 2 · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define trading bloc; ASEAN founded 1967 with Thailand, 10 members by 2016.
  2. For: bigger tariff-free market (population 185 million to 634 million) -> more Thai exports -> economies of scale and specialisation.
  3. For: cheaper imports and inputs, more choice; FDI attracted to sell across the region; data shows trade and GDP per capita ($122 to $4021) rising.
  4. Against: data compares different groups decades apart, so not all gains come from membership; more competition for Thai firms and job losses; reliance on the bloc and trade diversion.
  5. Judgement: clear benefits; depends on Thai firms' competitiveness and trade with non-members.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A trading bloc is an agreement between several countries, usually in one region, to cut or remove trade barriers such as tariffs and quotas between members. Thailand was a founder of ASEAN in 1967, which grew to a 10 member bloc by 2016.

The first benefit is a larger market. The population of the bloc rose from 185 million to 634 million. With fewer tariffs, Thai firms can sell to consumers in countries such as Vietnam and Malaysia more cheaply, so exports rise. Selling to a larger market lets Thai firms produce on a larger scale and gain economies of scale, which lowers average costs and makes them more competitive, even outside the bloc. Countries can also specialise in what they produce most efficiently, which raises total output.

Second, consumers gain. Imports from other members are cheaper without tariffs, so Thai consumers get lower prices and more choice, which raises living standards. Thai firms can also buy cheaper raw materials and components from other members, cutting their costs.

Third, the bloc can attract foreign direct investment, because a multinational that locates in Thailand can sell tariff-free across the region, bringing jobs and technology. The data suggests strong gains for the bloc: trade rose from $9 707 million to $2218534 million and GDP per capita from $122 to $4021, while life expectancy rose from 56 to 71 years.

However, the data compares different groups of countries decades apart, so not all of these gains were caused by membership; new technology and the growth of world trade also played a part. Membership also brings costs. Thai firms face more competition from imports from other members, and inefficient firms may close, causing job losses in some industries. Thailand may come to rely too much on trade within the bloc, and trade may be diverted away from cheaper suppliers outside it.

Overall, being a member of ASEAN brings important benefits to Thailand through a bigger market, lower costs, more choice and investment. The size of the benefit depends on how competitive Thai firms are within the region and on how much Thailand continues to trade with countries outside the bloc.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines a trading bloc, uses the population, trade, GDP per capita and life expectancy figures in chains (market size, economies of scale, consumer choice, FDI), questions what the data proves, weighs competition and trade diversion, and gives a judgement that depends on competitiveness and outside trade.

Evaluate · November 2024 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define trading bloc; RCEP: about 30% of global trade, 15 members incl. China, Japan, South Korea; India withdrew in 2019.
  2. For: lower tariffs -> cheaper Indian exports -> more output, jobs and multiplier; economies of scale.
  3. For: cheaper imported inputs, supply-chain links, more FDI and bargaining power.
  4. Against: strong competition from China, Japan and South Korea -> job losses in agriculture and manufacturing; possible trade deficit; firms relocating.
  5. Judgement: worthwhile only with a fair and balanced deal protecting sensitive industries; depends on the terms.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A trading bloc is an agreement between several countries to cut or remove trade barriers, such as tariffs, between members. The RCEP is the world's largest trading bloc, with about 30% of global trade and 15 member countries, including China, Japan and South Korea, the three largest economies in Asia. In October 2023 India was again considering joining.

The main benefit is access to a much larger market. If tariffs on Indian goods fall, Indian exports become cheaper in member countries, so demand for them rises. Higher exports raise output and create jobs in India, and the extra incomes are spent, giving a multiplier effect that boosts economic growth. Selling to more customers lets Indian firms grow and gain economies of scale, which lowers average costs and makes them more competitive worldwide.

Second, Indian firms and consumers could import more cheaply. Lower tariffs on components and raw materials from members reduce production costs, and consumers gain lower prices and more choice. Indian firms could also join regional supply chains, for example making parts for products assembled elsewhere in Asia.

Third, membership may attract foreign direct investment. Multinationals may build factories in India to use its large labour force while selling tariff-free across the bloc, bringing jobs, technology and skills. Being inside a bloc with about 30% of global trade could also give India more influence in trade negotiations.

However, there are significant risks, which is why India withdrew from the negotiations in 2019. Its concern was the impact on domestic industries, particularly agriculture and manufacturing. Removing tariffs would expose Indian firms to strong competition from large, efficient producers in China, Japan and South Korea. Cheaper imports could take market share from Indian farmers and manufacturers, leading to falling output and job losses in sectors that employ many people. India might also run a larger trade deficit with members if imports grow faster than exports, and some firms may move production to member countries with lower costs.

In conclusion, joining the RCEP could bring large benefits for India through bigger export markets, cheaper inputs and more investment, but these must be weighed against possible damage to agriculture and manufacturing. The overall benefit depends on the terms of the deal. If India obtains the fair and balanced agreement it is seeking, such as gradual tariff cuts for sensitive industries, membership is likely to be worthwhile; if not, the costs to domestic industries may outweigh the gains.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines a trading bloc, applies the 30% share of global trade, 15 members and the 2019 withdrawal over agriculture and manufacturing; develops chains on exports, multiplier, economies of scale, cheaper inputs and FDI; balances with competition and job losses; conclusion depends on the deal's terms.
