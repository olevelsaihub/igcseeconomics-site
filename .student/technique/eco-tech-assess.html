Economics / Assess: weigh up factors using the data and judge (9 marks)

Pearson Edexcel · International GCSE Economics · 4EC1

Assess: weigh up factors using the data and judge (9 marks)

Pearson's meaning: write a longer answer that uses the information you are given to weigh the factors against each other in an economic setting. You must show both sides (why it works or helps, and why it may not) and say which side is stronger in this case.

Command word: AssessUsually 9 marksAbout 10 minutes

What Pearson asks

Pearson's meaning: write a longer answer that uses the information you are given to weigh the factors against each other in an economic setting. You must show both sides (why it works or helps, and why it may not) and say which side is stronger in this case.

How the marks are earned

Nine marks in three equal parts: 3 for AO2 (economic terms and theory), 3 for AO3 (using the data in developed analysis) and 3 for AO4 (evaluation). Marked on levels 1-3, 4-6 and 7-9. Giving only one side caps the answer at Level 1. The top level needs more than one viewpoint, a balanced and well-organised argument, and good use of the case.

Method

  1. Find the claim in the question (for example: monetary policy will increase output) and the context (the country or firm).

    Why: Everything you write must be weighed against this claim.

  2. Write one or two developed chains FOR the claim, as in the Analyse card, each using the data.

    Why: This earns the AO2 and AO3 marks.

  3. Write one or two developed chains AGAINST, starting 'However, ...' and using the data again.

    Why: A second viewpoint is required for anything above Level 1.

  4. Add a 'depends on' point: what would make the effect bigger or smaller.

    Why: This shows weighing, not just listing two sides.

  5. End with a short judgement that answers the question for this case and gives one reason.

    Why: Assess means weigh up; a reasoned final view shows the weighing is complete.

Answer frame

Term means ... . The data shows ... . For: this means ..., so ..., which leads to ... . However: ... because ..., so ... . It depends on ... . Overall, in this case ... is likely/unlikely because ... .

Common errors

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Writing only the advantages (or only the 'for' side), which keeps the answer in Level 1.
  • Two-sided lists of single points with no chains of reasoning.
  • Ignoring the numbers and facts in the case, so the answer could be about any country.
  • A final line that repeats the question ('so it is effective') with no reason from the case.
  • Spending so long on one side that the counter-argument is a single sentence.

Worked illustration

November 2025 · Paper 2 · Q2(g)

The complete original question is shown; the illustration answers part (g).

Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Monetary policy means the central bank changing interest rates to influence spending in the economy. New Zealand's central bank cut its base rate from 5.5% to 5.25%.

Why it could raise output: a lower base rate makes borrowing cheaper. Firms pay less interest on loans, so more projects such as new machines become worth doing. This investment adds to total demand, so firms produce more. Households also pay less on loans and mortgages, which leaves them more income to spend, and saving earns less reward. Consumer spending has been weak, so a rise in spending would lift sales, and firms would increase output and may take on workers at a time when unemployment is rising. Lower interest rates can also reduce the value of the New Zealand dollar, which makes the country's exports cheaper abroad, so exporting firms sell and produce more.

However, the cut is only 0.25 percentage points, so the monthly cost of a loan hardly changes. Confidence is weak and unemployment is rising, so households may save in case they lose their jobs instead of borrowing to spend. Firms that expect low sales may still delay investment even when loans are cheaper. Monetary policy also works with a time lag, so any rise in output may take many months. If spending does rise while firms are near full capacity, prices may rise rather than output.

Judgement: monetary policy is only likely to be partly effective in raising New Zealand's output in the short run. The cut points in the right direction, but because it is small and confidence is low, the effect depends mainly on whether households and firms feel safe enough to borrow. Further cuts, or support from government spending, may be needed.

Why it earns the marks

Level 3 (7-9). AO2: interest rates, investment, consumption, exchange rate and time lag used correctly. AO3: chains use the cut from 5.5% to 5.25%, weak spending and rising unemployment. AO4: balanced counter-arguments plus a reasoned judgement on how far it works.
