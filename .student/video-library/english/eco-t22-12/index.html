Exchange rates: meaning and what moves a currency

Idea. Before a holiday in Spain, you swap your pounds for euros. The board might say one pound buys one euro fifteen. Money from one country has a price in another country's money. That price is the exchange rate. An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another currency. For example, one dollar equals nought point eight pounds. Like any price, it is set by demand and supply. Here, the price of pounds in dollars goes up the side, and the quantity of pounds along the bottom. Where they cross is the exchange rate. Demand for pounds comes from foreigners: buying UK exports, saving in UK banks, investing in the UK, or speculating. Supply comes from people selling pounds: UK residents buying imports or investing abroad.

Method. Step one, decide which side the factor works on. Are foreigners buying the currency? That is demand. Are people selling it? That is supply. Then decide: more, or less? Step two, give the reason. Higher interest rates give savers a better return. Savings that move between countries for the best return are called hot money. Demand rises, so the currency appreciates, or rises in value. Speculators buy a currency when they expect it to rise, and sell when they expect a fall, hoping for profit. A government can sell its own currency to hold it down, or buy it to push it up. Step three, finish with the effect on the rate: appreciation, a rise, or depreciation, a fall. Step four, use the currency and figures named in the question.

Worked example. Here is a real question from January 2020. South Africa raised interest rates by a quarter of a point, to six point seven five per cent. Analyse the likely impact on its currency, the rand. Six marks. Demand side. At six point seven five per cent, foreign savers earn more in South African banks. To deposit there, they must buy rand. This is hot money, and demand for the rand rises. Supply side. South African savers now have less reason to send money abroad. So fewer rand are sold, and the supply of rand falls. On the diagram, demand for rand shifts right. It crosses the supply curve higher up, so the price of the rand rises from E R to E R one. The rand appreciates. Speculators expecting a rise may buy rand early, adding to demand. But the rise was only a quarter of a point, so the appreciation may be small. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is thinking higher interest rates make a currency fall. They attract savers, so demand rises and the currency usually rises. Another is saying a government stops its currency rising by buying it. Buying adds demand and pushes it up. To hold it down, the government sells its own currency.

Your turn. Now you try. Imagine Kenya's central bank cuts interest rates from nine per cent to seven per cent. Explain the likely impact on the Kenyan shilling. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. At seven per cent, saving in Kenya is less rewarding. Fewer foreign savers buy shillings, so demand for the shilling falls. Demand shifts left, so the price of the shilling falls from E R to E R one. The shilling depreciates. Remember: decide demand or supply, give the reason, then say whether the named currency appreciates or depreciates. Now try the practice questions.
