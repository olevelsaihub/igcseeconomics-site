Taxes, subsidies and parks to protect the environment

Read the narration

Idea. Every car journey puts fumes into the air that everyone breathes. The driver pays for the petrol, but not for the harm to other people. That harm is a negative externality. A tax on a polluting good raises the cost of supplying it. Supply shifts left. The price rises from P e to P one, and the quantity falls from Q e to Q one. Less is bought, so there is less pollution. A subsidy is money from the government. Give it to bus companies, and their costs fall. Supply shifts right, fares fall, and more people take the bus instead of driving. The government can also provide parks. A park protects land and wildlife from building, gives space for exercise, and can attract tourists.

Method. Step one, name the measure. Step two, explain how it changes price or behaviour. Step three, finish the chain: less of the harmful activity, so less pollution. Use the figures in the data. For Assess, weigh the limits. A tax barely works when demand is price inelastic, and it can hurt low-income households. Subsidies and parks cost money. Then judge.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2021. Germany was thinking of raising the tax on meat to protect the environment. Assess the use of taxation to protect the environment in a country such as Germany. Nine marks. Read the data. Meat now has a reduced tax rate of seven per cent. Some want the standard nineteen per cent. Methane from animals is fourteen point five per cent of greenhouse gas emissions. The chain. A higher tax raises the price of meat. Households buy less, so farmers rear fewer animals, and methane emissions fall. A second benefit: the government collects more revenue for green projects, and shoppers have a reason to switch to plant-based foods. But meat is a regular part of many diets, so demand may be price inelastic. With a steep demand curve, price rises a lot but quantity falls only a little. The tax also hits low-income families and farmers. Judge. A meat tax is sensible, but how much it helps depends on how price elastic demand is. If inelastic, pair it with other measures. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is mixing up the tools: putting a subsidy on the polluting good. Another is ending without a judgement that says what the outcome depends on.

Your turn. Now you try. A government gives a subsidy to firms that make solar panels. Explain how this may protect the environment. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. The subsidy lowers the firms' costs, so supply shifts right. The price of solar panels falls from P e to P one, and more are bought. More homes make their own clean electricity, so less is needed from coal or gas power stations. That means less air pollution for third parties. Remember: name the measure, show how it changes price or behaviour, finish with less pollution, then weigh and judge. Now try the practice questions.
