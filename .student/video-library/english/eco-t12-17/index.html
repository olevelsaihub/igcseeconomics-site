Demand for labour: what makes firms hire

Idea. When a bakery becomes popular, the queue gets longer. To bake more bread, the owner hires another baker. The baker is hired because customers want bread. Demand for labour is the number of workers firms are willing and able to employ at each wage rate. At a higher wage, firms usually want fewer workers. That is a movement along the curve. Labour is a derived demand. Firms want workers only because consumers want the product the workers make. More demand for the product means more demand for workers.

Method. Step one, define demand for labour and name the factor. Step two, for derived demand: product demand rises, firms produce more, so they need more workers. The demand for labour shifts right. Step three, machines. If machines become cheaper or better, firms switch from workers to machines, and demand for labour shifts left. Step four, apply it to the job and the figures in the case. Step five, for Assess and Evaluate, weigh the main factor against the others, such as productivity: if each worker produces more, each is worth more to the firm. Then judge.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2025. Demand for wooden furniture is expected to rise by about five per cent in the next five years. Woodcutters cut down the trees for the wood. Analyse how this may affect the demand for woodcutters. Six marks. Start by defining it. Demand for woodcutters is derived: timber firms want them only because people buy wooden furniture. Furniture needs wood, and woodcutters cut it. Now the chain. Furniture demand rises by about five per cent. Furniture makers produce more and buy more wood. Timber firms cut more trees, so they hire more woodcutters, or give them more hours. On a diagram, the demand for woodcutters shifts right, from D to D one. Comment on the size. About five per cent over five years is modest, so hiring should rise gradually. It could be smaller if timber firms use more cutting machines. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is defining derived demand as consumers wanting a product. It is the firm's demand for workers that comes from that. Another is treating a wage rise as a shift. A change in the wage moves along the demand curve.

Your turn. Now you try. A city's online food delivery orders rise sharply. At the same time, some firms start testing delivery robots. Analyse how these changes may affect the demand for delivery riders. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. More orders: firms must deliver more meals, so they need more riders. Demand for riders shifts right. This is derived demand. Robots are a substitute. If they become cheaper than riders, firms switch, and demand for riders falls. But robots still need staff to run and repair them. The overall effect depends on how fast robots spread. Remember: define demand for labour, name the factor, build the chain to the number of workers, and use the job in the case. Now try the practice questions.
