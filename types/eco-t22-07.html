Knowledge card

Draw a tariff, subsidy or indirect tax change

Written
  • The diagram has Price on the vertical (y) axis and Quantity on the horizontal (x) axis, with a downward-sloping demand curve D and an upward-sloping supply curve S crossing at Pe and Qe.
  • A tariff (a tax on imports) or an indirect tax (a tax on spending, such as on fuel) adds to suppliers' costs, so supply shifts LEFT.
  • A subsidy (a government payment to producers) lowers suppliers' costs, so supply shifts RIGHT.
  • Removing works the opposite way: removing a tariff or indirect tax shifts supply right; removing a subsidy shifts supply left.
  • Supply left: new price higher (P1 above Pe), new quantity lower (Q1 left of Qe). Supply right: new price lower, new quantity higher.
  • Demand does not move in these questions; only the supply curve shifts.

Before you begin

  • How to read a supply and demand diagram.
  • What equilibrium price and quantity are.
  • That higher costs reduce supply and lower costs increase it.
  • What tariffs, subsidies and indirect taxes are.
1

The method

  1. Decide the direction: new tariff, new tax or removed subsidy means supply LEFT; new subsidy, removed tariff or removed tax means supply RIGHT.

    Why: The shift mark depends on the correct direction.

  2. Draw a new supply curve parallel to S on the correct side and label it S1 (you may add the cause, e.g. S1 (tariff)).

    Why: One mark is for the shifted curve, and it must be labelled.

  3. Find where S1 crosses the unchanged demand curve D.

    Why: That point is the new equilibrium.

  4. Draw a dashed line across to the price axis and label it P1.

    Why: One mark is for the new equilibrium price, labelled.

  5. Draw a dashed line down to the quantity axis and label it Q1.

    Why: One mark is for the new equilibrium quantity, labelled.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Shifting demand instead of supply.
  • ✗
    Getting the direction wrong when a tariff or subsidy is removed rather than introduced.
  • ✗
    Leaving the new curve, price or quantity unlabelled, which loses that mark.
  • ✗
    Marking the new price or quantity at a point that is not where the new supply curve meets demand.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Cause: China introduces a tariff on imported large tyres, a tax that raises suppliers' costs.
  2. Direction: higher costs mean supply shifts left.
  3. Draw S1 to the left of S, parallel to it, labelled S1.
  4. Mark where S1 meets D; read across to P1 above Pe and down to Q1 left of Qe.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

On the diagram I draw a new supply curve S1 to the left of the original supply curve S, parallel to it, and label it S1 (tariff). The new curve crosses the unchanged demand curve D at a higher point. From this point I draw a dashed line across to the price axis and label it P1, which is above Pe, showing the price of large tyres has risen. I draw a dashed line down to the quantity axis and label it Q1, which is to the left of Qe, showing that fewer large tyres are bought.

How the marks are earned

1 mark: supply shifted left and labelled S1. 1 mark: new equilibrium price P1, higher than Pe, labelled. 1 mark: new equilibrium quantity Q1, lower than Qe, labelled.
