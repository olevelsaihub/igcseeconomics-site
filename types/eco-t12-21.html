Regulation, fines and pollution permits

Rules and legal limits, fines and tradable pollution permits used to reduce negative externalities and protect the environment (and to deter harmful behaviour such as speeding or illegal parking), with their advantages and disadvantages.

Knowledge card

Regulation, fines and pollution permits

Written
  • A negative externality is a harmful side effect of producing or consuming something that falls on third parties (people not involved), e.g. pollution, litter, accidents.
  • Regulation is a rule or law that controls what firms or people may do, e.g. banning single-use plastic bags, setting air-quality standards, or a clean-air zone.
  • A fine is a money penalty for breaking a law or rule; it deters harmful behaviour (speeding, illegal parking, polluting) and can raise revenue.
  • A pollution permit gives a firm legal permission to release no more than a set amount of pollution. The total is capped; firms that pollute less can sell spare permits, so there is a money reason to cut pollution.
  • Strengths: regulation gives direct, clear control; fines make harm costly; permits cap total pollution and reward cleaner firms.
  • Weaknesses: monitoring and enforcement cost money and time; fines may be too low or paid without changing behaviour; the right limit is hard to set; rules can raise firms' costs and affect jobs; some firms ignore them.

Before you begin

  • What externalities, third parties and social costs are.
  • That governments intervene when markets cause harm.
  • How higher costs change behaviour.
1

The method

  1. Define the control in the question: regulation (a legal rule), fine (a money penalty), or pollution permit (legal permission to release a limited amount of pollution).

    Why: Definition marks and the opening of long answers need an accurate meaning.

  2. Name the externality in the case and who it harms (third parties, animals, the environment).

    Why: Shows why the government is intervening.

  3. Explain the mechanism: a ban stops the activity; a fine makes harm costly, so people avoid it; permits cap pollution and reward firms that cut it.

    Why: Explain and Analyse marks reward how the control changes behaviour.

  4. Apply the case evidence: size of fines, results such as cleaner streets or return rates, area covered.

    Why: Application to the data is required for context marks and higher levels.

  5. For 9-mark Assess or 12-mark Evaluate, follow those cards: weigh enforcement costs, compliance, setting the right level, effects on firms, and alternatives (taxes, other fines), then judge effectiveness.

    Why: Effectiveness questions require limitations and a supported judgement.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Describing a pollution permit as permission to pollute without limit; the key is the cap and the ability to trade.
  • ✗
    Saying a fine 'stops pollution' without explaining the deterrent mechanism.
  • ✗
    Ignoring the cost and difficulty of monitoring and enforcing rules.
  • ✗
    Confusing fines with taxes or subsidies in multiple choice.
  • ✗
    Concluding 'it is effective' with no reference to the case's evidence or limits.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Define negative externality and regulation.
  2. Use the case: Kenya's ban on single-use plastic bags, in place for over five years.
  3. Chain: the ban, backed by penalties, cuts bag use, so less visual pollution and fewer livestock harmed.
  4. Limits: problem not fully removed; enforcement costs; depends on compliance; plastic bags are only one source of pollution.
  5. Compare with alternatives (fines, taxes) and judge.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A negative externality is a harmful side effect of production or consumption that falls on third parties. Discarded plastic bags are an example: they litter towns and the countryside, and animals that eat them can die, so farmers and the public bear costs they did not choose. Regulation is a legal rule set by the government to control behaviour. Kenya has banned single-use plastic bags for more than five years. Because using or supplying them is illegal and can be punished, shops and consumers have switched to alternatives, so far fewer bags are thrown away. The case shows the environment is cleaner: there is less visual pollution and fewer livestock are put in danger. This suggests regulation is effective, because a ban tackles the cause directly rather than just making it more expensive. However, the case also says the ban has not removed the problem completely, so some people still use or dump bags illegally. Enforcing a ban means paying officials to inspect shops and borders, which has an opportunity cost, as the money could be used elsewhere. Its success also depends on the public cooperating and on penalties being large enough to deter. In addition, plastic bags are only one cause of pollution; other sources need other policies. Fines or taxes on polluting products could be used alongside the ban. Overall, regulation has been quite effective in Kenya, because it has produced a visible improvement. How effective it is in the long run depends on how strictly the government enforces the ban and whether it is combined with other measures to deal with the remaining pollution.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked out of 9 (3 application, 3 analysis, 3 evaluation). Definitions and the Kenyan ban give application; the chain from the ban to less litter and fewer livestock deaths gives analysis; incomplete success, enforcement costs, compliance, other pollution and alternatives, with an 'it depends' judgement, give Level 3.

Assess · January 2023 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define a fine and a negative externality; pollution killed more than 1,000 fish.
  2. For: the £2.3m fine raises costs, cuts profit and warns other firms, giving an incentive not to pollute.
  3. Against: £24.4m in fines for 9 cases since 2017 shows Thames Water kept polluting.
  4. Against: a £2.3m fine is small next to an operating profit of £513.4m in 2020.
  5. Against: polluted in 2016, fined in 2021, so the fine is slow and comes after the damage.
  6. Judge: fines work only if large relative to profit, quick and certain; combine with regulation.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A fine is a money penalty that the government charges a firm when it breaks an environmental law. Pollution is a negative externality: a cost paid by third parties, such as the fish and the people who use the stream, and not by the firm that caused it. A fine tries to make the polluter pay for this external cost.

Fines can be effective. Thames Water was fined £2.3m in 2021 for polluting a stream and killing more than 1,000 fish. The fine raises the firm's costs and cuts its profit, so polluting becomes more expensive than preventing pollution. If managers expect future fines, they have an incentive to spend money on better treatment and checks. A public fine can also damage the firm's reputation, and it warns other water firms that pollution will be punished, so they may take more care. The money raised could help pay to clean up the damage.

However, the data suggests fines have not changed Thames Water's behaviour much. Since 2017 it has paid £24.4m in fines for 9 separate cases, so the firm kept polluting after earlier penalties. One reason is the size of the fine compared with the firm's earnings. In 2020 Thames Water made an operating profit of £513.4m, so a £2.3m fine is a very small share of its profit. If paying the fine is cheaper than fixing the cause, the firm has little reason to change. Fines are also slow: the stream was polluted in 2016 but the fine came in 2021, and the fish were already dead. A fine punishes after the damage; it does not prevent it.

Overall, fines are only partly effective in a country such as the UK. They work best when the fine is large compared with the firm's profit, when cases are settled quickly, and when firms believe they will be caught. For a firm as profitable as Thames Water, fines will only protect the environment if they are raised sharply or combined with stricter regulation, such as regular checks on what it releases into rivers.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines fines and negative externality (AO2), builds chains from the £2.3m fine to costs, profit and incentives, then uses £24.4m, 9 cases and £513.4m profit to show weak deterrence (AO3). Balanced two sides and a judgement that depends on fine size, speed and likelihood of being caught (AO4).

Evaluate · June 2025 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define regulation and the negative externality of plastic: damage to oceans, rivers, land and emissions.
  2. For: the ban gives direct, immediate control over 2.7 billion cutlery items and 721 million plates, with only 10% recycled.
  3. For: clear rules for every food firm push a switch to reusable alternatives; bag sales fell from 197 million to 133 million.
  4. Against: higher costs for small takeaway firms, less consumer choice, and costly monitoring.
  5. Against: covers only some items, while consumer goods supply caused 26% of UK emissions in 2023.
  6. Judge: strong advantages if enforced and affordable alternatives exist; best used with taxes and fines.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Regulation means rules set by the government and backed by law that control what firms and consumers may do. Plastic pollution is a negative externality: making and throwing away plastic creates costs for third parties, such as damage to oceans, rivers and land and greenhouse gas emissions, which the buyer and seller do not pay for. Because these external costs are ignored, too much single-use plastic is used.

The main advantage of regulation is direct control. Since October 2023, no firm in the food industry can sell single-use plastic cutlery, polystyrene cups, plastic plates or food containers in England. A ban works at once: it does not depend on firms reacting to a price change. People in England use 2.7 billion items of single-use cutlery and 721 million single-use plates every year, and only 10% of these are recycled, so most of them become waste. Removing these items from sale should cut litter that takes hundreds of years to break down, and reduce the emissions from producing them.

A second advantage is certainty. Every food firm faces the same rule, so it is easy to understand and fair between competitors. It also pushes firms and consumers towards reusable or more sustainable alternatives, which may become cheaper as more are made. The data shows that plastic use can fall quickly: single-use plastic bags sold by the main retailers fell from 197 million in 2021 to 133 million in 2023.

However, regulation has costs. Small takeaway shops may have to pay more for alternative materials, which raises their costs and could lead to higher prices for consumers. Consumers also lose the choice of cheap, convenient items. The ban has to be policed: checking every food firm in England takes time and money, and if inspections are rare or fines are low, some firms may ignore it. In addition, the ban covers only some items in the food industry, while the supply of consumer goods and services caused 26% of UK emissions in 2023, so it tackles only a small part of the wider problem.

In my judgement, the advantages of regulation are strong here, because single-use plastic causes clear harm and has easy substitutes, so a ban can cut waste quickly. How large the benefit is depends on how strictly the ban is enforced and on whether small firms can find affordable alternatives. Regulation is likely to work best alongside other policies, such as taxes on plastic and fines for firms that break the rules.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines regulation and externality (AO2); chains from the ban to fewer items, less litter and emissions using 2.7 billion, 721 million and 10% (AO3); weighs costs to small firms, lost choice, enforcement and the 26% emissions context, ending in a judgement that depends on enforcement and affordable substitutes (AO4).

Assess · June 2022 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

  1. Explain pollution permits: a legal right to emit up to a limit, which firms can buy and sell.
  2. Link to data: 46 million tonnes (36%) of Colorado's pollution comes from 119 large polluters, including 35 power stations.
  3. For: firms that cut pollution can sell spare permits, a market incentive to switch away from coal.
  4. Against: hard to set the right number of permits; too many means little cut, too few may hurt output.

  6. Judge: effective if the cap is set tightly and monitored; may need fines or other measures too.
