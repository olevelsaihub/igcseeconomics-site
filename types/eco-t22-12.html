Knowledge card

Exchange rates: meaning and what moves a currency

Written
  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency expressed in terms of another currency (for example $1 = £0.80).
  • Like any price, it is set by demand for and supply of the currency. More demand raises the exchange rate; more supply lowers it.
  • Demand for a currency comes from foreigners buying the country's exports, foreign savers depositing money in its banks, foreign investment and speculators buying it.
  • Supply of a currency comes from residents buying imports, saving or investing abroad, and speculators or the government selling it.
  • Higher interest rates attract 'hot money' (savings that move between countries to earn the best return), so demand for the currency rises and it appreciates (rises in value). Lower interest rates do the opposite.
  • Currency speculators buy and sell currencies hoping to make a profit when exchange rates move; buying because they expect a rise pushes the rate up, selling pushes it down.
  • A government can hold its currency down by selling its own currency, or push it up by buying its own currency with its foreign currency reserves or raising interest rates.

Before you begin

  • Supply and demand setting a price.
  • What interest rates are and why savers prefer higher ones.
  • Imports and exports.

The method

  1. If asked for the meaning, say the price of one currency in terms of another.

    Why: Both parts of the definition earn a mark.

  2. Decide which side of the currency market the factor works on: demand (foreigners buying the currency) or supply (people selling it).

    Why: Correctly naming demand or supply is central to the marks.

  3. Say why the factor changes that side, e.g. higher interest rates give savers a better return, so they buy the currency to deposit it.

    Why: The development mark needs the reason for the change.

  4. State the effect on the exchange rate: appreciation (rise) or depreciation (fall).

    Why: The answer must end at the change in the currency's value.

  5. Apply it to the currency and figures named (the rand, the pound, a given interest rate).

    Why: Context earns a mark in Explain and AO3 in Analyse.

  6. For a 6-mark Analyse, follow the Analyse card: develop both the demand and supply effects, and any speculation, with the data.

    Why: Level 3 needs thorough, applied analysis.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Thinking higher interest rates make a currency fall; they usually raise demand for it.
  • ✗
    Mixing up demand and supply: residents buying imports adds to supply of their own currency, not demand.
  • ✗
    Saying a government stops appreciation by buying its own currency or raising interest rates; both push the rate up.
  • ✗
    Stopping at 'demand rises' without saying the exchange rate appreciates.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Data: in November 2018 South Africa raised interest rates by 0.25 percentage points to 6.75%.
  2. Demand side: foreign savers earn more in South African banks, so they buy rand to deposit; this is hot money.
  3. Supply side: South African savers have less reason to move money abroad, so fewer rand are sold.
  4. Result: higher demand and lower supply make the rand appreciate.
  5. Extra chain: speculators expecting a rise buy rand, adding to demand.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

When South Africa raised its interest rate to 6.75%, saving money in South African banks became more rewarding compared with saving in countries where rates are lower. Foreign savers and investors want this better return, so they move money into South Africa. To do this they must buy South African rand, so demand for the rand increases. Money that moves between countries in search of the highest interest rate is called hot money.

At the same time, South African savers have less reason to send their savings abroad, because they can now earn 6.75% at home. Fewer rand are exchanged for foreign currencies, so the supply of rand on the currency market falls.

With demand for the rand rising and supply falling, the price of the rand in terms of other currencies rises, so the rand appreciates. Currency speculators who expect this rise may also buy rand early to make a profit, which increases demand further and strengthens the appreciation. However, because the rise was only 0.25 percentage points, the size of the appreciation may be small.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked, 3 AO2 and 3 AO3. Level 3 needs a clear chain from higher interest rates to more saving in South Africa, higher demand (and lower supply) of the rand, and appreciation, using the 6.75% rate and the 0.25-point rise.
