External costs and external benefits

Spillover costs and benefits falling on third parties; standard examples (pollution, congestion, litter; education, healthcare, vaccination); the ways business activity damages the environment (air, water, noise and visual pollution); and judging whether an activity or public spending is worthwhile on externality grounds.

External costs and external benefits

Written

An externality is an effect on a third party, someone outside the deal. Harm such as pollution is an external cost; a gain such as less disease is an external benefit.

4

The method

  1. Define

    An effect on a third party outside the transaction.

  2. Name it

    Air, water, noise, visual pollution, congestion, or a spillover benefit.

  3. Who and how

    Say which activity causes it and who is affected.

  4. Wider effects

    Add health, productivity or government spending.

  5. Assess

    Weigh gains against cost and opportunity cost, then judge.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Any cost

    An external cost must fall on a third party.

  • ✗
    Rent or wages

    These are private costs.

  • ✗
    Wrong third party

    The buyer and seller are not third parties.

Written-answer check

After your attempt, see the full worked explanation

Worked explanation

  1. Define an external benefit.
  2. Private benefit to the vaccinated child.
  3. Spillover: fewer cases passed on, so the unvaccinated are protected too.
  4. Wider spillovers: parents' work time, NHS spending.
  5. Link to the NHS catch-up campaign.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

An external benefit is a positive effect of producing or consuming something that is enjoyed by a third party, someone who is not part of the decision. When a child receives the MMR vaccination, the child gains a private benefit: they are protected from measles, mumps and rubella. But the benefit spreads further. A vaccinated child is unlikely to catch these diseases, so they cannot pass them on to classmates, younger brothers and sisters, or adults they meet. The more children the NHS vaccinates through its 2019 catch-up campaign, the fewer people in England can spread the diseases, so even people who have not been vaccinated, such as babies too young for the injection, are less likely to fall ill. These people took no part in the decision to vaccinate, yet they gain, which is why vaccination is an external benefit. There are further spillovers. If fewer children catch measles, parents take less time off work to look after them, so firms lose less output, and the NHS spends less on treating outbreaks, which frees money for other healthcare. This explains why the NHS checked records and invited children who had missed the vaccination: the benefit to society is greater than the benefit to each child alone.

How the marks are earned

Level 3 (5-6): external benefit defined, the private benefit separated from the third-party benefit, the chain of reduced spread developed with the NHS campaign, and wider effects on work and NHS spending added.

Grade 9 long answers

Assess · June 2022 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define external cost and social cost = private cost + external cost.
  2. External: smoke and dust from 23 billion bricks a year worsen air pollution for children, the elderly and the sick.
  3. Chain: ill health means healthcare costs and lost work; rising demand from construction means more pollution.
  4. Not external: firms pay wages and materials; over one million people are employed and buyers gain buildings.
  5. Measurement: brick-making only contributes to the pollution, so its share of the harm is uncertain.
  6. Judge: largely external; depends on brick-making's share of pollution and how many of those affected also benefit.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

An external cost is a cost of producing or consuming a good that falls on a third party: someone who is not the buyer or the seller and who is not paid for the harm. The full cost to society is the private cost paid by the firm plus the external cost.

Many of the costs of brick-making in Bangladesh are external. About 23 billion bricks are made each year, and production releases smoke and dust clouds that add to the country's severe air pollution. People who breathe this air, such as children, the elderly, pregnant women and those with heart or breathing conditions, did not make or buy the bricks, yet they suffer poorer health. This is a clear external cost. Poor health can also mean more healthcare spending and lost working days, which reduces output. Because demand for bricks is rising with growth in construction, more bricks will be made, so the pollution and its external costs are likely to grow. Brick firms do not pay for this damage, so they have little reason to cut smoke and they produce more than is best for society.

However, not all the costs are external. The industry employs over one million people, who earn incomes from it, and many people in Bangladesh benefit from the buildings made with the bricks. Some of the people breathing the polluted air are workers or customers, so they are not pure third parties: they receive private benefits as well as suffering costs. It is also hard to measure the external cost: brick-making only contributes to the air pollution, so we cannot be sure how much of the ill health it causes.

Overall, a large part of the cost of producing bricks is external, because the health damage from smoke and dust falls mainly on people who are not paid for it, including vulnerable groups. The extent depends on how much of the air pollution comes from brick-making rather than other sources, and on how many of those affected also gain jobs or buildings from the industry.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines external and social cost (AO2); develops the chain smoke and dust to third-party health damage to healthcare costs and lost output, using 23 billion bricks and rising demand (AO3); counters with private costs, one million jobs and measurement problems, reaching a judgement on extent with clear conditions (AO4).

Assess · June 2019 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q2(g)

  1. Define social benefit = private benefit + external benefit; vaccination protects others from catching flu.
  2. For: free injections for older people, pregnant women and people with health problems prevent severe illness and death.
  3. For: preventing illness avoids later, costlier healthcare and keeps people able to work and produce.
  4. Against: over £100m a year has an opportunity cost, such as other healthcare needs.
  5. Against: the vaccine may not work against an unpredictable virus, and external benefits are hard to measure.
  6. Judge: justified if the healthcare costs and deaths avoided are worth more than over £100m a year.

Assess · January 2020 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

  1. Define externalities as spillover costs or benefits to third parties.
  2. Negative: loud bangs bring back war memories for former military personnel and frighten animals.
  3. Negative: displays happen for many festivals and events, so the noise can happen at many times of year.
  4. Positive: people outside paid venues can see the colourful sparks free, an external benefit.
  5. Some third parties feel both a benefit and a cost at different times.
  6. Judge: depends on how long displays last, the time of day and how close people and animals are.

Assess · June 2021 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

  1. Define social benefit = private benefit + external benefit; education spreads benefits to society.
  2. For: free university education gives students skills for higher-skilled, more productive jobs.
  3. For: Norway has one of the lowest unemployment rates, saving welfare spending and raising living standards.
  4. Against: Norway spends more on education as a share of GDP than any other country, an opportunity cost.
  5. Against: benefits take time, graduates may leave Norway, and external benefits are hard to value.
  6. Judge: worthwhile if graduates stay and find skilled jobs; depends on what else the money could fund.

Assess · June 2025 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

  1. Explain healthcare as having external benefits: healthy people help others and the economy.
  2. Data: 9.4% of the budget in 2021; per capita spending to rise from $114 to $269 by 2027.
  3. For: healthier workers are more productive, so firms can meet rising demand for Indonesian exports.
  4. Against: opportunity cost, such as education, and benefits take time as people return to work.
  5. Against: people may leave Indonesia, and the benefits are hard to measure in money.
  6. Judge: beneficial if spending improves health and jobs; depends on how well it is used and continued growth.
