Knowledge card

Inflation and deflation: meaning, measurement and causes

Written
  • Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level (the average price of goods and services) over time.
  • Deflation is a fall in the general price level over time.
  • The consumer price index (CPI) tracks the price of a typical 'basket' of goods and services that households buy; it is used to work out the inflation rate.
  • Demand-pull inflation: total spending in the economy grows faster than firms can supply, so prices are pulled up.
  • Cost-push inflation: firms' production costs rise (for example wages or raw material prices) and firms pass these on as higher prices.
  • Higher consumer spending or government spending tends to cause demand-pull; higher wages, raw material or energy costs cause cost-push. Higher productivity lowers costs, so it does not cause cost-push.

Before you begin

  • What demand and supply are
  • What firms' costs of production are (wages, materials, energy)
  • That a price index compares prices with a base year
1

The method

  1. Define using 'general price level': a sustained rise for inflation, a fall for deflation.

    Why: Definition marks need both the direction and the general price level idea.

  2. Ask whether the cause works through spending or through costs.

    Why: This sorts any cause into demand-pull or cost-push.

  3. For multiple choice, test each option: does it raise spending, raise costs, or reduce either?

    Why: Distractors often lower inflation (higher interest rates, higher productivity) or cause the other type.

  4. For analysis, trace the chain: the cause raises firms' costs or households' spending, firms raise prices, the general price level rises.

    Why: Analyse marks reward linked steps applied to the data.

  5. For a 6-mark Analyse, follow the Analyse card: two developed chains (often one cost-push and one demand-pull) using the figures given.

    Why: Showing both routes with data reaches the top level.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining inflation as 'prices go up' without the general price level or the idea of a sustained rise.
  • ✗
    Confusing deflation (falling prices) with disinflation or with a recession.
  • ✗
    Classifying a rise in wages as demand-pull only; it is mainly a cost-push cause.
  • ✗
    Thinking higher productivity or higher interest rates cause inflation; both tend to reduce it.
  • ✗
    In analysis, stating 'inflation rises' without showing how firms pass on costs or how extra spending outstrips supply.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the data: Mexico raised the minimum wage by 15% to 141.7 pesos; nearly 11 million workers earn the minimum wage.
  2. Chain 1 (cost-push): wages are a cost to firms, so a 15% rise raises costs; firms pass costs on as higher prices.
  3. Show scale: with 11 million workers affected, many firms raise prices together, so the general price level rises.
  4. Chain 2 (demand-pull): those workers now have 15% more income and spend more; if supply cannot keep up, prices are pulled up.
  5. Finish each chain with the type of inflation named.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Wages are one of the biggest costs for many firms. In Mexico the minimum wage rose by 15% to 141.7 pesos, and nearly 11 million workers are paid this rate. Firms such as shops, restaurants and factories that employ minimum-wage workers now face much higher labour costs. To protect their profits, many of them are likely to raise the prices of the goods and services they sell. Because so many workers and firms are affected at the same time, prices rise across the economy and the general price level goes up. This is cost-push inflation.

The wage rise also affects spending. The 11 million workers on the minimum wage now have 15% more income. Low-income workers tend to spend most of any extra income on goods and services such as food, clothes and transport. This raises total demand in the Mexican economy. If firms cannot increase output quickly enough to meet this extra demand, they will raise prices instead, which causes demand-pull inflation.

So a 15% rise in the minimum wage is likely to increase inflation in Mexico through two routes: higher costs for firms (cost-push) and higher spending by workers (demand-pull). The effect will be larger the more firms pass on their costs and the less able they are to raise output.

How the marks are earned

Levels marked out of 6 (3 AO2, 3 AO3). AO2 for correct cost-push and demand-pull chains from a wage rise to the general price level; AO3 for using the 15% rise and the 11 million workers throughout. Two developed, applied chains reach Level 3 (5-6).
