Knowledge card

Tariffs, quotas and subsidies: how they protect, gains and costs

Written
  • Tariff: a tax on imported goods. It raises the price of imports, so buyers switch to domestic goods; the government also collects revenue.
  • Quota: a physical limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported. Fewer imports leave more of the market for domestic producers.
  • Subsidy: a payment from the government to producers that lowers their costs. Domestic goods become cheaper at home and abroad, so sales and exports rise.
  • Supply diagrams: a new tariff or the removal of a subsidy shifts supply to the left (S to S1); a new subsidy or the removal of a tariff shifts supply to the right.
  • Benefits of protection: domestic firms gain market share and revenue, jobs are protected, imports fall and the current account can improve, and tariffs raise tax revenue.
  • Costs of protection: consumers pay higher prices and have less choice, domestic firms have less incentive to become efficient or innovate, other countries may retaliate (a trade war), and subsidies cost the government money that could be spent elsewhere.

Before you begin

  • Supply and demand diagrams and what shifts supply.
  • Imports, exports and the current account.
  • Costs, prices and market share.
  • Why governments protect trade.
1

The method

  1. Identify the instrument and define it in a few words (import tax, import limit, payment to producers).

    Why: The definition anchors the answer and is often the first credited point.

  2. Explain its direct effect: a tariff raises import prices, a quota cuts import quantity, a subsidy lowers domestic costs and prices.

    Why: This is the mechanism every chain starts from.

  3. Follow the effect to the result: domestic firms win market share and revenue, jobs are kept, exports rise or the trade deficit shrinks.

    Why: Developed consequences earn the development marks.

  4. Use the case numbers, such as the quota size, the tariff percentage or the value of the subsidy.

    Why: Context is a mark in Explain and AO3 in longer answers.

  5. For a 9-mark Assess, follow the Assess card: weigh the gains against higher prices, less efficiency, retaliation and cost to government, and judge.

    Why: AO4 needs a balanced, supported judgement.

  6. For a multiple-choice diagram, decide whether supply moves left (tariff, subsidy removed) or right (subsidy, tariff removed).

    Why: Only one diagram shows the correct supply shift.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Confusing a tariff (a tax) with a quota (a quantity limit) or a subsidy (a payment).
  • ✗
    Shifting supply the wrong way: treating a tariff as lowering supply costs or a subsidy as raising them.
  • ✗
    Saying subsidies reduce government spending; they increase it.
  • ✗
    Explaining motives for protection in general without saying how the named instrument works.
  • ✗
    In Assess answers, ignoring costs like higher consumer prices and retaliation.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Instrument: a quota, a limit on the quantity of imports.
  2. Data: Brazil only lets in 600 million litres of US ethanol a year.
  3. Advantage: Brazilian ethanol producers face less foreign competition.
  4. Develop: once the 600 million litres are sold, buyers must turn to domestic producers, raising their sales and market share.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

One advantage is that Brazil's own ethanol producers face less competition from imports. Because only 600 million litres of ethanol a year can come in from the US, any demand above that amount must be met by Brazilian producers. They therefore sell more ethanol, gain a bigger share of the Brazilian market and earn more revenue, which helps protect jobs in the industry.

How the marks are earned

1 mark: the advantage (less competition for domestic producers). 1 mark: development (demand above the limit goes to domestic firms, raising their sales and market share). 1 mark: context (the 600 million litre limit on US ethanol).

Assess · June 2024 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define a tariff as a tax on imports used to protect domestic producers.
  2. For: the rise from 0% to 16% makes imported tyres dearer, so demand switches to Brazilian tyres.
  3. For: domestic output recovers and jobs are protected, after warnings of stopped production; tariff revenue for government.
  4. Against: higher tyre prices for consumers and firms; less competition reduces efficiency and innovation.
  5. Against: trading partners may retaliate, harming Brazilian exports and overall trade.
  6. Judge: short-term benefit for jobs; lasting gain depends on firms becoming efficient and no retaliation.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A tariff is a tax placed on goods coming into the country. It is a method of protection, used to shield domestic producers from foreign competition. In March 2023 Brazil raised the import tariff on tyres from 0% to 16%.

The main benefit is protection of jobs. Brazilian tyre producers had seen production fall because of rising imports, and were warning they might stop production, causing job losses. The 16% tariff raises the price of imported tyres in Brazil. Consumers and firms buying tyres will switch towards cheaper domestic tyres, so demand for Brazilian tyres rises. Domestic producers can then increase output, keep their factories open and protect jobs. Those workers keep earning and spending, which supports other firms in the economy. Fewer imports may also improve Brazil's balance of trade. In addition, the government collects tax revenue from the tyres that are still imported, which it can use for public services.

However, the benefits may be limited. Imported tyres become more expensive, and domestic producers face less competition, so they may also raise their prices. Car owners, transport firms and businesses that use tyres pay more, which raises their costs and could push up prices elsewhere. With less competition, Brazilian tyre firms have less incentive to become efficient, improve quality or innovate, so they may stay uncompetitive. Other countries may retaliate with tariffs on Brazilian exports, which could cost jobs in exporting industries and reduce trade overall. Finally, if most tyres are imported, the jobs saved may be small compared with the cost to consumers.

Overall, the tariff can benefit the Brazilian economy in the short term by protecting tyre producers and their workers. Whether the benefit is lasting depends on whether domestic firms use the protection to become more efficient, on how much prices rise for tyre users, and on whether trading partners retaliate. If firms do not improve, the tariff may protect inefficiency at a cost to Brazilian consumers.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines a tariff and protection (AO2); chains the 0% to 16% tariff to dearer imports, switching demand, higher domestic output and saved jobs, plus revenue (AO3); weighs higher prices, reduced competition, inefficiency and retaliation, concluding with clear conditions on efficiency, prices and retaliation (AO4).

Assess · June 2024 · Paper 2 · Q2(g)

  1. Define a subsidy as a government payment to producers that lowers their costs.
  2. For: $369bn in grants, loans and tax credits lowers costs for battery, solar panel and wind turbine makers.
  3. For: new factories and millions of green jobs; cheaper US green goods may sell more abroad.
  4. Against: very costly, with an opportunity cost, and firms may come to depend on subsidies.
  5. Against: money may be poorly targeted; the EU and UK see harm and may retaliate with their own subsidies.
  6. Judge: benefits are likely for jobs and green output; depend on efficient use and the response of other countries.
