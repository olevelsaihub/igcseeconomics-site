Knowledge card

GDP and economic growth: meaning, uses and limits

Written
  • Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total market value of every final good and service a country produces in one year.
  • Economic growth is an increase in a country's output (real GDP) over a period of time.
  • Real GDP is GDP adjusted to remove the effect of inflation, so it shows the true change in output.
  • GDP is the standard measure of growth. CPI measures inflation, the ILO method measures unemployment, and FDI is foreign investment.
  • Strengths of GDP: every country collects it, so growth can be compared over time and between countries; rising GDP usually means rising incomes and living standards; it helps governments plan.
  • Limits of GDP: inflation can make nominal GDP look bigger than real output; the hidden (informal) economy and unpaid or subsistence work are left out; it ignores population size, how income is shared, and environmental damage.

Before you begin

  • What goods and services are
  • That inflation means prices are rising
  • What 'living standards' means: how well off people are

The method

  1. Start with the meaning: GDP is the value of output produced in a country in a year; growth is a rise in that output over time.

    Why: Definition marks need both the 'value of output' idea and the time idea.

  2. For a multiple-choice item, match each option to what it measures and cross out CPI, ILO and FDI.

    Why: The wrong options are usually real measures of other things.

  3. Decide which side the question wants: benefits (usefulness), limitations, or both.

    Why: Analyse questions usually ask for one side; Assess needs both sides.

  4. Pick a named strength or limitation and chain it: what it is, why it happens, what it means for judging growth or living standards.

    Why: Marks come from developing the point into its consequence, not from listing.

  5. Use the country and numbers given (growth rate, the main industries, the size of the hidden economy).

    Why: Application to the data is a separate source of marks in Explain, Analyse and Assess.

  6. For a 9-mark Assess, follow the Assess card: two developed strengths, two developed limits, then a judgement that says what the usefulness depends on.

    Why: The top level needs balance and a supported conclusion.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining GDP as goods traded, exported or spent rather than produced.
  • ✗
    Leaving out the time period ('in a year') in a definition.
  • ✗
    Choosing 'it is an accurate measure of all activity' as a benefit, when missing activity is actually a weakness.
  • ✗
    Listing limitations without explaining how each one distorts the picture of growth or living standards.
  • ✗
    Writing about GDP in general without using the named country's figures.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the data: Ghana's growth forecast was 8.8% in 2019, about twice that of other emerging economies; it was very poor 30 years ago; growth now relies on crude oil as well as cocoa.
  2. Define GDP briefly so the examiner sees you know what is being measured.
  3. Strength 1: one common measure lets Ghana be compared with other countries and with its own past; use the 8.8% and 'double' figures.
  4. Strength 2: rising GDP usually means more income and jobs, so it signals better living standards for people who were very poor.
  5. Limit 1: inflation and the hidden economy can make the figure wrong in either direction; only real GDP shows true growth.
  6. Limit 2: GDP ignores who receives the income and the environmental cost of oil extraction.
  7. Judge: GDP is a useful first indicator but should be read alongside other information; say what it depends on.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

GDP is the total value of the final goods and services that Ghana produces in a year, and economic growth is measured by the rise in this figure. One reason GDP is a useful measure is that almost every country calculates it in the same way. This lets us compare Ghana with other economies: its forecast growth of 8.8% in 2019 was about twice that of other emerging economies, which shows clearly that Ghana was growing faster than similar countries, and also how far it had come since the near collapse of 30 years ago. A second strength is that GDP gives a signal about living standards. If Ghana produces more cocoa and oil, firms earn more and employ more people, so household incomes tend to rise. Rapid GDP growth therefore suggests that many Ghanaians may be able to afford more food, housing and education.

However, GDP has important limits. First, if prices in Ghana are rising quickly, nominal GDP can grow even when the quantity of output has hardly changed, so growth looks bigger than it is unless real GDP is used. Second, much work in a country like Ghana happens in the informal economy, such as small street traders or subsistence farmers who grow their own food. This output is not recorded, so GDP may understate what the economy really produces. Third, GDP says nothing about how the extra income is shared. Much of the growth comes from crude oil, which may benefit oil companies and a small number of workers, while many people stay in poverty. Oil extraction can also cause pollution and damage to land, a cost to wellbeing that GDP does not subtract.

Overall, GDP is a good starting point for judging Ghana's growth because it is widely available and easy to compare, and an 8.8% rise is strong evidence that output is increasing. But it is not enough on its own to judge whether Ghanaians are better off. How useful it is depends on whether real figures are used and how large the informal economy is, and it should be read alongside measures of income distribution, poverty and environmental damage.

How the marks are earned

Levels marked out of 9 (3 AO2, 3 AO3, 3 AO4). Accurate knowledge of GDP and developed strengths earn AO2; using Ghana's 8.8%, 'double' and oil dependence earns AO3; developed limitations plus a judgement on what usefulness depends on earn AO4. Balanced, applied, judged answers reach Level 3 (7-9).

Assess · November 2021 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define GDP and economic growth as a rise in GDP over time.
  2. For: GDP allows comparison; Ghana's 8.8% growth is double emerging economies and ahead of the world.
  3. For: rising GDP suggests higher incomes and living standards after recovery from crisis.
  4. Against: inflation distorts money GDP, and the informal economy is left out.
  5. Against: oil-led growth may be unevenly shared and cause environmental costs; the figure is an estimate.
  6. Judge: useful for comparison but incomplete; depends on using real GDP and other indicators such as poverty.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Gross domestic product (GDP) measures the total value of the final goods and services a country produces in a year. Economic growth is an increase in GDP over time, so the GDP growth rate is the usual way to measure growth.

GDP is a useful measure for Ghana. It is calculated in a similar way all over the world, so Ghana's growth can be compared with other countries and over time. The IMF estimates Ghana's GDP growth rate at 8.8%, double that of emerging economies and well ahead of world growth, and predicted Ghana to be the world's fastest growing economy in 2019. This shows clearly how far Ghana has recovered since it was in crisis just 30 years ago. Rising GDP usually means more output and higher incomes, so it can indicate that living standards are improving, which helps the government and investors judge how the economy is doing.

However, GDP has limitations. If prices are rising, money GDP can grow even when output does not, so only real GDP, adjusted for inflation, shows true growth. GDP does not include the hidden or informal economy, such as unrecorded trading, so it may understate output. It also says nothing about how income is shared: much of the growth now comes from crude oil extraction, and the gains may go to a small number of firms and workers, so many people could still live in poverty. GDP ignores external costs, such as environmental damage from oil extraction, which reduce people's wellbeing. Finally, the 8.8% figure is an estimate and forecast, so it may turn out to be wrong, and relying on one commodity such as oil makes growth unstable if its price falls.

Overall, GDP is a useful starting point for measuring Ghana's growth, especially for comparing it with other countries. However, it does not show the whole picture. How useful it is depends on whether real rather than money figures are used, how large the informal economy is, and how widely the benefits of oil-led growth are shared. It is best used alongside other indicators, such as poverty rates.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines GDP and growth (AO2); applies the 8.8% rate, double emerging economies, and the recovery to show comparison and living-standard uses (AO3); weighs inflation, informal economy, distribution, oil's environmental costs and forecast reliability, concluding with clear conditions (AO4).
