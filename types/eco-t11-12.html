The method

  1. Define PED

    How strongly quantity demanded reacts to price.

  2. Classify the value

    0, below 1, exactly 1, above 1, infinity.

  3. Apply a determinant

    Substitutes, necessity, income share, time.

  4. Link to the decision

    Revenue, pricing or what to tax.

Worked explanation

  1. Define PED and inelastic demand.
  2. Determinant 1: few close substitutes, because of the sea and mountain views.
  3. Use the data: customers already pay €2.00 against €0.80 elsewhere.
  4. Determinant 2: coffee is a small share of income.
  5. Conclude: a price rise causes a smaller percentage fall in sales, so revenue could rise.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how much the quantity demanded responds to a change in price. Demand is price inelastic when the percentage change in quantity demanded is smaller than the percentage change in price, so the PED is between 0 and 1 if we ignore the minus sign. Demand at Marina Café is likely to be fairly inelastic for several reasons. First, it has few close substitutes. Other cafés in Budva sell the same brand of coffee, but not beside the sea with views of the mountains. Customers are buying the coffee and the setting together, and that setting is hard to find elsewhere, so they are unlikely to switch if the café raises its price. The data supports this: many customers already pay €2.00 a cup when the same coffee costs €0.80 elsewhere, which is €1.20 or 150% more. Second, a cup of coffee is only a small part of a tourist's or local person's income, so a small price rise hardly affects their budget and they barely cut how much they buy. Third, for tourists the visit is a special part of their holiday, so the experience matters more to them than the price. Because of these factors, a price rise at Marina Café would cause a smaller percentage fall in the number of coffees sold, so demand is relatively price inelastic and the café could increase its total revenue by raising its price.

How the marks are earned

Level 3 (5-6): PED and inelastic demand defined, determinants (few substitutes, small share of income) applied to the café, the €2.00 versus €0.80 data used, and a link to what a price rise does to sales and revenue.

Calculation check

  • 2.00-0.80 = 1.2
  • (2.00-0.80) ÷ 0.80 × 100 = 150.0

Assess · June 2024 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define PED, inelastic demand and total revenue (price x quantity).
  2. For: celebrity link and popularity with teenagers suggest inelastic demand with few substitutes.
  3. For: prices of £8.25 to £19.99 against £1.99 recommended show buyers kept paying, so revenue rose.
  4. Against: availability was not guaranteed; no stock means no revenue.
  5. Against: demand becomes more elastic as the craze fades or substitutes appear; very high prices are unaffordable for teenagers.
  6. Judge: higher revenue in the short term only; depends on stock, popularity and size of the price rise.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how much the quantity demanded responds to a change in price. Demand is price inelastic when the percentage fall in quantity is smaller than the percentage rise in price. Total revenue is price multiplied by quantity sold. If demand is inelastic, a price rise increases total revenue; if demand is elastic, a price rise reduces it.

Demand for Prime Hydration at launch was probably price inelastic. It was linked to the YouTube celebrities KSI and Logan Paul and was instantly very popular, especially with teenagers. Fans saw it as special, so they felt there were few close substitutes, and one bottle is a small part of most people's spending. This helps explain why sellers on Amazon could charge between £8.25 and £19.99 when the recommended retail price was £1.99. If fans kept buying at these higher prices, the quantity sold fell by a smaller percentage than the rise in price, so retailers' total revenue increased.

However, this will not always happen. First, revenue needs sales: availability was not guaranteed, so if a retailer has no stock, a higher price brings no revenue at all. Second, PED changes over time. The drink was a new craze; as the excitement fades, or as supply increases and other drinks copy it, consumers will see more substitutes and demand will become more elastic. Then a price rise would cause a large fall in sales, and total revenue would fall. Third, it depends on the size of the price rise. Teenagers have limited incomes, so at a very high price such as £19.99 many cannot afford the drink or do not think it is worth the money. Demand becomes more elastic at higher prices.

Overall, a price increase is likely to raise total revenue for retailers in the short term, while Prime is a scarce, fashionable product with inelastic demand, but it is not always true. It depends on whether retailers have stock to sell, how long the drink stays popular and how large the price increase is.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines PED, inelastic demand and total revenue and links them (AO2); explains why demand was inelastic using the celebrity link and the £1.99 versus £8.25 to £19.99 prices (AO3); counters with availability, PED changing over time and size of rise, with a short-term judgement and conditions (AO4).

Evaluate · January 2020 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define PED and VAT as an indirect tax that raises prices.
  2. Useful: inelastic demand means a VAT rise raises revenue; elastic demand means sales fall and revenue may not rise.
  3. Data: VAT rose from 14% to 15% in 2018 and spending and confidence fell, hinting at elastic demand.
  4. Useful: necessities like fruit, vegetables and bread are inelastic, so PED shows who bears the tax.
  5. Limits: PED is an estimate, differs across many goods, other factors affected spending, and the size of the rise matters.
  6. Judge: useful but not enough alone; depends on accurate estimates and wider goals such as fairness and confidence.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how much the quantity demanded responds to a change in price. It is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. VAT is an indirect tax added to the price of most food and household goods, so a higher VAT rate raises prices.

PED is useful to the South African Government because it shows what will happen to sales and to tax revenue. If demand for a good is price inelastic, a VAT rise increases the price but sales fall only a little, so the government collects more revenue. If demand is price elastic, sales fall by a larger percentage than the price rises, so revenue from that good may rise little or even fall, and firms lose sales. After VAT rose from 14% to 15% on 1st April 2018, consumer spending and business confidence fell. This suggests that demand for many taxed goods responded strongly, so another rise could raise less revenue than hoped and harm firms and jobs. By estimating PED for different goods, the government can predict both the revenue gain and the fall in spending before it decides.

PED also helps with fairness. Fruit, vegetables and bread are not taxed. These are necessities with inelastic demand, so taxing them would raise revenue, but it would hit poorer households hardest, because they spend a large share of their income on food. PED therefore helps the government see who would bear the burden of the tax.

However, PED has limits. It is only an estimate based on past behaviour, and consumer behaviour can change, so predictions may be wrong. Many different goods are taxed and each has a different PED, so the government would need many calculations. The fall in spending after 2018 may not have been caused by VAT alone; other factors may have played a part, so past data could mislead. The result also depends on the size of the rise: another small rise would have a different effect from a large one. Finally, the government must think about more than revenue, such as inflation, living standards and how much money it needs for its spending.

In conclusion, PED is a useful tool for this decision, because it predicts how sales and revenue will respond to a VAT rise, and the fall in spending after 2018 shows this matters. However, it should not be the only factor. Its usefulness depends on how accurate the estimates are and on whether the government also considers fairness, business confidence and wider economic conditions.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines PED and VAT (AO2); chains elastic versus inelastic demand to sales and revenue, applied to the rise from 14% to 15% and the fall in spending and confidence (AO3); weighs estimate accuracy, many goods, other causes and size of rise, with a supported conclusion and conditions (AO4).
