Knowledge card

Minimum wage: reasons, benefits and costs

Written
  • A minimum wage is the lowest amount an employer is legally allowed to pay a worker (per hour or per month). It is a type of government intervention.
  • Reasons for it: raise the incomes and living standards of the lowest-paid, reduce the gap between rich and poor, and reduce the need for government benefits.
  • Benefits: workers have more disposable income to spend, which raises firms' revenue; better-paid workers may be more motivated and productive; the government may collect more tax.
  • Costs: firms' wage costs rise, which may cut profits, raise prices or lead firms to employ fewer workers.
  • If set above the equilibrium wage, the quantity of labour demanded falls and the quantity supplied rises, creating a surplus of workers (unemployment).
  • Effects depend on how far above equilibrium the minimum is, whether productivity rises, and whether firms can raise prices (price elasticity of demand).

Before you begin

  • The labour market diagram (demand and supply of labour, equilibrium wage).
  • What disposable income and standard of living mean.
  • How to calculate a percentage change.
1

The method

  1. Define it: the lowest wage that can legally be paid.

    Why: Knowledge marks start with the legal floor idea.

  2. Use the case's numbers: old and new minimum, percentage rise, share of workers paid it.

    Why: Application marks reward using the data, ideally with a calculated change.

  3. Benefit chains: higher income, better living standards and more spending; higher motivation and productivity; more tax revenue.

    Why: Analysis marks reward developed, linked effects.

  4. Cost chains: higher wage bills, fewer workers hired or hours cut, higher prices, lower profits; possible unemployment.

    Why: Long answers need the other side for evaluation marks.

  5. For 9-mark Assess or 12-mark Evaluate, follow those cards and judge using 'it depends on' factors: size of rise, productivity, price elasticity, share of workers affected, short versus long run.

    Why: A supported conclusion is needed for top levels.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Assuming a minimum wage always causes unemployment, ignoring productivity gains and the size of the rise.
  • ✗
    Saying it benefits firms without explaining how (e.g. more spending or higher motivation).
  • ✗
    Not using the case figures, or miscalculating the percentage increase.
  • ✗
    Mixing up quantity of labour demanded and supplied when the wage is above equilibrium.
  • ✗
    Ending an Evaluate without a judgement on who benefits overall.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Define minimum wage and calculate the rise: from 620 to 700 euros a month is 80 euros, about 12.9%.
  2. Benefits to workers: more income, better living standards, more motivation.
  3. Benefits to firms and government: more spending raises revenue; more tax collected.
  4. Costs: firms that cannot afford it may hire fewer workers; unemployed workers and other firms lose out.
  5. Use the 16% figure and 'it depends' factors to judge whether everyone benefits.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A minimum wage is the lowest wage employers are legally allowed to pay. In Latvia it rose from 620 to 700 euros a month in 2024, an increase of 80 euros, or about 12.9%. For the lowest-paid workers this is a large rise in income, so they can afford more food, housing and other essentials, improving their standard of living. Feeling better rewarded may also make them more motivated, so they work harder, raising productivity and helping firms to cover part of the extra wage cost. Because low-paid workers tend to spend most of any extra income, demand for goods and services in Latvia should rise, increasing the revenue of shops and other firms. Higher incomes also mean more income tax and spending taxes for the Latvian Government, which could fund public services. However, the increase may not benefit everyone. Firms' wage costs rise, and those that cannot raise productivity or prices may employ fewer workers or cut hours. If the minimum wage is pushed further above the equilibrium wage, the quantity of labour demanded falls while more people want to work, so unemployment can rise. Workers who lose their jobs are clearly worse off, and if unemployment rises, total spending and tax revenue may fall, harming other firms too. Firms selling goods with price-elastic demand will find it hard to pass on the cost without losing sales. On balance, the increase is likely to benefit many people, but not everyone. Only 16% of the population are now paid the minimum, a five-year low, so many workers are already paid above the old minimum, so for some firms the extra cost may be small. This makes large job losses less likely. The overall effect depends on whether productivity rises and whether firms can pass on costs; in the short run some small firms and their workers may lose out, but in the long run higher spending may leave the economy as a whole better off.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked out of 12 (4 application, 4 analysis, 4 evaluation). The definition and calculated 12.9% rise apply the data; chains on living standards, productivity, spending and tax give analysis; job losses, falling spending and price elasticity, with a judgement using the 16% figure and short versus long run, give Level 3.

Calculation check

  • 700-620 = 80
  • round((700-620) ÷ 620 × 100, 1) = 12.9

Assess · June 2024 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define minimum wage; data: up 10.15% to EUR 1 934.40 a month in January 2023.
  2. Employees: more disposable income, helps cope with 17% inflation in 2022, better living standards.
  3. Firms: motivated staff, higher productivity, lower turnover; more consumer spending raises revenue.
  4. Against: weak economy after the health crisis -> firms cannot afford costs -> job cuts and less spending; 10.15% is below 17% inflation, so real wages may still fall.
  5. Judgement: employees who keep jobs gain; firms' benefits uncertain; depends on productivity, strength of economy and inflation.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A minimum wage is the lowest wage that firms are legally allowed to pay their workers. In January 2023, the Netherlands raised its minimum wage by 10.15% to €1 934.40 per month.

For employees, the main benefit is higher income. Workers on the minimum wage now earn 10.15% more each month, so they have more disposable income to spend on food, rent and energy. This matters because inflation rose to 17% during 2022, which cut the real value of wages. The increase helps low-paid workers keep up with rising prices and reduces relative poverty. Better pay may also make working more attractive, so more people look for jobs.

For firms, there can also be benefits. Better-paid workers may feel more valued and motivated, so productivity can rise, which partly covers the higher wage cost. Staff turnover may fall, reducing the cost of recruiting and training new workers. In addition, low-paid workers spend most of any extra income, so demand for goods and services rises and firms such as shops and restaurants earn more revenue.

However, these benefits may be limited. The 10.15% rise is smaller than the 17% inflation in 2022, so many workers may still be worse off in real terms. For firms, wages are a cost of production. The data says the economy is still weak after the global health crisis, so many firms may not be able to afford higher wage costs. They might cut jobs, reduce hours or hire fewer new workers, so the demand for labour falls and unemployment rises. Workers who lose their jobs are clearly worse off. Firms may also pass higher costs on to customers as higher prices, adding to inflation.

Overall, the increase is likely to benefit employees who keep their jobs, but the benefits for firms are much less certain. The final effect depends on how weak the economy is, whether productivity rises enough to cover the higher wages, and how quickly inflation falls.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines minimum wage, applies the 10.15%, the new monthly wage and 17% inflation in chains for both employees and firms, weighs real-wage erosion and job losses in a weak economy, and reaches a balanced judgement that depends on productivity, the state of the economy and inflation.

Evaluate · June 2025 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define minimum wage; data: up from EUR 620 to EUR 700 per month in 2024; prices of food, energy and clothing had risen.
  2. For: low-paid workers gain income and pay bills; more spending -> firm revenue, multiplier; more tax revenue for the government.
  3. For: motivation and productivity may rise; lower staff turnover.
  4. Against: higher costs -> job cuts or fewer hires (diagram: wage above equilibrium), higher prices hurt all consumers; workers above EUR 700 gain nothing.
  5. Evidence: only 16% paid the minimum wage, lowest for five years, so cost impact may be modest.
  6. Judgement: not everyone benefits; overall likely positive; depends on productivity and whether firms cut jobs or raise prices.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A minimum wage is the lowest wage that firms are legally allowed to pay. In 2024, Latvia raised its national minimum wage from €620 to €700 per month.

Workers on the minimum wage gain most. Their monthly pay rises from €620 to €700, giving them more disposable income. This matters because many people had been struggling to pay bills after rises in the prices of food, energy and clothing. Higher pay protects their real income and living standards and may reduce poverty.

Firms may also gain. Better-paid workers may be more motivated, so productivity rises and helps to cover the higher wage cost, and staff turnover may fall. Low-income households spend most of any extra income, so demand for goods and services rises and firms' revenues increase, creating a multiplier effect. Higher incomes and spending also raise tax revenue, giving the government more money for public services.

However, not everyone benefits. For firms, wages are a cost. If they cannot afford the higher wage, they may cut jobs or hours, or hire fewer workers. On a labour market diagram, a minimum wage set above the equilibrium wage raises the quantity of labour supplied but reduces the quantity demanded, which creates unemployment. Workers who lose their jobs, often young or low-skilled, are worse off, and fewer jobs means less spending and lower tax revenue. Firms may also raise prices to cover costs, which adds to inflation and hurts all consumers at a time when food, energy and clothing are already more expensive. Workers who already earn more than €700 gain nothing and may face higher prices.

The size of these effects depends on how many workers are affected. Only 16% of the population were paid the minimum wage, the lowest percentage for five years. This suggests that most firms already pay above the minimum, so the rise in total costs may be modest and job losses small.

In conclusion, the increase will not benefit everyone. It helps low-paid workers who keep their jobs, but it may hurt firms with many low-paid staff, workers who lose their jobs and consumers who face higher prices. Overall it is likely to do more good than harm in Latvia because only 16% are directly affected. This depends on whether firms can raise productivity, or raise prices without losing sales, rather than cutting jobs.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines minimum wage, applies the change from 620 to 700 euros, the cost-of-living pressures and the 16% figure; develops chains for workers, firms and government; uses diagram reasoning for unemployment; considers who loses; supported conclusion on 'everyone' with clear conditions.

Evaluate · June 2021 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

  1. Define minimum wage; data: monthly wage rose around 22% in 2019 to EUR 900.00, compared with only a 4% rise the year before.
  2. For: workers' incomes rise sharply -> more spending -> higher firm revenue, multiplier, more tax revenue; fits the aim that a rich country should not have poor workers.
  3. For: motivation and productivity may rise, offsetting some of the higher wage cost.
  4. Against: firms' costs jump after years of small rises (EUR 633.30 in 2010 to EUR 735.90 in 2018) -> job cuts, unemployment, higher prices; diagram: wage above equilibrium cuts quantity of labour demanded.
  5. Judgement: benefits depend on how many workers are affected, the size of the rise, productivity and whether firms can pass on costs; may be harder short term, better long term.

Assess · November 2025 · Paper 1 · Q2(g)

  1. Define minimum wage; data: 75 KWD (about $243) a month in Kuwait in 2024.
  2. Effect: if 75 KWD is above the equilibrium wage, labour costs rise -> firms demand fewer workers (diagram: quantity of labour demanded falls from Q1 to Q2) -> unemployment and lower profit.
  3. Other side: many firms may already pay more than 75 KWD, so their costs and demand for labour are unchanged.
  4. Other side: higher pay may raise productivity and spending, supporting demand for labour.
  5. Judgement: effect depends on how many firms paid below 75 KWD before and how far the minimum is above equilibrium.
