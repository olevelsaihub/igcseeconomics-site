What shifts the supply curve

Written

Supply shifts when something other than the good's own price changes costs or how much can be made. Right: more at every price. Left: less.

The method

  1. Find the factor

    Weather, technology, tax, subsidy, costs.

  2. Link to costs

    Say how it changes cost or output.

  3. Give the direction

    Right or left.

  4. Price and quantity

    Right: price down, quantity up.

  5. Use the figures

    Quote the case data.

Written-answer check

After your attempt, see the full worked explanation

Worked explanation

  1. Define what a supply curve shows.
  2. Identify the factor: better weather after years of heatwaves and drought.
  3. Explain lower cost per tonne and bigger harvests, so supply shifts right.
  4. Use the data: Greece up 100,000 tonnes; price expected to fall by at least half.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The supply curve shows how much producers are willing and able to sell at each price. Natural factors such as the weather can shift the whole curve, because they change how much can be grown and how much it costs. After years of heatwaves and droughts, Greece, Spain and Tunisia now expect better weather and improved olive harvests. With more olives from each tree, producers can make more olive oil from the same land and workers, so the cost of producing each tonne falls. Producers are therefore willing to supply more olive oil at every price, and the supply curve shifts to the right from S to S1. Greece alone expects output to rise from 130,000 tonnes to 230,000 tonnes, an increase of 100,000 tonnes, and Spain and Tunisia also forecast large rises. If demand stays the same, there is now excess supply at the old price, so sellers cut their prices: the equilibrium price falls from Pe to P1 and the equilibrium quantity rises from Qe to Q1. The data supports this, as the price is expected to fall from a peak of €10 000 per tonne in 2024 to somewhere from €4 000 to €5 000 a tonne in 2025, a fall of at least 50%.

How the marks are earned

Level 3 (5-6): supply curve explained, weather linked to lower costs and higher output, a right shift, then lower price and higher quantity supported by the 100,000-tonne rise and the fall of at least 50% in price.

Calculation check

  • 230000-130000 = 100000
  • (10000-5000) ÷ 10000 × 100 = 50.0

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Assess · June 2019 · Paper 1 · Q2(h)

Answer plan

  1. Define a shortage (excess demand) and identify technology as a cause of a shift in supply.
  2. Data: lack of space in busy cities; Japan's APS stacks cars on racks so many fit in a small area; cheaper to build than multi-storey car parks; cheaper fees and time saved for drivers.
  3. Chain: more spaces per area and lower building costs -> supply curve shifts right -> new equilibrium with lower price and higher quantity -> shortage narrows (draw the diagram).
  4. Against: time lag in building; growing car numbers and cheaper fees shift demand right too; distrust of technology and breakdowns; land still needed.
  5. Judgement: reduces the shortage to a significant extent; depends on build speed relative to growth in demand for parking.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A shortage happens when quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied at the current price. Busy city centres lack space and parking is an increasing problem, so demand for spaces is greater than supply.

Technology is a cause of a shift in supply. Automated Parking Systems (APS), developed in Japan, stack cars on racks one above the other, so many cars fit into a very small area. More spaces can therefore be supplied on the same piece of land, so the supply curve for parking spaces shifts to the right. APS also cost less to build than traditional multi-storey car parks. Lower costs of production mean firms can afford to build more car parks, which shifts supply further to the right. As supply rises, the gap between demand and supply narrows, so the shortage falls. On a diagram, the new equilibrium has a lower price and a higher quantity, which matches the cheaper parking fees drivers enjoy. Drivers also save time, so city centres work more efficiently.

However, technology may reduce the shortage only to a limited extent. Building APS takes time: planning, finance and construction mean the supply curve cannot shift straight away. If the number of cars in cities keeps growing, demand also shifts to the right and could grow faster than supply, so a shortage remains. Cheaper fees may even encourage more people to drive into the city, raising demand. Some drivers may not trust the technology, fearing breakdowns or damage to their cars, so they may avoid APS, and if a machine fails its spaces cannot be used at all. Even an APS needs some land, which may not be available in the most crowded centres.

Overall, changes in technology such as APS are likely to reduce the shortage of parking spaces to a significant extent, because they raise supply on very little land at lower cost. The extent depends on how quickly APS can be built and whether the growth in car numbers is slower than the growth in spaces. Alongside other policies, such as better public transport, the shortage could be greatly reduced.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines a shortage and applies APS stacking, lower build costs and cheaper fees in developed supply-shift chains to a new equilibrium; balances with time lag, rising demand, induced driving, distrust and breakdowns; judgement on the extent depends on build speed and growth in car numbers.
