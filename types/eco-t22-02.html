Who gains and who loses from globalisation

The gains and costs of globalisation for different groups: living standards, choice, prices, communication, closure of traditional industries, environmental harm and dependence on world markets.

Knowledge card

Who gains and who loses from globalisation

Written
  • Globalisation: the world's economies becoming more connected and dependent on one another through trade, investment and firms working across borders.
  • Consumers can gain: wider choice (goods from other countries, even out of season), lower prices from more competition and economies of scale (lower cost per unit when producing more), and higher living standards (the quality of life people can afford).
  • Producers can gain: new overseas markets, more sales revenue, cheaper labour and raw materials from abroad, and lower costs per unit.
  • Workers and countries can gain: more jobs in export industries, higher incomes, more tax revenue for government and faster economic growth (a rise in the output of the economy).
  • Possible costs: traditional industries close because they cannot compete, jobs are lost in some regions, unsafe products may enter, pollution from production and transport rises, and some workers face low pay and poor conditions.
  • Dependence risk: a country relying on one export (for example one crop) suffers if world prices or demand fall.
  • Gains are often unevenly shared, for example when farms are foreign-owned or large buyers keep most of the value.

Before you begin

  • What globalisation means.
  • Basic supply and demand: more competition tends to lower prices.
  • Economies of scale: cost per unit falls as output rises.
  • Meaning of living standards and economic growth.
1

The method

  1. Underline the group the question is about.

    Why: An effect on producers earns nothing if the question asks about consumers.

  2. Name a specific effect on that group (wider choice, lower prices, new markets, job losses, pollution).

    Why: The first mark in any form is for a relevant, specific effect.

  3. Explain how globalisation causes it, step by step (more competition leads to lower prices leads to higher purchasing power).

    Why: Development marks and higher levels need a chain of reasoning.

  4. Use the case material: the product, country and any figures given.

    Why: Context earns a mark in Explain and is AO3 in longer answers.

  5. For a 6-mark Analyse, follow the Analyse card: two or three developed chains on the named group only.

    Why: Level 3 needs thorough, organised analysis applied to the data.

  6. For a 12-mark Evaluate, follow the Evaluate card: develop the benefits, then real counter-points (price swings, dependence, environment, uneven sharing), then a conclusion saying what the outcome depends on.

    Why: AO4 marks need balance and a supported judgement.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Writing about the wrong group, such as benefits to firms when the question asks about consumers.
  • ✗
    Choosing 'higher prices' or 'reduced choice' as an impact of globalisation in multiple choice.
  • ✗
    Listing many benefits in single sentences without developing any of them.
  • ✗
    In a 12-mark Evaluate, giving only benefits, or ending with a conclusion that just repeats points without saying what the result depends on.
  • ✗
    Not using the case details, such as the named country or product.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

State1One word-level impact on the group named, such as greater choice, lower prices or higher living standards.
Explain1One effect on the named group, a reason it happens, and a link to the product or country in the case.

What a complete response includes

Credit goes to a specific effect on the named group, a developed explanation of why globalisation causes it, use of the case, and, in evaluations, real counter-arguments and a reasoned judgement.

Worked explanation

  1. Group: consumers. Data: global trade hit a record $29 trillion in 2022 and is still growing, more slowly since the health crisis.
  2. Chain 1: more imports widen choice, including goods not made at home.
  3. Chain 2: more competition and larger-scale production push down costs and prices.
  4. Chain 3: lower prices raise what incomes can buy, lifting living standards.
  5. Chain 4 (optional): technology spreads between countries, bringing new products to consumers.
  6. Tie each chain back to the record level of trade in the data.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Globalisation means consumers can buy far more goods from other countries. With world trade at a record $29 trillion in 2022, shops can stock products that are not made at home, such as tropical fruit, foreign cars or online services, so consumers have much greater choice and can find products that suit their tastes better.

Second, more trade means domestic firms face competition from foreign producers. To keep customers they must cut costs and prices. Firms selling to a world market also produce on a very large scale, gaining economies of scale, so the cost of making each item falls and some of this is passed on as lower prices for items like clothes and electronics.

Third, because prices are lower, each dollar of a consumer's income buys more. Their real purchasing power rises, so they can afford more goods and services and their standard of living improves.

Finally, globalisation spreads technology between countries. New products such as smartphones and online shopping reach consumers more quickly than if each country had to invent them itself. Although trade growth has slowed since the health crisis, the continued rise in trade means these benefits keep reaching more consumers.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked, 3 AO2 and 3 AO3. Level 3 (5-6) needs several clearly developed chains about consumers (choice, lower prices through competition and scale, higher living standards, new technology), accurate terms, and the trade data used to ground the argument. This answer gives four developed chains tied to the record trade figure.

Evaluate · June 2025 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define globalisation; Ghana is the second-largest cocoa producer, cocoa is 3.5% of GDP and almost all is exported for over $2bn a year.
  2. Chain: access to world markets and rising chocolate demand -> higher demand and prices for cocoa -> higher farm revenue; foreign investment -> better methods and processing.
  3. Data against: 90% of farmers cannot afford basics; many of 867,000 farmers live on $2 a day while the four biggest chocolate makers raised profits by 16%.
  4. Chain against: few large buyers have power -> farmers get a small share; volatile world prices; dependence on one crop; competition from advanced producers.
  5. Judgement: potential benefits but small so far; depends on how gains are shared (cooperatives, fair trade, processing in Ghana).
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Globalisation is the growing integration of the world's economies through trade, investment and the movement of ideas and people. For Ghana, it means cocoa farmers can sell to buyers all over the world.

Globalisation brings possible benefits to cocoa farmers. Ghana is the world's second-largest producer of cocoa, and almost all of it is exported, earning more than $2bn a year in foreign exchange. Without access to world markets, farmers could sell only to the small home market, so global trade gives them far greater demand. As world demand for chocolate has risen, demand for cocoa should rise too, which can push up its price and raise farmers' revenues. Cocoa makes up 3.5% of GDP, so higher export earnings also raise national income and tax revenue, which the government could spend on roads, schools and health care that help farmers. Links with foreign firms may also bring investment in processing and better farming methods and tools, so farmers can grow more and better cocoa on each farm and earn more.

However, the data suggest farmers gain little in practice. Oxfam says 90% of Ghana's cocoa farmers do not earn enough to afford food, clothing, housing or medical care, and many of the 867,000 farmers survive on just $2 a day. Meanwhile, Hershey, Lindt, Mondelez and Nestle have increased their profits by 16%. This shows that most of the value from globalisation goes to the large chocolate makers, which have strong buying power because there are many small farmers and only a few big buyers. Farmers receive only a small share of the final price of chocolate. Globalisation also exposes farmers to volatile world prices: if world cocoa prices fall, their already low incomes fall further. Ghana's dependence on one export crop makes the whole economy vulnerable, and small farms may struggle to compete with larger, more advanced producers abroad.

Overall, globalisation offers cocoa farmers in Ghana possible benefits, mainly access to a huge global market, but so far these benefits seem small, as most farmers remain in poverty while the chocolate companies' profits grow. Whether farmers benefit depends on how the gains are shared: if farmers form cooperatives to bargain for better prices, secure fair trade deals, or process cocoa in Ghana to capture more of its value, globalisation could raise their incomes. Without this, the main benefits are likely to go to the multinational firms.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines globalisation and applies $2bn exports, 3.5% of GDP, 90% of farmers in poverty, 867,000 farmers on $2 a day and 16% profit growth in chains on demand, revenue and buyer power; balances with price volatility and dependence; judgement depends on how gains are shared.

Evaluate · January 2020 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

  1. Define globalisation as growing integration of economies through trade and investment.
  2. Data: Kenya is the third largest exporter of cut flowers, 1.3% of GDP; about 100,000 direct jobs and over 500,000 people benefit indirectly; a dedicated airport terminal; 127 flower farms, over half near Lake Naivasha.
  3. Chain: global demand -> exports -> jobs and incomes -> higher living standards, more aggregate demand and tax revenue; export earnings let Kenya import more goods.
  4. Against: heavy water use may damage Lake Naivasha (negative externality); pollution from air freight; dependence on one export if world demand falls.
  5. Against: profits may go to foreign owners; farm work may be low paid with long hours.
  6. Judgement: clear benefits in jobs and income; depends on the industry being sustainable and gains being shared with workers.
