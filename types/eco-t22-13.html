Knowledge card

Draw a change in a currency's exchange rate

Written
  • The currency diagram has the price of the currency (the exchange rate, e.g. price of £s in $) on the vertical axis and the quantity of the currency (e.g. quantity of £s) on the horizontal axis.
  • Demand for pounds comes from foreigners who need pounds: buying UK exports, saving in UK banks when interest rates rise, or speculators expecting the pound to rise.
  • Supply of pounds comes from UK residents swapping pounds for other currencies: buying imports or investing abroad.
  • More UK exports, higher UK interest rates or speculative buying: demand shifts RIGHT, so the exchange rate rises and quantity rises.
  • Fewer UK exports: demand shifts LEFT, so the exchange rate falls and quantity falls.
  • More UK imports: supply shifts RIGHT, so the exchange rate falls and quantity rises. Fewer UK imports: supply shifts LEFT, so the exchange rate rises and quantity falls.
  • Only one curve moves in each question.

Before you begin

  • How to read a supply and demand diagram.
  • What an exchange rate is.
  • Who demands and who supplies a currency.
  • Exports, imports and interest rates.
1

The method

  1. Ask who has to buy or sell the currency: foreigners buying it (exports, saving, speculation) means demand; residents selling it to buy foreign goods (imports) means supply.

    Why: The curve mark is only given if the correct curve moves.

  2. Decide the direction: more of the activity shifts the curve right, less shifts it left.

    Why: Direction is part of the same mark.

  3. Draw the new curve parallel to the old one and label it (D1 or S1).

    Why: The new curve must be labelled to earn the mark.

  4. From the new crossing point, draw a dashed line to the price axis and label the new exchange rate.

    Why: One mark is for the new equilibrium exchange rate, labelled.

  5. Draw a dashed line down to the quantity axis and label the new quantity.

    Why: One mark is for the new equilibrium quantity, labelled.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Shifting supply for a change in exports, or demand for a change in imports.
  • ✗
    Moving the curve the wrong way for a decrease in exports or imports.
  • ✗
    Using the original labels again so the new exchange rate or quantity cannot be identified.
  • ✗
    Forgetting to label the new curve.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Cause: more UK exports to the US.
  2. US buyers must pay in pounds, so they buy more pounds: demand for £ rises.
  3. Shift demand right from D to D1, labelled.
  4. New crossing point: exchange rate ER2 above ER1, quantity Q2 to the right of Q1.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Because US buyers need pounds to pay for more UK exports, demand for the pound increases. I draw a new demand curve D1 to the right of the original demand curve D, parallel to it, and label it D1. It crosses the unchanged supply curve S at a higher point. I draw a dashed line from this point across to the price axis and label it ER2, which is above ER1, showing the pound has risen in value against the dollar. I draw a dashed line down to the quantity axis and label it Q2, which is to the right of Q1, showing more pounds are traded.

How the marks are earned

1 mark: demand shifted right and labelled D1. 1 mark: new exchange rate ER2, higher than ER1, labelled. 1 mark: new quantity Q2, higher than Q1, labelled.
