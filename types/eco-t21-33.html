Supply-side policy: training, infrastructure and tax cuts

What supply-side policy is and how measures that raise productive capacity work: education and training, infrastructure spending, and lower business or income taxes to encourage investment and work; their effects on productivity, output and unemployment.

Knowledge card

Supply-side policy: training, infrastructure and tax cuts

Written
  • Supply-side policy: government measures that raise how much the economy is able to produce (its productive capacity, or aggregate supply).
  • Education and training give workers skills that match job vacancies, raising productivity and reducing structural unemployment (caused by a mismatch of skills).
  • Infrastructure is the basic structures an economy needs to function, such as roads, railways, broadband and energy networks. Building it creates construction jobs now and lowers firms' costs and journey times later.
  • Lower business (corporation) tax leaves firms more profit to invest in machines, technology and training, and can attract multinational firms.
  • Lower income tax raises take-home pay, giving people a bigger incentive to work or work more hours.
  • Limits: high cost and opportunity cost, lost tax revenue or a fiscal deficit, long time lags, and firms or workers may not respond (for example profits paid out to shareholders).

Before you begin

  • Productivity (output per worker) and productive capacity
  • Types of unemployment, especially structural
  • Direct taxes on income and profit
  • Opportunity cost and fiscal deficits
1

The method

  1. Define supply-side policy as raising the productive capacity of the economy.

    Why: Definitions give one mark for productive capacity and one for aggregate supply or government measures.

  2. Name the measure and its own mechanism: skills matched to vacancies; cheaper and faster transport; more retained profit to invest; higher take-home pay.

    Why: Credit is specific to how each measure works.

  3. Link to productivity, output and unemployment (structural unemployment for training; short-run construction jobs for infrastructure).

    Why: Marks reward reaching the objective.

  4. Use the extract's figures (amount spent, km of road, tax rates, vacancies).

    Why: AO3 marks need application.

  5. For Assess or Evaluate, follow the card: weigh cost, funding, lost revenue, time lags and whether the cause of the problem matches the measure, then judge.

    Why: AO4 needs balance and a supported conclusion.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining supply-side policy as boosting demand rather than raising productive capacity.
  • ✗
    Treating interest rate cuts or asset purchasing as supply-side policy.
  • ✗
    Explaining infrastructure only through construction jobs and missing the long-run fall in costs.
  • ✗
    Assuming tax cuts are always invested, ignoring dividends paid to shareholders or lost revenue.
  • ✗
    Not asking whether the unemployment is structural before praising training.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the data: Ghana agreed a 31.2 km highway costing $50m, part of a 67 km road linking central Ghana to the north and neighbouring countries.
  2. Define infrastructure and link it to supply-side policy.
  3. Chain 1: construction jobs and spending now.
  4. Chain 2: cheaper, faster transport lowers firms' costs and opens markets in the north.
  5. Chain 3: links to neighbours help exports; finish with capacity and growth.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Infrastructure is the basic physical systems an economy needs to work, such as roads, railways and power networks. Spending on it is a supply-side policy because it raises the productive capacity of the economy.

First, in the short run, building the 31.2 km section costing $50m creates jobs for construction workers, engineers and the firms that supply materials. These workers earn incomes that they spend in local shops, which raises demand and creates further jobs in Ghana.

Second, once complete, the 67 km highway will connect central Ghana with the northern regions. Journeys become quicker and cheaper, so firms' transport costs fall. Lower costs let firms cut prices or expand output, and businesses in the north can reach bigger markets in the centre, helping regions that may have been left behind to grow.

Third, because the highway also reaches neighbouring countries, Ghanaian producers can move goods across borders more easily. This makes Ghana's goods more competitive abroad and can raise exports and foreign currency earnings.

Overall, better roads raise productivity and the capacity of the economy, so Ghana can produce more output in the long run, increasing economic growth and living standards.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked, 3 AO2 + 3 AO3. A correct meaning and developed chains (jobs, lower transport costs, regional and cross-border trade, growth) give AO2; using the 31.2 km, $50m, 67 km and the north-to-neighbours link gives AO3, reaching Level 3 (5-6).

Assess · June 2022 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define infrastructure spending as a supply-side policy that also adds to aggregate demand in the short run.
  2. Data: Alberta doubles its budget to Canadian $1.9 bn after losing 117,000 jobs in March 2020, with unemployment at 8.7%; money goes to roads, bridges and school technology.
  3. Short-run chain: construction firms hire -> incomes spent -> multiplier -> more jobs elsewhere, little inflation risk with many idle workers.
  4. Long-run chain: better transport cuts costs and school technology raises skills -> productive capacity rises -> structural unemployment falls.
  5. Against: temporary construction jobs; skills mismatch for laid-off service workers; time lags; opportunity cost and possible borrowing.
  6. Judgement: fairly effective in the short run; depends on how fast projects start and whether the jobless have the right skills.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Infrastructure spending is government spending on capital such as roads, bridges and technology in schools. It is a supply-side policy because it raises the productive capacity of the economy, but in the short run it also adds to aggregate demand (total spending).

In the short run, Alberta's plan to double its infrastructure budget to Canadian $1.9 bn creates jobs directly. Firms repairing roads and bridges must hire builders, engineers and drivers. These workers earn incomes and spend them in local shops, so demand for other goods rises and those firms also need more staff. This multiplier effect could win back some of the 117,000 jobs lost in March 2020 and pull the unemployment rate down from 8.7%. Because so many workers are idle, the extra spending is unlikely to cause much inflation.

In the long run, better roads and bridges cut transport costs and journey times, so firms can produce and deliver more cheaply and may expand. Upgrading technology in schools and universities improves the skills of future workers, which reduces structural unemployment because people can do the jobs that firms offer.

However, the effect may be limited. Construction jobs are temporary; once a bridge is repaired the workers may be unemployed again. Many of the 117,000 people who lost jobs may have worked in services, such as shops or hotels, and may not have the skills for building work, so the new jobs do not match them. Projects take time to plan, so the jobs may arrive too late. There is also an opportunity cost: the $1.9 bn could have funded direct support for firms or health care, and it may need to be borrowed.

On balance, infrastructure spending is likely to be fairly effective at cutting unemployment in Alberta, mainly in the short run through extra demand. How effective it is depends on how quickly the projects start and whether the unemployed have the skills the projects need, so it works best alongside training and other fiscal policy.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines infrastructure as supply-side, applies $1.9 bn, 117,000 jobs and 8.7% in developed demand-side and supply-side chains, gives a balanced counter-argument (temporary jobs, skills mismatch, time lag, opportunity cost) and ends with a supported judgement that depends on project speed and skills match.

Evaluate · June 2024 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define structural unemployment and education and training as supply-side policy that raises human capital.
  2. Data: unemployment at 5% for four months with one million vacancies, a quarter in low skilled jobs; $815m adult learner support covering up to 50% of fees; $595m for labour mobility.
  3. Chain: digital and foundation skills -> unemployed fill vacancies -> structural unemployment falls -> output and tax revenue rise; mobility money matches workers to jobs in other areas.
  4. Against: training takes time; remaining fees may be unaffordable; skills may not match employers; firms may want experience; opportunity cost of spending.
  5. Against: some of the 5% may be cyclical or frictional, which training does not solve.
  6. Judgement: strong advantages because vacancies point to a skills mismatch; depends on how much unemployment is structural and on courses being designed with employers.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Structural unemployment happens when workers' skills do not match the jobs available, often because industries change. Education and training are supply-side policies: they raise the skills, or human capital, of the labour force and so increase the productive potential of the economy.

The data suggest Canada's problem is mainly structural. The unemployment rate has stayed at 5% for four months, yet there are one million job vacancies. If jobs exist but people remain unemployed, the main issue is a mismatch of skills, not a lack of demand. The 'Skills for success' programme teaches foundation and digital skills. Since a quarter of the vacancies are for low skilled jobs, even short basic courses could let many unemployed people fill these posts quickly. As vacancies are filled, firms produce more, so real GDP grows, the government collects more tax and it pays less in benefits.

The $815m of financial support for adult learners covers up to 50% of training fees. This lowers the cost of retraining for older workers whose old skills are no longer wanted, so more of them are likely to take a course. Trained workers are more productive, so they can earn higher wages, which also reduces poverty.

The $595m for labour mobility tackles a second cause of structural unemployment: the jobs may be in a different area from the workers. Helping people travel to where the jobs are improves geographical mobility, so vacancies and unemployed workers are matched more quickly.

However, there are limits. Education and training take time; someone on a long course is still without work while studying and may need income now. Even with 50% of fees paid, the other half may be too expensive for a low-income adult. Courses may teach skills that employers do not actually want, and some firms may still refuse to hire people without experience. There is also an opportunity cost: the $815m and $595m could have been spent on health care or lower taxes. Finally, not all of the 5% may be structural; some may be cyclical or frictional, which training cannot fix.

Overall, education and training have strong advantages for Canada, because one million vacancies alongside 5% unemployment points clearly to a skills mismatch. The benefits will be greatest if courses are designed with employers so that skills match the vacancies, and if they are short enough for people to finish and start work quickly. How large the advantages are depends on how much of the unemployment is truly structural.

Why it reaches the top level: Accurate supply-side and structural unemployment knowledge applied to 5%, one million vacancies, $815m, 50% and $595m in developed chains to output and incomes; balanced by time lag, cost, mismatch, employer reluctance and cyclical unemployment; judgement depends on how much unemployment is structural and course design.

Evaluate · January 2020 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

  1. Define education and training as a supply-side policy that raises skills (human capital) and targets structural unemployment.
  2. For: PwC says UK GDP could rise by around £40bn a year if fewer young people are outside education, employment or training; chain skills for automation -> more employable -> structural unemployment falls -> output rises.
  3. For: the UK ranks only 19th out of 35, behind Germany, so there is room to improve; retraining older workers for new technology keeps them in work.
  4. Against: long time lag before trainees find jobs; cost to government and opportunity cost; needs employers to work with schools and universities.
  5. Against: training does not fix cyclical unemployment, where fiscal or monetary policy that raises demand may work better.
  6. Judgement: most effective only if UK unemployment is mainly structural (skills gaps from automation); otherwise a mix of supply-side and demand-side policies is best.

  1. Define seasonal workers and a labour shortage; name ways: training and hiring incentives, lower income tax, subsidies for machinery, higher wages paid by growers.
  2. Data: industry needs an extra 7,000 seasonal workers to double in size by 2027; it was short of 1,200 workers with 70% of the crop still to pick.

Assess · June 2023 · Paper 2 · Q2(g)

  1. Define productive potential (the maximum output an economy can make) and infrastructure spending as a supply-side policy.
  2. Data: over £130bn on economic infrastructure, £5.7bn for transport links in eight city regions, £5bn for super-fast broadband in hard-to-reach areas.
  3. Chain: better transport links -> lower costs and better labour mobility between cities -> firms produce more -> productive potential rises.

  1. Define business tax as a direct tax on company profit; higher rates leave less retained profit for investment.
  2. Data: UK rate rose from 19% to 25% in April 2023; Associated British Foods pays an extra £20m per year; Figure 3 compares major economies.
  3. Chain: higher tax -> less retained profit -> fewer new machines and factories -> slower output growth and fewer jobs; multinationals may choose lower-tax countries.

Assess · June 2025 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

  1. Define income tax as a direct tax on earnings; lower rates raise take-home pay and the reward for working.
  2. Data: rate for €8,501-€28,000 falls from 25% to 23%; €28,001-€50,000 falls from 35% to 33%; the 43% top rate stays the same.
  3. Chain: higher take-home pay -> extra hours or a job become more worthwhile -> people outside the workforce join -> labour supply rises.
  4. Against: the cut is only 2 percentage points, so the incentive is small; top earners see no change; some may work fewer hours if they can now reach their target income sooner.
  5. Against: lower revenue may cut public services; job availability, wages and childcare may matter more than tax.
  6. Judgement: a small positive effect, mostly for low and middle earners; depends on whether jobs and childcare are available.
