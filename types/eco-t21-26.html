The method

  1. Define

    Spending, tax and borrowing; direct vs indirect.

  2. Pick the direction

    Expansionary for jobs; contractionary for inflation.

  3. Chain

    Tax or spending → income → spending → demand → jobs or prices.

  4. Use the data

    Quote the rates and sectors in the extract.

  5. Weigh

    Deficit, saving, imports, time lags, type of problem.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Interest rates

    They are monetary policy, not fiscal.

  • ✗
    Direct vs indirect

    Income = direct; spending = indirect.

  • ✗
    Short chain

    Reach demand and jobs or prices.

Written-answer check

After your attempt, see the full worked explanation

Worked explanation

  1. Read the data: Spain had 4 million unemployed in February 2021, five monthly rises in a row; tourism and commerce worst hit; construction recovering.
  2. Separate direct and indirect tax cuts and chain each to demand.
  3. Continue the chain to output, hiring and the multiplier.
  4. Weigh: fiscal deficit, saving, imports, inflation, and the type of unemployment.
  5. Judge, saying what the benefit depends on.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Taxes can be direct, charged on income and profit (for example income tax), or indirect, charged on spending (for example VAT). Cutting either is an expansionary fiscal policy.

If Spain cuts income tax, workers take home more of their pay, so their disposable income rises. They are likely to spend more, including in the hard-hit tourism and commerce sectors. If Spain cuts VAT, prices in shops fall, which also encourages people to buy more. In both cases total demand in the economy rises. Firms respond by producing more, and to do so they need to hire more workers. Those newly employed people then spend their wages, creating further demand and more jobs. With 4 million people unemployed and five monthly rises in a row, a boost to demand could bring unemployment down noticeably.

However, the benefits are not guaranteed. Taxes are the government's main source of income, so cutting them could create or widen a fiscal deficit, especially if spending does not rise as hoped. With unemployment at its highest since 2016, confidence may be low, and households may save their tax cut rather than spend it. Some of the extra spending may go on imported goods, which supports jobs abroad rather than in Spain and worsens the current account. If demand grows faster than firms can supply, prices may rise and cause inflation. Finally, tax cuts mainly help when unemployment is caused by too little demand (cyclical). If much of Spain's unemployment is structural, for example workers lacking the skills that growing sectors such as construction need, or seasonal, as in tourism, a tax cut will do little.

Overall, lower taxes are likely to reduce cyclical unemployment in Spain in the short run, especially in consumer-facing sectors, but the size of the benefit depends on consumer confidence and the type of unemployment, and it costs the government revenue. It would work best alongside supply-side measures such as training.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked, 3 AO2 + 3 AO3 + 3 AO4. Direct and indirect tax chains to demand, output and jobs give AO2; using the 4 million unemployed, the run of rises and the named sectors gives AO3; deficit, saving, imports, inflation and type of unemployment, then a conditional judgement, give Level 3 (7-9).

Assess · January 2023 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define fiscal policy and direct versus indirect taxes.
  2. Data: 4 million unemployed in February 2021, up 44,436 in a month, worst in tourism and commerce.
  3. For: lower income tax and VAT raise spending, so firms produce more and hire more workers; repeat effect as new workers spend.
  4. Against: lower revenue may increase the fiscal deficit; worried consumers may save; spending may go on imports.
  5. Against: structural unemployment needs retraining, not just more demand; possible inflation.
  6. Judge: some benefit; depends on type of unemployment and confidence; targeted support may be better.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Fiscal policy means the government changing its spending and taxes to influence the economy. Taxes can be direct, such as income tax taken from earnings, or indirect, such as VAT added to the price of goods. Cutting taxes is expansionary fiscal policy, used to raise total demand.

Unemployment in Spain reached 4 million in February 2021, a rise of 44,436 in one month, the fifth monthly increase and the highest level since May 2016. Lower income tax lets workers keep more of their pay, so their disposable income rises and they spend more. Lower VAT reduces prices in shops, so demand for goods and services rises. As demand increases, firms need to produce more, so they hire more workers. Those new workers then earn and spend more, which creates further demand and more jobs. Lower business taxes leave firms with more profit to invest and expand. This could help the hardest-hit sectors, tourism and commerce, recover and take back staff.

However, the benefits may be limited. Tax cuts reduce government revenue, so if they do not create enough jobs, Spain could face a larger fiscal deficit and more borrowing. If people are worried about losing their jobs, they may save the extra income instead of spending it. Some extra spending may go on imported goods, which supports jobs in other countries rather than in Spain. If the unemployment is structural, because jobs in some sectors have gone for a long time, a general tax cut may not bring them back; workers may need new skills to move into a recovering sector such as construction. Finally, if demand grows faster than output, prices may rise.

Overall, lowering taxes could reduce unemployment in Spain, but the benefit is uncertain. It depends on the type of unemployment, on whether households and firms feel confident enough to spend the extra money, and on whether that spending stays in Spain. Targeted help for tourism and commerce, combined with training, may work better than a general tax cut.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines fiscal policy and direct and indirect taxes (AO2); chains tax cuts to disposable income, spending, output and hiring, applied to 4 million unemployed and tourism and commerce (AO3); counters with deficit, saving, imports, structural unemployment and inflation, with a judgement depending on type of unemployment and confidence (AO4).

Evaluate · January 2023 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define fiscal policy and inflation; inflation rose from 2.7% in 2019 to 6.3% in 2020.
  2. Diagnose demand-pull: growth of 2.8% to 4% expected and unemployment down from 7.8% to 7.3%.
  3. For: higher direct taxes and lower government spending cut total demand, easing pressure on prices.
  4. Against: higher indirect taxes raise prices; contractionary policy conflicts with growth and jobs; time lags.
  5. Against: if inflation is cost-push (e.g. dearer imports from a weak currency linked to the current account deficit), fiscal policy misses the cause.
  6. Judge: limited extent; depends on the type of inflation; combine with monetary and supply-side policies.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Fiscal policy means the government changing its spending and taxes to reach goals such as low inflation, growth and low unemployment. Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. Egypt's inflation rose from 2.7% in 2019 to 6.3% in 2020.

The data suggests some of this may be demand-pull inflation, where total demand grows faster than the economy can produce. The economy is expected to grow by between 2.8% and 4% in 2020–2021, with faster growth forecast after that, and unemployment fell from 7.8% in 2019 to 7.3% in the third quarter of 2020. More people in work means more income and more spending, which can pull prices up.

Contractionary fiscal policy could reduce this pressure. If the government raises direct taxes such as income tax, households have less disposable income, so they spend less. Higher taxes on company profits leave firms with less to invest. If the government also cuts its own spending, total demand falls directly, because government spending is part of total demand. With less demand chasing the same goods, firms have less reason to raise prices, so inflation should slow. A tighter budget would also reduce government borrowing.

However, there are limits. Raising indirect taxes, such as VAT, adds to prices immediately, so it could push inflation up in the short run. Fiscal policy also conflicts with other goals: lower demand would slow the growth Egypt expects and could reverse the fall in unemployment. Tax rises and spending cuts take time to plan and are unpopular, so they are often slow. Most importantly, fiscal policy works mainly on demand. If the inflation is cost-push, for example caused by more expensive imports, cutting demand will reduce output and jobs but will not remove the cause. Egypt's continuous current account deficit could weaken its currency and make imports dearer, which would be cost-push inflation.

Other policies may help, such as higher interest rates, or supply-side policies that raise the economy's productive capacity so that it can produce more without prices rising.

In conclusion, fiscal policy can control inflation in Egypt only to a limited extent. It is most useful if the rise to 6.3% comes from strong demand as growth picks up, and least useful if it comes from rising costs. Its effectiveness depends on the cause of inflation and on how much growth and employment the government is willing to give up, so it works best as part of a mix with monetary and supply-side policies.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines fiscal policy and inflation (AO2); links growth of 2.8% to 4% and falling unemployment to demand-pull pressure, then chains tax rises and spending cuts to lower demand and prices (AO3); weighs indirect-tax effects, conflicts with growth and jobs, lags and cost-push causes, with a judgement depending on the cause of inflation (AO4).

Assess · November 2024 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

  1. Define income tax and a progressive tax; the first $18 200 is tax-free.
  2. For: income tax is 47% of revenue, the largest source, and the fastest growing in recent years.
  3. For: a large, steady source helps budget planning and funds healthcare, education and welfare.
  4. Against: revenue depends on how many people work; it falls in a recession or with high unemployment.
  5. Against: high rates may discourage work; other sources like sales tax at 14% depend on spending instead.
  6. Judge: fairly reliable while employment is high; depends on the economic cycle and a spread of other revenues.

Evaluate · November 2025 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

  1. Define fiscal policy and government expenditure; healthcare spending is around 9.3% of GDP.
  2. For: more funding and higher pay for doctors create jobs and raise demand for medical goods and services.
  3. For: shorter waits for the 7.6 million people still waiting means people return to work sooner, raising productivity.
  4. Against: the government is unsure how to fund it; higher taxes or borrowing may cut spending or raise the deficit.
  5. Against: opportunity cost, such as education, and benefits depend on how well the money is used.
  6. Judge: benefits are likely if spending cuts waiting lists efficiently; depends on funding method and value for money.
