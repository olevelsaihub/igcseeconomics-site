Knowledge card

Productivity and how to raise it

Written
  • Productivity = how much output is produced from a given amount of inputs (resources) or in a given time. Labour productivity = output per worker (or per worker-hour).
  • Higher productivity means more output from the same resources, so the cost of making each unit (average cost) usually falls.
  • Land productivity rises with fertiliser, irrigation (adding water), drainage (removing excess water) and reclamation (turning unusable land into usable land).
  • Labour productivity rises with education and training, which build human capital: the skills, knowledge and experience of workers. Better-quality labour works faster and makes fewer mistakes.
  • Capital productivity rises with new technology, for example faster machines or ordering tablets in a restaurant.
  • Limits: improvements cost money, take time to work, may need skilled staff to use them, and some workers may leave after being trained.
  • Things such as bureaucracy (too many rules and paperwork), congestion and heavy regulation tend to lower productivity.

Before you begin

  • The four factors of production (land, labour, capital, enterprise).
  • The difference between output (what is made) and inputs (what is used).
  • That average cost is total cost divided by output.
1

The method

  1. Define productivity as output in relation to the inputs used.

    Why: Definition marks need both parts: the output and the resources (or time) used.

  2. Name the improvement and the factor it acts on (land, labour or capital).

    Why: This shows you know how the change works, e.g. drainage improves land.

  3. Build the chain: the improvement lets the same resources produce more, so output per input rises, so average costs fall.

    Why: Explain marks are for an identified way, a development and the context.

  4. Use the case: name the farmers, country, restaurant or firm and the details given.

    Why: Context is one of the three marks in Explain and is needed for higher levels.

  5. For a 9-mark Assess or 12-mark Evaluate, follow those cards: weigh benefits against cost, time lags, skills needed and other ways to raise productivity, then judge.

    Why: The top level needs balanced weighing and a supported conclusion, not just benefits.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Confusing productivity with production: producing more by hiring more workers does not raise output per worker.
  • ✗
    Defining productivity with only one part (just 'output') and missing the link to inputs or time.
  • ✗
    Saying an improvement raises productivity without explaining how it changes output from the same land, labour or capital.
  • ✗
    Writing only benefits in 9- and 12-mark answers with no costs, time lags or alternatives weighed.
  • ✗
    Mixing up irrigation (adding water) and drainage (removing water).

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Now see it used on a real past-paper question

What a complete response includes

Definitions need output and input. Explain rewards a named way, a developed effect on output per input, and context. Long answers reward case-based chains to lower costs or higher output, then weighing and a supported judgement.

Worked explanation

  1. Identify what drainage does: it removes excess water from fields.
  2. Develop: dry, usable land lets crops grow where they could not before, or stops crops rotting.
  3. Link to productivity: more crops from the same farmland and labour.
  4. Put it in context: Indian farmers.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Better drainage takes excess water off fields that would otherwise flood or stay waterlogged (identify). This means Indian farmers can plant and harvest crops on land that was previously too wet to use, and fewer crops are lost to rotting (develop). As a result, each farmer gets more crop output from the same amount of land and work, so the productivity of Indian farmers rises (context and effect).

How the marks are earned

3 marks: 1 for identifying what drainage does to the land, 1 for developing it into more crops grown on the same land, and 1 for applying it to Indian farmers and higher output per input.

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Assess · June 2019 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Define productivity and human capital; link productivity to income per head as the data state.
  2. Data: Canada's labour productivity growth lagged other leading economies for decades, hurting competitiveness; since 2011 it rose to 3rd of 16 leading economies.
  3. Chain: education and training -> more skilled, motivated workers -> more output per worker and lower turnover -> lower unit costs -> greater international competitiveness.
  4. Against: investment in capital and technology may raise productivity more quickly; training is costly and trained workers may leave; education has a long time lag; training quality must fit employers.
  5. Judgement: important but not always best; depends on the industry (skilled services vs automated manufacturing) and the time scale.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Productivity is output per worker (or per unit of input) in a given time. Raising it lets a country produce more from the same resources, which, as the data say, is the most important driver of income per head.

Education and training raise the quality of labour, or human capital. Schools and colleges can teach technical skills, and firms can train staff on the job. Skilled workers make fewer mistakes and finish tasks faster, so output per worker rises. Lower costs per unit then make firms more competitive internationally, which matters for Canada because weak productivity growth over many decades reduced its competitiveness. Training can also motivate workers, because they feel valued, so labour turnover falls and firms lose less time recruiting. Since 2011, Canada's labour productivity has improved so much that it is now the 3rd most productive of the 16 leading economies, which shows productivity can be raised, and better-trained workers may be part of the reason.

However, education and training may not be the best way. Investment in capital, such as machines, computers and automation, can raise output per worker more, and more quickly. Education takes years before it affects output, so it is a slow method. Training is costly for firms and the government, and a firm may lose its investment if trained workers leave for a rival. Training only works if its quality matches what employers need. The data also do not say what caused Canada's improvement since 2011, so we cannot be sure education was the cause.

Overall, education and training are an important way to raise productivity, but not always the best. They work best in service industries that rely on skilled people, while manufacturing may gain more from new technology. The best method depends on the industry and the time scale, and usually combines better-trained workers with better capital, because skilled workers are needed to use new technology well.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines productivity and human capital, applies Canada's past weak growth and rise to 3rd of 16 since 2011 in chains to output, costs and competitiveness; weighs capital investment, time lag, cost and labour turnover; judges education important but not always best, depending on industry and time scale.

Evaluate · June 2024 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define productivity; identify the programmes: land reclamation and guidance on fertiliser and irrigation.
  2. Data: in 2023 the government raised the price it pays for wheat; land for agriculture is limited; many farmers know only traditional methods and lack skills or finance.
  3. Chain: reclamation adds farmland; fertiliser and irrigation raise yield per field -> more output from the same inputs -> higher incomes -> reinvestment in equipment.
  4. Chain: guidance improves farmers' skills (quality of labour) -> better methods -> higher productivity.
  5. Against: lack of finance to buy inputs; resistance to change; long time lags; environmental harm from fertiliser and water use.
  6. Judgement: moderate effect; depends on farmers' access to finance and willingness to adopt new methods.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Productivity is the amount of output produced from a given quantity of inputs, such as wheat per field or per farmer. The Egyptian Government wants more wheat: in 2023 it raised the price it paid for wheat, and it runs programmes for land reclamation and guidance on fertiliser and irrigation.

Land reclamation turns unusable land, such as desert or wetland, into farmland. This tackles the main limit farmers face, a limited amount of land for agriculture. More land means total wheat output can rise. Reclamation alone raises output rather than productivity, but combined with better methods it can raise output per farmer too.

Guidance on fertiliser and irrigation improves the quality of land. Fertiliser adds nutrients and irrigation gives crops a steady water supply, so each field produces a larger harvest. With the same land and labour, output rises, so productivity rises. The guidance also improves the quality of labour: many farmers know only traditional methods, so advice teaches them new skills. Higher output, together with the higher price paid for wheat, raises farmers' incomes, so they can afford more equipment, which raises productivity further over time.

However, the programmes may have a limited effect. The data say many farmers lack the finance to change methods. Guidance is useful only if farmers can pay for fertiliser and irrigation systems; without loans or subsidies, many may not adopt them. Farmers used to traditional methods may not trust new advice, so they may be slow to change. Reclamation and irrigation take years to set up, so gains will be slow. Heavy fertiliser use can damage soil, water and wildlife, which may reduce productivity in the long run, and irrigation uses scarce water.

Overall, the programmes are likely to raise farming productivity in Egypt to a moderate extent. Reclamation tackles the land problem and guidance tackles the skills problem, but neither tackles the lack of finance. The effect depends on whether farmers can afford the inputs, which the higher wheat price may help with, and on whether they are willing to give up traditional methods. If the government added finance support, such as grants or cheap loans, the effect would be much larger.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines productivity and applies the limited land, reclamation, fertiliser and irrigation guidance and higher 2023 wheat price in developed chains via land and labour quality; balances with lack of finance, traditional methods, time lags and environmental harm; judgement of moderate effect depends on finance and farmers' willingness.

Evaluate · November 2020 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

  1. Define productivity (output per unit of input) and technology as capital.
  2. Data: robots and tablets replace waiters and chefs; machines make espresso and cappuccinos; driven by efficiency, rising labour costs and customer desire for convenience.
  3. Chain: tablets take orders faster and cheaper than waiters -> more diners served per worker -> higher productivity -> savings reinvested in more capital.
  4. Against: customers may be confused by tablets, slowing orders; staff may still be needed to help at first; ordering errors waste food and time; cost of buying the technology.
  5. Against: some diners value human service, so demand could fall if the dining experience changes.
  6. Judgement: productivity may dip in the short run but rise in the long run; depends on how quickly customers and staff adapt.

Assess · November 2024 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

  1. Define productivity and quality of labour (skills, education, knowledge of workers).
  2. Data: a worker's education level affects their ability at work; migrants bring their knowledge, skills and information with them.
  3. Chain: education -> more skilled workers -> more output in less time -> lower unit costs -> higher profits and competitiveness.
  4. Chain: skilled immigration -> better quality and quantity of labour -> competition encourages workers to improve their own education.
  5. Against: education takes time to pay off; skilled workers may emigrate; immigration may lower wages and demotivate; capital and technology also drive productivity.
  6. Judgement: quality of labour is very important in the long run; depends on the country's industries and its investment in technology.
