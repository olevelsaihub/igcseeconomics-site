Knowledge card

Draw a demand or supply shift and new equilibrium

Written
  • First decide which curve moves: a change affecting buyers (income, tastes, population, advertising, substitutes, complements) moves demand; a change affecting producers (costs, technology, weather, taxes, subsidies) moves supply.
  • Increase = shift right; decrease = shift left. Draw the new curve parallel to the old one and label it D1 or S1.
  • Demand right: price and quantity both rise. Demand left: both fall.
  • Supply right: price falls, quantity rises. Supply left: price rises, quantity falls.
  • Mark the new equilibrium where the new curve crosses the unchanged curve, then draw dashed lines across to the price axis (P1) and down to the quantity axis (Q1).

Before you begin

  • Causes of demand shifts (ECO-T11-08) and supply shifts (ECO-T11-09).
  • What equilibrium price and quantity are (ECO-T11-11).
1

The method

  1. Decide whether the event affects demand or supply, and whether it is an increase or a decrease.

    Why: Shifting the wrong curve loses all the marks.

  2. Draw the new curve parallel to the old one, on the correct side, and label it D1 or S1.

    Why: One mark is for the correctly shifted, labelled curve.

  3. Find where the new curve crosses the curve that did not move.

    Why: That crossing point is the new equilibrium.

  4. Draw a dashed line across to the price axis and label it P1.

    Why: One mark is for the correct new price.

  5. Draw a dashed line down to the quantity axis and label it Q1.

    Why: One mark is for the correct new quantity.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Shifting supply when the event affects buyers, for example an income change.
  • ✗
    Shifting the correct curve in the wrong direction, such as a fall in a complement's price drawn as a left shift.
  • ✗
    Leaving the new curve, P1 or Q1 unlabelled.
  • ✗
    Marking P1 or Q1 at the old intersection instead of the new one.
  • ✗
    Moving both curves when only one should move.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

What a complete response includes

Worked explanation

  1. Wireless headphones are a normal good, so higher income raises demand: shift demand right.
  2. Draw D1 parallel to D, to the right.
  3. Find where D1 crosses the unchanged S.
  4. Label P1 above Pe and Q1 to the right of Qe.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Higher incomes mean consumers can afford more wireless headphones, so demand increases at every price. On the diagram I draw a new demand curve, D1, parallel to the original D and to its right, leaving the supply curve S where it is. The new equilibrium is where D1 crosses S. From this point I draw a dashed line across to the price axis and label it P1, which is above the original price Pe, and a dashed line down to the quantity axis labelled Q1, which is to the right of the original quantity Qe. So the equilibrium price and quantity of wireless headphones both rise.

How the marks are earned

1 mark: demand shifted right and labelled D1. 1 mark: higher equilibrium price labelled P1. 1 mark: higher equilibrium quantity labelled Q1.
