Knowledge card

Macroeconomic objectives and trade-offs

Written
  • The government's macroeconomic objectives: low unemployment, economic growth, low and stable inflation, a balance or surplus on the current account, protection of the environment, and redistribution of income.
  • A trade-off is a choice between two objectives where getting more of one means getting less of the other.
  • Unemployment and inflation: when unemployment falls, people spend more (demand-pull) and firms must pay higher wages to find staff (cost-push), so inflation tends to rise.
  • Growth and inflation: fast growth raises demand; if supply cannot keep up, prices rise.
  • Growth and the environment: more output uses more resources and causes more pollution; strict environmental rules can raise firms' costs and slow growth.
  • Inflation and the current account: higher domestic prices make exports less competitive and imports relatively cheaper, worsening the current account.

Before you begin

  • Meanings of growth, inflation, unemployment and the current account
  • Demand-pull and cost-push inflation
  • How price changes affect exports and imports
1

The method

  1. Name the two objectives involved and use the correct wording (for example 'low and stable inflation').

    Why: One-mark items reward a correctly worded objective.

  2. Explain the chain by which gaining one harms the other, for example more jobs, more income, more spending, demand above supply, higher prices.

    Why: Marks reward a clear causal link between the two objectives.

  3. Where possible, give both routes (demand-pull and cost-push, or exports and imports).

    Why: A second route adds development in Analyse answers.

  4. Apply to the country's data (inflation rate, emissions, growth figures).

    Why: Context and AO3 marks.

  5. For a 12-mark Evaluate, follow the Evaluate card: ask whether the trade-off can be avoided (green technology, supply-side growth), then judge.

    Why: AO4 needs balance and a conclusion.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Listing two objectives without explaining the link between them.
  • ✗
    Getting the direction wrong, for example saying lower unemployment reduces inflation.
  • ✗
    Confusing trade-off with opportunity cost.
  • ✗
    Writing a vague objective such as 'a good economy' instead of a named macroeconomic objective.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

How each command word is answered

Command wordTimes askedWhat to write
State4One objective or trade-off in a short phrase, for example low unemployment, or inflation as a trade-off with growth.
multiple-choice4Falling unemployment or rising growth usually goes with rising inflation; 'more of one, less of another' is a trade-off.

Explain3One reason for the link, the step that causes it (demand outruns supply, higher business costs, dearer exports), and the result in context.

Written-answer check

After your attempt, see the full worked explanation

Worked explanation

  1. Read the data: in Vietnam unemployment fell between 2015 and 2016 while inflation rose.
  2. Route 1 (demand-pull): more jobs, more income, more spending, demand greater than output, higher prices.
  3. Route 2 (cost-push): fewer spare workers, higher wages, higher costs, higher prices.
  4. Link both back to Vietnam and name the trade-off.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

When unemployment falls, more people in Vietnam have jobs and earn a regular wage instead of little or no income. With more income, households spend more on goods and services, so consumer spending rises. If total demand grows faster than the economy can increase its output, firms cannot meet all of the extra demand, so they raise their prices. This is demand-pull inflation, and it helps explain why Vietnam's inflation rose in the same period that unemployment fell.

A second link works through costs. As unemployment falls, there are fewer spare workers available to hire. Firms that want to expand must compete for staff by offering higher wages. Wages are a major cost of production, so firms pass these higher costs on to customers as higher prices, which is cost-push inflation. Higher wages also give workers more to spend, adding to the demand-pull pressure, so both effects can push Vietnam's inflation rate up together. This shows the trade-off between low unemployment and low inflation.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked, 3 AO2 + 3 AO3. Two developed routes (demand-pull from higher spending, cost-push from higher wages) give AO2; tying both to Vietnam's fall in unemployment and rise in inflation over 2015-2016 gives AO3, reaching Level 3 (5-6).

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Evaluate · November 2020 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define trade-off; data: US carbon dioxide emissions rose in 2018 mainly because of high economic growth.
  2. Trade-off: boom -> more industrial production, truck and air travel, heating of workplaces -> more fossil fuel use -> more emissions (negative externality).
  3. Trade-off reverse: meeting commitments needs taxes, regulation or permits -> higher costs for firms -> slower growth, possible job losses.
  4. Against a trade-off: growth funds green technology; coal plants closed and electric car purchases nearly doubled; green industries create growth; services pollute less.
  5. Judgement: clear short-run trade-off, smaller in the long run; depends on speed of switching to low-carbon energy and government priorities.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A trade-off exists when achieving more of one objective means achieving less of another. Economic growth is an increase in real GDP; environmental protection means reducing damage such as pollution and greenhouse gas emissions.

The data suggests there is a trade-off in the USA. In 2018 the US increased its emissions of carbon dioxide, and the main reason was high economic growth. A booming economy means more industrial production, more truck and air travel and more offices and workplaces to heat. Producing more output uses more energy, and much of that energy still comes from burning fossil fuels, so emissions rise. These emissions are a negative externality: their cost falls on society, not on the firms and consumers who cause them. This happened even though large numbers of coal-fired power stations closed and consumers bought nearly double the number of electric cars, which shows how strong the link between growth and emissions still is.

The trade-off also works the other way. To meet its international commitments, the US will need more environmental protection, such as carbon taxes, stricter regulation or pollution permits. These policies raise firms' costs of production. Higher costs can reduce output, investment and international competitiveness, and some firms may move production abroad, so economic growth slows and jobs may be lost.

However, the trade-off may not be permanent. Economic growth creates income and tax revenue that can be spent on cleaner technology. The data shows this is already happening: coal power stations are closing and electric car sales nearly doubled. Richer consumers can afford electric cars, and richer governments can afford to subsidise renewable energy. Green industries, such as making electric cars or renewable energy equipment, are themselves a source of growth and jobs, so environmental protection can create growth as well as limit it. Not all growth pollutes either: growth in services such as software or finance uses far less energy than heavy industry.

In conclusion, in the short run there is a clear trade-off in the USA, because the boom raised carbon dioxide emissions in 2018 despite green progress. In the long run the trade-off can be reduced if growth is used to pay for cleaner technology. Whether it remains depends on how quickly the US switches to low-carbon energy, on which industries grow, and on how much priority the government gives to environmental protection compared with growth.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines trade-off, growth and externality; applies the 2018 rise in emissions, industrial production, travel, closed coal plants and electric car sales in chains both ways; argues the trade-off can shrink through green growth; supported short-run versus long-run judgement with conditions.
