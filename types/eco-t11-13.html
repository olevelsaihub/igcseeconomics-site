Knowledge card

Price elasticity of supply: meaning, values and causes

Written
  • Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how much quantity supplied responds to a change in price. PES = percentage change in quantity supplied divided by percentage change in price.
  • PES is positive. 0 = perfectly inelastic; between 0 and 1 = inelastic; exactly 1 = unitary; above 1 = elastic; infinity = perfectly elastic.
  • Supply is more elastic when extra factors of production are easy to get, there are stocks that can be released, firms have spare capacity, or there is more time to adjust.
  • Primary products such as fruit and crops usually have inelastic supply: they take time to grow, depend on the weather and are often perishable, so cannot be stored.
  • Manufactured goods usually have more elastic supply: factories can add shifts, use spare capacity and hold stocks.

Before you begin

  • Supply and movements along the supply curve (ECO-T11-07).
  • Factors of production and the idea of capacity.
  • PED categories (ECO-T11-12), which use the same number scale.
1

The method

  1. Define PES or give its formula: percentage change in quantity supplied divided by percentage change in price.

    Why: Short items and the opening of long answers both need this.

  2. Classify the value: below 1 inelastic, above 1 elastic, 0 and infinity the perfect cases.

    Why: Classification is the core of the multiple-choice items.

  3. Apply determinants to each product: growing time, weather, storage, spare capacity, availability of factors.

    Why: Determinants applied to named products earn the application marks.

  4. Use the case data, such as factories below capacity or annual harvests.

    Why: Data use moves the answer up the levels.

  5. For a 9-mark Assess, follow the Assess card: weigh what could make the comparison less clear (full capacity, longer time periods), then judge.

    Why: AO4 marks need balance and a conclusion.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Writing the PED formula (quantity demanded) instead of quantity supplied.
  • ✗
    Calling a PES of 0.7 elastic; values below 1 are inelastic.
  • ✗
    Listing determinants without linking them to the named products.
  • ✗
    Forgetting that supply becomes more elastic over a longer time period.
  • ✗
    Assuming a factory can always raise output, even at full capacity.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how much the quantity supplied responds to a change in price. Supply is price elastic when quantity supplied changes by a bigger percentage than the price does, giving a PES above 1.

Supply of textiles in Bangladesh is likely to be price elastic. The factories are often working below capacity, so they have spare machines and space. If the price of cotton shirts or knitwear rises, firms can quickly use this spare capacity, for example by adding an extra shift, and raise output by a larger percentage than the price rise. Textiles can also be stored, so firms can build up stocks and release them when the price rises, which makes supply respond even faster.

Supply of agricultural products is likely to be price inelastic. Wheat, corn and fruit are produced once a year, so after planting, farmers cannot add much output until the next harvest, however high the price goes. Output also depends on the weather, which farmers cannot control, and fruit is perishable, so it cannot be held in stock and sold later when prices rise.

However, the difference may not always be so clear. If the textile factories became fully used, extra output would need new machines and trained workers, which takes time, so supply would become less elastic. Over a longer period, farmers can respond: with about 40% of the population working in agriculture, there may be enough labour to plant more land for the next season, so agricultural supply becomes more elastic over time.

Overall, supply is likely to be more price elastic for textiles than for agricultural products, mainly because the factories have spare capacity and goods that can be stored, while crops are tied to yearly growing seasons and the weather. This is most true in the short run; the gap is likely to narrow over a longer period.

How the marks are earned

AO2/AO3 (up to 6): PES defined, spare capacity and storage applied to textiles, growing time, weather and perishability applied to crops, using the case data. AO4 (up to 3): full capacity and long-run counter-points, then a judgement with a time-period condition.

Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how much the quantity supplied responds to a change in price. Supply is price elastic when quantity supplied changes by a bigger percentage than price. PES depends mainly on how quickly and easily firms can change output, which is affected by spare capacity, stocks, the availability of factors of production and time.

Supply of textiles in Bangladesh is likely to be more price elastic. The data says textile factories are often operating below capacity. This spare capacity means that if the price of shirts, knitwear or suits rises, firms can quickly produce more by using idle machines or adding an extra shift, without building new factories. Textiles can also be stored, so firms can hold stocks and release them when prices rise.

Agricultural products are likely to be less price elastic. Wheat, corn and fruit are produced annually, so if their price rises, farmers cannot supply much more until the next harvest. Output also depends on natural factors such as weather, which farmers cannot control, and fruit is perishable, so it cannot be stored for long to meet a later rise in price. Even though about 40% of the population works in agriculture, these workers cannot quickly increase output in the short run.

However, the difference may not always be so clear. If textile factories reach full capacity, raising output needs more machinery and trained workers, which takes time and money, so textile supply becomes less elastic. A shortage of inputs such as cotton could also limit supply. In the long run, farmers can also respond, by planting more land with a crop whose price has risen or using better techniques.

Overall, supply is likely to be more price elastic for textiles than for agricultural products, especially in the short run, because textile factories have spare capacity and goods can be stored. This depends on factories continuing to have spare capacity and enough raw materials; over a longer period the gap between the two will be smaller.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines PES and its determinants (AO2); chains spare capacity, stocks and planning to elastic textile supply and annual harvests, weather and perishability to inelastic farm supply, using the data and 40% figure (AO3); weighs full capacity, cotton inputs and long-run farm response, with a time-dependent judgement (AO4).
