Knowledge card

Free trade: meaning, gains, losses and trade patterns

Written
  • Free trade: buying and selling between countries with no tariffs (import taxes), quotas (import limits) or other restrictions.
  • Gains for consumers: lower prices, wider choice and sometimes better quality, which raise living standards.
  • Gains for firms: a wider market to sell into (more sales and revenue), cheaper imported inputs (raw materials and components), economies of scale and less risk from relying on one home market.
  • Losses: domestic firms lose protection and face cheaper foreign rivals, so sales, profits and jobs may fall; government loses tariff revenue; infant industries (new, small industries) may be wiped out.
  • Removing tariffs abroad helps exporters: their goods become cheaper in the foreign market, so they can win market share.
  • Trade patterns: developed countries tend to export manufactured goods and services and import primary products (food, raw materials); developing countries tend to export primary products such as crops and minerals.
  • Whether free trade helps depends on how competitive the domestic firms are in price and quality.

Before you begin

  • What imports, exports, tariffs and quotas are.
  • Competition and how it affects prices.
  • Costs, revenue and profit.
  • The primary, secondary and tertiary sectors.
1

The method

  1. If asked for the meaning, say trade between countries that is not taxed or restricted.

    Why: Marks go for the cross-border trade idea and for the absence of controls.

  2. Identify the group (consumers, domestic firms, exporters, the government) and whether the question wants advantages or disadvantages.

    Why: Answers about the wrong group or side earn little.

  3. State the gain or loss and explain how removing barriers causes it (no tariff means lower cost means lower price means more sales).

    Why: Development marks come from the cause-and-effect chain.

  4. Use the case: the bloc or agreement, the size of the market, the tariff cut or the products named.

    Why: Context is a mark in Explain and AO3 in longer answers.

  5. For trade patterns, contrast what each type of country exports and imports, and why (sizes of primary, secondary and tertiary sectors).

    Why: The pattern must be explained, not just listed.

  6. For a 9-mark Assess or 12-mark Evaluate, follow the Assess or Evaluate card: gains, then real losses (competition, job losses, lost revenue, retaliation), then a judgement on what the outcome depends on.

    Why: AO4 marks need balance and a supported conclusion.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining free trade as 'trade that costs nothing' instead of trade without taxes or restrictions.
  • ✗
    Writing about consumers when the question asks about domestic firms, or about advantages when it asks for disadvantages.
  • ✗
    Saying free trade raises prices or reduces choice.
  • ✗
    For trade patterns, describing only one type of country instead of contrasting the two.
  • ✗
    Ignoring the data such as the bloc size, members or tariff percentages.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

How each command word is answered

multiple-choice2Free trade means trade with no restrictions; it increases choice and market opportunities and lowers prices.

State2One word-level advantage (lower prices, more choice, wider markets) or disadvantage (foreign competition, job losses).
Describe2One effect plus a short reason, e.g. domestic firms may close because foreign products are cheaper or better.

Worked explanation

  1. Group: domestic firms inside the RCEP; side: benefits.
  2. Data: the RCEP is the biggest free trade agreement, nearly 30% of world GDP, 15 member countries.
  3. Chain 1: no tariffs within the bloc, so exports to other members are cheaper, so sales and revenue rise.
  4. Chain 2: selling in 14 other countries spreads risk if demand falls at home.
  5. Chain 3: cheaper imported inputs from members cut costs and raise profits.
  6. Chain 4 (optional): larger output gives economies of scale.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Free trade means goods and services can move between member countries without tariffs or other restrictions. The RCEP has 15 members and accounts for nearly 30% of world GDP, so a firm inside it gains tariff-free access to a huge market. Because its exports no longer carry a tariff, they can be sold more cheaply in other member countries, so the firm can win more customers and increase its sales revenue.

Selling across 15 countries also spreads risk. If demand falls in the firm's home country, for example during a recession there, it can still sell to customers in the other members, so its revenue is more stable.

Firms can also import raw materials and components from other RCEP members without paying tariffs. If these inputs are cheaper than those available at home, the firm's costs of production fall, so it can either cut prices to be more competitive or keep prices the same and earn higher profits.

Finally, producing more to supply a bigger market lets firms gain economies of scale, lowering the average cost of each unit and strengthening their position against rivals outside the bloc.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked, 3 AO2 and 3 AO3. Level 3 needs clear definitions and several developed chains for RCEP firms (wider tariff-free market and revenue, spreading risk, cheaper inputs and profit, scale economies), applied to the 15 members and 30% of GDP figures.

Assess · June 2025 · Paper 2 · Q2(g)

Answer plan

  1. Define tariffs and a free trade agreement.
  2. Data: the Philippines removed a 5% tariff on Korean cars and 30% on Korean car parts; 12th largest population; 366,000 car sales in 2023; Korean share 2.5%, Japanese share 82.5%.
  3. Chain: no tariff -> lower prices or higher margins -> more price competitive -> more exports, revenue and jobs in Korea; economies of scale.
  4. Chain: large 30% cut on parts -> big boost for Korean parts exporters.
  5. Against: Japanese dominance and brand loyalty; 5% is a small price change; rivals may cut prices; exchange rate changes.
  6. Judgement: likely benefit, larger for parts than cars; depends on winning buyers from Japanese brands.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A tariff is a tax on imports. Under the free trade agreement signed in September 2023, the Philippines removed a 5% tariff on South Korean cars and a 30% tariff on South Korean car parts.

The main benefit is that South Korean cars become cheaper in the Philippines. Without the 5% tariff, Korean firms can lower their prices, or keep prices the same and earn a higher profit margin on each car. Lower prices make Korean cars more price competitive against other foreign brands, so demand for them should rise. Korean makers currently have only 2.5% of a market with 366,000 car sales in 2023, so there is plenty of room to grow. The removal of the 30% tariff on car parts is even larger. Korean parts makers can sell parts much more cheaply to Philippine garages and assembly plants, so their exports and revenues could rise sharply. The Philippines has the 12th largest population in the world and is one of the biggest importers of cars and parts, so this is a large market. Higher exports mean more output and jobs in South Korea's car industry, and higher sales may bring economies of scale, lowering average costs.

However, the benefits may be limited. Japanese-made cars hold an 82.5% market share, so Filipino consumers may have strong brand loyalty to Japanese cars and not switch, even if Korean cars are a little cheaper. Even if all of the 5% tariff were passed on, the price would fall by at most about 5%, which is small for an expensive item like a car. Japanese firms may cut their own prices to protect their share. A change in the exchange rate could also remove the price advantage if the Korean currency rises in value.

Overall, the removal of tariffs is likely to benefit the South Korean car industry, especially car parts makers, because the 30% tariff cut is large. The benefit for car sales depends on whether Korean firms can attract buyers away from Japanese brands, for example through marketing and quality, and on how Japanese competitors react.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines tariffs and applies the 5% and 30% cuts, 2.5% share, 366,000 sales and 12th largest population in developed chains on price, demand, exports and scale; balances with 82.5% Japanese dominance, brand loyalty, small price change, rival reaction and exchange rates; judgement depends on winning buyers from Japanese brands.

Evaluate · June 2019 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define tariffs and free trade.
  2. Data: China cut tariffs from 17.3% to 7.7% on over 200 consumer products (pharmaceuticals, food, supplements, clothing); Nestle's sales could rise by 15% or $18.7bn; Procter & Gamble also gains.
  3. Chain: lower tariffs -> cheaper imports -> higher real incomes, more choice and quality -> higher living standards.
  4. Chain: more import competition -> Chinese firms cut costs and innovate; other countries may cut tariffs in return -> more Chinese exports.
  5. Against: domestic firms lose sales -> unemployment; infant industries unprotected; less tariff revenue; smaller current account surplus.
  6. Judgement: real but limited benefits as only about 200 products are covered; depends on whether consumers switch and how other countries respond.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A tariff is a tax placed on goods coming into the country. Reducing tariffs moves a country closer to free trade, where goods move between countries without taxes or limits.

China has cut tariffs from 17.3% to 7.7% on over 200 consumer products, including pharmaceuticals, food, health supplements and clothing. The first benefit is for Chinese consumers. A lower tax on imports means foreign products reach shops at lower prices. Consumers can buy the same goods for less, so their real incomes rise and they can buy more, which raises living standards. Cheaper pharmaceuticals and health supplements may also improve health. Consumers also get more choice and access to good-quality goods, such as shampoos and electronic toothbrushes from Procter & Gamble, that Chinese firms may not make as well.

Second, more competition from imports puts pressure on Chinese firms. To keep customers, they must cut costs, improve quality and innovate, so they become more efficient. In the long run this can make Chinese products more competitive abroad. Third, cutting tariffs may encourage other countries, such as the US, to lower their tariffs on Chinese goods in return, which helps Chinese exporters sell more and raises output and jobs. Chinese firms that import supplies also face lower costs.

However, there are costs. Chinese firms making similar consumer goods may lose sales to multinationals: Nestle's sales could rise by 15% or $18.7bn, and some of this may come from customers who used to buy Chinese products. Some Chinese firms may close, causing structural unemployment, and new infant industries lose their protection. The government also collects less tariff revenue, which may mean less spending on public services. More imports could also reduce China's current account surplus. Much of the gain may go to the profits of foreign multinationals rather than to China.

Overall, reducing tariffs is likely to bring real benefits to China, mainly through lower prices and more choice for consumers and pressure on domestic firms to become more efficient. The scale may be limited, though, because the cut covers only around 200 products. The size of the benefit depends on whether Chinese consumers actually switch to imports, how well Chinese firms respond to the competition, and whether other countries cut their tariffs on Chinese exports in return.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines tariffs and free trade, applies the 17.3% to 7.7% cut on over 200 products and Nestle's 15% or $18.7bn in chains on prices, living standards, efficiency and reciprocal cuts; evaluates job losses, infant industries, lost revenue and current account; judgement depends on consumer switching and others' response.

Assess · June 2022 · Paper 2 · Q2(g)

  1. Define free trade and a free trade area.
  2. Data: EU-Mercosur area of 770 million people; current trade of €88bn in goods and €34bn in services a year; Mercosur removes tariffs on 91% of EU goods (clothing, chemicals, wine, canned peaches).
  3. Chain: no tariffs -> EU exports cheaper in Mercosur -> wider market, higher sales and revenue -> economies of scale; spreading sales across blocs reduces risk.
  4. Against: EU ends 92% of tariffs on Mercosur imports -> more competition from lower-cost firms; EU farmers of beef, sugar and chicken lose out.
  5. Judgement: clear advantages for EU exporters of manufactured goods; depends on the price and quality of EU products compared with Mercosur products and the sector of the firm.
