Idea. A shop in New York buys tea from a UK firm. The UK firm wants pounds, so the American shop swaps its dollars for pounds. Every UK export sold abroad creates demand for pounds. So a currency has its own market. The price of pounds in dollars, the exchange rate, goes up the side. The quantity of pounds goes along the bottom. They cross at E R one and Q one. Demand for pounds comes from foreigners: buying UK exports, saving in UK banks, or speculators expecting the pound to rise. Supply of pounds comes from UK residents swapping pounds to buy imports or invest abroad. Learn the results. More exports, higher UK interest rates or speculative buying: demand right, the rate rises. Fewer exports: demand left, it falls. More imports: supply right, it falls. Fewer imports: supply left, it rises.

Method. Step one, ask who has to buy or sell the currency. Step two, decide the direction: more of the activity shifts the curve right, less shifts it left. Step three, draw the new curve parallel to the old one, and label it. Here UK imports rise, so UK residents sell more pounds. Supply shifts right to S one. Steps four and five. From the new crossing, draw a dashed line across to the price axis, E R two, and down to the quantity axis, Q two. Here the pound falls, and more pounds are traded.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2022. Using the diagram, draw the effect on the pound of an increase in UK exports to the US. Label the new curve, the new exchange rate and the new quantity. Three marks. US buyers must pay for UK goods in pounds. More exports means they buy more pounds. So demand for pounds rises, and shifts to the right. Draw D one parallel to D, to its right. Leave S where it is. D one crosses S higher up. Draw across to E R two, above E R one, and down to Q two, to the right of Q one. The pound has risen against the dollar. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is shifting supply for a change in exports. Exports bring foreign buyers who need pounds, so exports move demand. Imports move supply. Another is reusing the old labels. If the new point is also called E R one and Q one, the examiner cannot see the change.

Your turn. Now you try. UK consumers buy far fewer cars imported from Japan. On a diagram for the pound in Japanese yen, show the effect. Label the new curve, exchange rate and quantity. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. To buy Japanese cars, UK buyers sell pounds for yen. Fewer imports means fewer pounds sold. Supply of pounds falls, and shifts to the left. Draw S one to the left of S. It crosses D higher up. So E R two is above E R one, and Q two is to the left of Q one. The pound rises, and fewer pounds are traded. Remember: which curve and which way, draw and label it, then mark the new exchange rate and quantity at the new crossing. Now try the practice questions.
