A famous footballer wears a new pair of trainers. Suddenly many fans want them, even though the price has not changed. Demand rises at every price. A shift in demand means buyers want more, or less, at every price. It is caused by something other than the good's own price. An increase moves demand right. A decrease moves it left. The main causes are advertising, income, tastes and fashion, the prices of substitutes and complements, and population. A substitute is bought instead, like tea instead of coffee. If coffee gets dearer, demand for tea shifts right. A complement is used together, like motorbikes and helmets. If helmets get cheaper, demand for motorbikes shifts right.

Step one, identify the factor. Step two, state the direction. Step three, explain why buyers want more or less at every price, using the case. Step four, give the effect on equilibrium. Demand right: price and quantity both rise. Demand left: both fall.

Here is a real question from June 2025. Amazon Prime ended its live tennis streaming in the UK. Sky took over at a much higher price, and Tennis TV is the only other option. Analyse how demand for Tennis TV was affected. Six marks. A demand curve shows how much buyers are willing and able to buy at each price. The factor here is the price of substitutes: the main substitute, Sky, is now much dearer. Fans who want value switch to Tennis TV rather than pay Sky's higher price. So more subscriptions are demanded at every price. Demand shifts right, from D to D one. Price rises from P e to P one, and quantity from Q e to Q one. This fits the data: Tennis TV raised its price. A common mistake is calling a change in the good's own price a shift. It is a movement along the demand curve. Another is getting complements backwards. A cheaper complement increases demand.
