Imagine a local juice maker. Cheap juice floods in from abroad, and her sales fall. The government can step in to protect her. This is called protection. There are three main tools. A tariff is a tax on imports. A quota is a limit on the quantity imported. A subsidy is a payment from the government to domestic producers. A tariff adds to the cost of supplying imports. So the supply of imports shifts left. Their price rises and fewer are bought. A subsidy lowers domestic producers' costs. So their supply shifts right. Their price falls and they sell more, at home and abroad. A quota fixes the quantity of imports. However high the price, no more can come in, so supply becomes a vertical line at the quota. The price rises from P e to P one, and the quantity falls from Q e to Q one.

Step one, name and define the tool. Step two, give its direct effect: dearer imports, fewer imports, or cheaper domestic goods. Step three, follow it to the result for domestic firms, jobs and trade. Then use the case numbers. For an Assess question, weigh both sides. Domestic firms win sales, jobs are kept, and tariffs raise revenue. But consumers pay more, firms grow lazy, other countries may retaliate, and subsidies cost the government money. Who pays a tariff? When demand is inelastic, as for fuel, buyers have few alternatives. So the price rises a lot, and the quantity bought hardly falls. Consumers carry most of the cost.

Here is a real question from June 2021. Brazil lets in only six hundred million litres of ethanol a year from the U S. Explain one advantage of using quotas for a country such as Brazil. Three marks. The tool is a quota, a limit on imports. Only six hundred million litres of U S ethanol can come in each year. So Brazilian producers face less foreign competition. Develop it. Once the six hundred million litres are sold, any extra demand must be met by Brazilian producers. They sell more, gain market share and earn more revenue, which protects jobs. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is mixing up the tools. A tariff is a tax. A quota is a quantity limit. A subsidy is a payment. Another is shifting supply the wrong way. A subsidy cuts costs, so supply shifts right. A tariff adds costs, so supply shifts left.

Now you try. Country Y pays its rice farmers a subsidy of fifty dollars per tonne. The figure is just an example. Explain one advantage for Country Y's rice farmers. Pause here and work it out.

The subsidy cuts farmers' costs by fifty dollars a tonne. Supply shifts right, so local rice is cheaper. Cheaper local rice wins buyers from imports at home, and sells better abroad. So farmers' sales, exports and incomes rise. Remember: define the tool, give its direct effect, follow it to the result, and use the case numbers. Now try the practice questions.
