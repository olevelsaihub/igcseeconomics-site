Read the narration

Think of a family that spends more than it earns. It has to use its savings, or borrow. A country can do the same in its trade with the rest of the world. A current account deficit means imports are worth more than exports. Here, exports of eighty billion dollars and imports of one hundred billion give a deficit of twenty billion. A surplus is the opposite: exports worth more than imports. Three things cause changes. Quality: better home goods sell more abroad. Price: cheaper home goods or dearer imports help. And the exchange rate: a depreciation makes exports cheaper and imports dearer.

Step one, decide if the question asks for a cause, why it happened, or an impact, what it does. Causes start from quality, price or the exchange rate. Impacts start from the deficit or surplus itself. Steps two and three, for a cause. Name the factor, link it to exports or imports, and finish at the current account. Better home quality means more exports, fewer imports, and a smaller deficit. Step four, for an impact. A deficit is a leakage: money spent abroad, not at home. Home firms get less demand, so output falls and unemployment can rise. Step five, use the country's figures. Step six, for Assess, weigh the costs against the upsides, then judge on the size of the deficit and how long it lasts.

Here is a real question from November 2020. Indonesia's deficit was expected to reach three per cent of G D P by the end of 2018. The minister said it was nothing to worry about. An economist said it was serious. Assess the impact of a deficit. Nine marks. Cost one, a leakage. When Indonesians buy imports instead of home goods, Indonesian firms lose demand. They may cut output and jobs, so unemployment can rise and growth slows. Cost two, paying for it. The gap must be covered with foreign currency reserves or borrowing. Reserves can run out and debts carry interest, so a growing deficit, as the economist warned, becomes serious. Now the upsides. Cheaper or better imports raise living standards and keep inflation down. And one possible route: more rupiah are sold to buy imports, so the supply of rupiah rises and the rupiah may fall. That makes exports cheaper and could help reduce the deficit. Weigh both sides. Lost output, jobs and reserves on one side. Cheaper imports, lower inflation and a possible fall in the exchange rate on the other. Judge. At three per cent of G D P, and lower than in 2014, it may not yet be a crisis. But if it keeps growing, the costs are likely to outweigh the benefits. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is stopping at 'exports rise'. Finish the chain: what happens to the current account? Another is treating every deficit as a disaster. Weigh cheaper imports and lower inflation, and judge on its size and how long it lasts.

Now you try. A country's car makers improve quality so much that their cars win world awards. Explain the likely effect on the country's current account deficit. Pause here and work it out.

Better cars mean foreign buyers buy more of them, so exports rise. Local buyers also choose home cars instead of imports. Exports rise and imports fall, so the deficit gets smaller. Remember: decide cause or impact, build the chain to exports, imports or the economy, use the figures, then weigh and judge. Now try the practice questions.
