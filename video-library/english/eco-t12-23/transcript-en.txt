Imagine a cleaner paid so little that rent and food are hard to afford. The government can pass a law saying no one may be paid less than a set amount. That law is a minimum wage. Why have one? To raise the incomes and living standards of the lowest paid, to narrow the gap between rich and poor, and to reduce the need for government benefits. There is a risk. If it is set above the equilibrium wage, firms want fewer workers, Q d, while more people want to work, Q s. The gap is a surplus of labour: unemployment.

Step one, define it. Step two, use the case's numbers. For example, a rise from five hundred to five hundred and fifty euros is fifty euros. Fifty divided by five hundred, times one hundred, is ten per cent. Step three, benefit chains. Workers get more income and better living standards. They spend more, so firms' revenue rises. Workers may be more motivated, and the government may collect more tax. Step four, cost chains. Firms' wage bills rise. They may hire fewer workers, cut hours, raise prices or accept lower profits. Step five, judge. The effect depends on the size of the rise, whether productivity rises, price elasticity of demand, the share of workers paid it, and the short or long run.

Here is a real question from June 2025. Latvia raised its minimum wage from six hundred and twenty to seven hundred euros a month. Sixteen per cent of people were paid it. Evaluate whether this would benefit everyone. Twelve marks. Define it, then use the data. Seven hundred minus six hundred and twenty is eighty euros. Eighty divided by six hundred and twenty, times one hundred, is about twelve point nine per cent. Benefits to workers. Eighty euros more a month buys more food, housing and essentials, so living standards rise. Feeling better rewarded, they may work harder, raising productivity. Benefits to firms and government. Low-paid workers spend most of any extra income, so shops in Latvia earn more revenue. Higher incomes and spending also bring more tax for public services. But not everyone gains. Wage costs rise, and some firms hire fewer workers or cut hours. If the wage is pushed further above equilibrium, unemployment can rise, and those who lose jobs are worse off. Judge. Many benefit, but not everyone. Only sixteen per cent are paid the minimum, so for many firms the extra cost is small, and big job losses are less likely. In the short run some small firms may lose out. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is saying a minimum wage always causes unemployment. It depends on the size of the rise and on productivity. Another is saying it helps firms without saying how. Explain the link through spending or motivation.

The rise is sixty cents. Sixty cents divided by eight dollars, times one hundred, is seven point five per cent. Benefit: workers earn more each hour, so living standards rise.
