If petrol gets dearer, drivers still need to fill up. If one brand of chocolate gets dearer, people switch to another. Same price rise, very different reactions. Elastic means quantity reacts a lot. So we draw elastic demand fairly flat. A small change in price gives a big change in quantity. Inelastic means quantity reacts only a little. So we draw inelastic demand steep. A big change in price gives a small change in quantity. Supply works the same way, but slopes upward. Elastic supply is flat: when demand rises, quantity supplied rises a lot and price only a little.

Step one. Decide: demand or supply, and elastic or inelastic. Demand slopes down, supply slopes up. Elastic is flat, inelastic is steep. Steps two to four. Draw the curve and label it. Pick two points. Draw dashed lines from each to the price axis, P one and P two, and to the quantity axis, Q one and Q two. Step five. Check the gaps. For elastic, the quantity gap must be clearly bigger than the price gap. For inelastic, the reverse.

Here is a real question from June 2024. Using the diagram, draw a price elastic demand curve. Label it, and show the impact on both axes from a change in price. Three marks. Demand slopes down, and elastic means fairly flat. Draw a gentle downward curve and label it D. Pick a higher point on the curve. Draw a dashed line across to the price axis, P one, and down to the quantity axis, Q one. Pick a slightly lower point. Mark P two just below P one, and Q two well to the right of Q one. A small price gap and a big quantity gap show elastic demand. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is swapping the slopes: drawing elastic steep and inelastic flat. Another is drawing the price gap and the quantity gap about the same size, or showing the change on only one axis.
