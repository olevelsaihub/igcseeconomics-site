A bakery makes bread, a factory makes phones, a taxi gives rides. Add up the value of everything a country produces in a year, and you get one big number. That number is gross domestic product, or G D P: the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year. Economic growth is a rise in real G D P over time. Two hundred billion to two hundred and ten billion is five per cent growth. Long-run growth, from more resources or better technology, raises what a country is able to produce. On a production possibility curve, the whole curve shifts outwards. Watch out for inflation. If G D P rises eight per cent but prices rose six per cent, real output grew by roughly two per cent. Real G D P removes the effect of rising prices.

Step one, define G D P with a time period. Step two, decide what the question wants: strengths, limits, or both. Learn these lists. Step three, develop each point. Say what it is, why it happens, and what it means for judging growth. Step four, use the country's figures. For a nine-mark Assess, write two strengths, two limits, then a judgement that says what the usefulness depends on.

Here is a real question from November 2021. Ghana's growth rate was forecast at eight point eight per cent, double that of emerging economies. Growth now relies on crude oil as well as cocoa. Assess G D P as a measure of growth. Nine marks. Strength one. Almost every country measures G D P the same way, so we can compare. Ghana's eight point eight per cent is double the rate for emerging economies, so they were growing at roughly half that. Ghana is clearly growing faster. Strength two. More cocoa and oil means firms earn more and hire more, so incomes tend to rise. Growth signals better living standards. Limit one. Street traders and farmers growing their own food are not recorded. So G D P can understate what Ghana really produces. And if prices rise fast, only real G D P shows true growth. Limit two. Oil money may go to a few firms and workers while many stay poor. Oil extraction can also pollute land. G D P shows neither. Judge. G D P is a good starting point: easy to compare, and eight point eight per cent shows real progress. But it depends on using real figures and on the size of the hidden economy. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is defining G D P as what a country sells or exports. It is what the country produces, in a year. Another is calling accuracy a strength. Missing activity is a weakness. The strength is easy comparison.

Now you try. Islandia's G D P rose six per cent last year, but prices rose five per cent. About a third of workers sell food at street stalls and are not recorded. Explain two reasons why Islandia's G D P figures may be misleading. Pause here and work it out.

Reason one: inflation. Prices rose five per cent, so real output grew only about one per cent, roughly six minus five. The six per cent overstates growth. Reason two: the hidden economy. Street stall sales are not recorded, so G D P understates what Islandia produces. Remember: define G D P with a year, choose your side, develop each point, and use the country's figures.
