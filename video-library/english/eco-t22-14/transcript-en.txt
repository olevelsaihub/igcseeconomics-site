Last year, one hundred pounds bought one hundred and forty dollars for a trip to New York. This year, the same hundred pounds buys only one hundred and thirty dollars. Each pound buys fewer dollars, so the pound has fallen in value. The words depend on who moves the rate. In a floating system, the market sets it: a rise is an appreciation, a fall is a depreciation. In a fixed system, the government sets it: a rise is a revaluation, a fall is a devaluation. In a floating system, demand and supply move the rate. If demand for pounds falls, the price of pounds falls from E R one to E R two. The pound depreciates.

Step one, name the change. Step two, if rates are given, check whether one unit of the home currency now buys more or less foreign currency. Fewer dollars per pound means the pound has fallen. Step three, the price effect. A UK car priced at ten thousand pounds costs fourteen thousand dollars at the old rate, but only thirteen thousand dollars at the new one. Meanwhile, a thirteen hundred dollar laptop now costs a thousand pounds instead of about nine hundred and twenty-nine. Step four, follow through to quantities. Foreign buyers buy more exports, and residents buy fewer imports. The current account records trade in goods and services, so net exports rise and it improves. Step five, in longer answers, judge. The effect depends on price elasticity of demand, how big and how long the change is, and quality. A weaker currency can also raise inflation, because imports cost more.

Here is a real question from November 2020. Germany had a current account surplus of two hundred and sixty-eight billion euros, and the euro had weakened. Explain one reason why the fall may have helped Germany's current account. Three marks. The reason. When the euro falls, foreign buyers need less of their own currency to buy German goods. So German exports become cheaper abroad. Develop it. If foreign buyers respond to the lower prices, demand for German exports rises, so exports grow compared with imports. Apply it. Net exports rise, so Germany's surplus, already two hundred and sixty-eight billion euros, becomes even larger. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is misreading the rates. One pound equals one dollar thirty, after one dollar forty, is a fall in the pound, not a rise. Another is mixing up the terms. A government cutting its fixed rate is a devaluation, not a depreciation.

Now you try. A country with a fixed exchange rate raises its currency from one unit equals fifty US cents to one unit equals sixty cents. Name this change, and explain one likely effect on its current account. Pause here and work it out.

Each unit now buys more dollars, so the currency has risen. The government set it, in a fixed system. So this is a revaluation. Its exports become dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home. Exports fall and imports rise, so the current account worsens. Remember: name the change, find the effect on export and import prices, follow it to volumes, then the current account.
